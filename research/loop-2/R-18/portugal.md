# R18 Portugal trade and industrialization review

**Program:** R-18 component review, with dependencies to R-03, R-05, R-06, R-12 and R-15  
**Claims audited:** C-042 and C-046, including the historical premises used to motivate ownership advice  
**Research date:** 4 October 2026  
**Status:** Research findings for author review, not manuscript revision or thesis adoption

## Finding and scope

The Portugal analogy survives as a question about the difference between present gains from exchange, subsequent productive development, and the distribution of returns. It does **not** survive unchanged as a verified story in which Portuguese wine owners merely consumed their earnings while English cloth owners alone invested and compounded. Nor can it supply a historical proof that knowledge workers should become owners.

C-046 contains a correctable theoretical error: Ricardo's demonstration does not require each country to possess an absolute advantage in its export. C-042 combines some recoverable treaty and trade facts with an unresolved vineyard-yield assertion, a misleading national consumption/investment dichotomy, and a disputed causal account of Portuguese divergence. Correcting these does not require selecting the author's normative position. Whether to retain a carefully limited Portugal illustration, replace it with a hypothetical example, or remove it remains an authorial choice.

This review examines the opening, “Most readings,” and closing prescription of `source/essays/01-we-were-portugal-all-along.md` and “What actually happened to Portugal,” “Building the loom,” and the concluding prescription in `source/retired/03-portugal-didnt-have-to-lose.md`. The retired draft is intellectual provenance, not presumed current doctrine. The accepted, revisable D-002 already distinguishes ownership from productive architectural, educational, and operational work. This research tests historical evidence; it neither reverses nor empirically validates that decision. The remainder of R-18 is outside this component's completion claim.

## Ricardo's actual demonstration

### A real trading setting with hypothetical quantities

Ricardo first published the *Principles* in 1817. The accessible text inspected here is his third edition of 1821, chapter VII, “On Foreign Trade” [P01]. Its examples use conditional language: England “may be so circumstanced,” and production in Portugal “might require” the specified labor. These are illustrative suppositions, not reported factory or vineyard observations. A real wine-and-textile trading relationship does not turn those numbers into historical measurements. The chapter does not identify the Methuen Treaty as the empirical basis of its calculation.

Ricardo supposes that the relevant quantity of cloth takes 100 English laborers for a year and wine 120; corresponding Portuguese quantities take 90 and 80. For the same specified outputs, Portugal is more labor-efficient in both. Nevertheless, exchanging English cloth for Portuguese wine can save labor in each country. Ricardo's stated exchange obtains for England wine that would have cost 120 labor-years by producing cloth costing 100; Portugal obtains cloth that would have cost 90 by producing wine costing 80. This is the direct counterexample to the essay's final assertion that mutual gains require absolute-advantage specialization.

For clarity, a modern opportunity-cost restatement can normalize those quantities as one cloth unit and one wine unit. Then:

| Country | Labor per cloth unit | Labor per wine unit | Cloth forgone per extra wine unit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| England | 100 | 120 | 1.2 |
| Portugal | 90 | 80 | 0.8889 |

These normalized units are an explanatory calculation, not newly recovered historical data. Portugal's opportunity cost of wine is 80/90 cloth; England's is 120/100 cloth. A wine price strictly between these ratios, expressed in cloth, permits gains for both within the example. The one-for-one exchange lies inside that interval. There is no need to invent a natural monopoly, and nothing here guarantees an equal division of gains.

The opening of the essay captures the central relative-cost point. “Faster, cheaper, better” is nonetheless too loose if it mixes labor required, money prices, and product quality: the calculation holds outputs comparable and specifies labor requirements. Lower labor requirements are not themselves a complete historical statement about wages, quality-adjusted costs, or market prices.

### Larger productivity gaps do not mechanically erase comparative advantage

The proposition that a widening absolute productivity gap necessarily eliminates the less productive party's comparative advantage is not a theorem. A simple counterexample is enough. If England's labor requirements both double, their ratio stays 120/100. Its comparative ranking is unchanged even though it becomes absolutely worse at both goods. Likewise, proportional improvement in every activity by one party changes absolute productivities without necessarily changing its relative opportunity costs. Disproportionate improvement can change the ranking; the direction must be calculated rather than asserted.

This observation should not be converted into the opposite overstatement that comparative advantage guarantees every worker a satisfactory job under arbitrary automation. That conclusion requires a model of demands, factor supplies, wages, outside options, ownership, and income distribution. “A country can gain from trade in the specified model” and “each displaced individual retains bargaining power” are different propositions.

Ricardo also does not ignore changing technology. Immediately after the numerical demonstration, he supposes England discovers a better way to make wine and traces changes in production, trade, money flows, and prices. He even considers reversal of the countries' employments. The essay may argue that AI changes the relevant technologies unusually quickly, but it cannot present technological reassignment as a correction Ricardo was incapable of considering [P01].

Another boundary is especially important for the book. Ricardo explains why capital does not simply move to the absolutely more productive country by invoking impediments to international relocation. A human and an AI system working within one organization do not reproduce that institutional setting. Nor are the model's finite English wine-production costs equivalent to the essay's asserted structural impossibility of machine sense-making. A zero-capability premise would change the model and needs independent evidence under R-06/R-14.

## What the commercial treaty actually says

The commercial treaty was concluded at Lisbon on **27 December 1703**. It should be distinguished from the political and defensive agreements of May that year. The inspected Portuguese transcription identifies John Methuen and the Marquis of Alegrete; Smith reproduces an English translation of its three articles [P02–P03]. Modern diplomatic history corroborates the setting [P05–P06].

- Article I re-admits English **woollen cloth and woollen manufactures** on the pre-prohibition footing. It does not establish unrestricted duty-free access for every industrial product.
- Article II provides a relative tariff advantage for Portuguese wines against French wines: the corresponding French duty less one-third, conventionally expressed as two-thirds of the French rate. It is not a one-third tariff, a guaranteed price, a guaranteed purchase quantity, or a prohibition of every competing wine.
- The text uses perpetual language, but also expressly permits Portugal to prohibit the woollens again if the wine-duty undertaking is impaired. “Forever” describes an undertaking with a remedy, not evidence that later policy could never change.
- Article III concerns ratification and exchange of ratifications within two months.

Smith's contemporary interpretation is particularly useful against the recurring duty-free myth: Portugal restored admission at the former duties, without promising English woollens better terms than French or Dutch woollens [P03, IV.vi]. The treaty's wording concerns access conditions, not orders to abolish every other domestic occupation. Neither sovereign literally assigned the whole national labor force between two products.

The original signed instruments were not inspected. P02 is a recoverable modern transcription, checked against Smith's older translation and modern scholarship. The essentials are well supported; a publication requiring diplomatic quotation should use a critical edition or archive facsimile. Contemporary English sources can use a different calendar and year convention. The conventional Lisbon date is the appropriate unambiguous date here.

### The tariff preference and its endpoint

The nationality-based wine-duty preference did **not** continue forever. In July 1831, Lord Althorp proposed equalizing duties on French, Portuguese, Spanish, and other foreign wines at 5 shillings 6 pence per gallon, with Cape wine excepted. The Wine Duties Bill passed its third reading in the Lords on 30 September 1831. Neither a proposal nor that legislative stage alone proves implementation. The Chancellor of the Exchequer's July 1856 statement supplies the subsequent check: it explicitly dates the equalization to 1831 and describes foreign wines as then subject to a uniform rate [P16, 11 July and 30 September 1831; 15 July 1856]. Thus **1831 is the verified policy endpoint of the explicit Portuguese-versus-French nationality preference** used in this review.

That endpoint is distinct from the later commercial changes around 1860. Equal duties per gallon could still favor stronger or more valuable wines relative to lighter or cheaper wines. The 1860 debate concerns a scale tied to alcoholic strength; Milner Gibson expressly rejected the description of it as a treaty obligation to discriminate by nationality against Portugal [P16, 24 February 1860]. Duguid's discussion of the older port trade's loss of protection and reorganization around 1860 should therefore not be read as dating the first equalization of French and Portuguese duties to that year [P12, manuscript pp. 5, 22–23].

**Formal treaty termination remains a separate investigated gap.** The 1831 parliamentary debates themselves dispute how Methuen interacted with the 1810 treaty and what notice or consequences an alteration required. This review has not inspected the relevant diplomatic termination instruments or adjudicated those competing legal readings. It consequently gives a verified tariff-policy endpoint without assigning an unsupported single date to the treaty's formal extinction. Nor does it assert an unchanged effective tariff ratio throughout 1703–1831.


## Competing explanations of the relationship

### Smith and List ask different counterfactual questions

Smith does not supply a simple pro-Methuen free-trade endorsement. In *Wealth of Nations*, IV.vi, he argues that preferential trade may benefit favored merchants while giving consumers and producers in the favoring country less advantageous terms than nondiscriminatory competition would. He sees the wine preference as favorable to Portugal on its face and rejects the proposition that a British gold inflow, by itself, proves a uniquely advantageous bargain. His relevant benchmark is the alternative trade Britain could have conducted, not merely whether British merchants earned money from Portugal [P03].

This distinction is analytically durable. Trade can be beneficial compared with autarky and still inferior to another trading arrangement. A bilateral deficit is also not automatically a national welfare loss. One must identify what financed it, what was acquired, and what opportunity costs or dynamic effects followed. Smith is a primary source for this reasoning and for contemporary controversy, not an econometric estimate of the treaty's effect.

List's *National System* (1841), book I, chapter V, offers a sharply different reading [P04]. He presents the loss of protected Portuguese manufactures and the expansion of British commerce as a warning against exchanging industrial development for agricultural market access. His chapter even begins with a national-character contrast between Iberian windfall spending and English accumulated wealth. The retired draft's moral contrast therefore resembles a long-standing polemical interpretation rather than a fact established merely by revisiting Ricardo.

List's most important substantive challenge is dynamic: preserving or developing productive capacities may matter in addition to immediate exchange values. But his sweeping claims of complete industrial ruin, his national stereotypes, and his retrospective certainty are not substitutes for evidence about firms, regions, investment, and plausible alternatives. His chapter acknowledges adverse Portuguese social conditions beyond trade policy. It cannot establish that one treaty alone explains a century's outcomes, still less that every landowner consumed while every manufacturer invested.

There is no need to choose between “Ricardo mathematically disproved” and “actual history must validate free trade.” A valid conditional demonstration and an unsuccessful historical development path can coexist. The relevant question is which assumptions connect them, and whether those assumptions fit this case.

### Diplomatic revisionism and unequal power

Antunes and Salvado [P05, pp. 248–251] emphasize military vulnerability, customs-dependent royal finances, and older commercial connections. They find no evidence for the frequently repeated claim that an organized wine-growers' lobby or the negotiators' own wine estates explains the agreement. They also point to other foreign merchant communities and a comparable Dutch agreement. Their compact synthesis limits a uniquely English, treaty-created dependency story, without denying unequal bargaining power.

Duguid's study of political debate [P06, especially pp. 9–14, 32–34] shows that the commercial agreement's later iconic status should not be projected backward as an obvious economic verdict in 1703. Its survival during the 1713 Anglo-French negotiations involved merchants, party conflict, Portuguese diplomacy, and anti-French politics. That evidence identifies contingency and constituencies; it does not estimate national gains or establish a frictionless choice for Portugal.

Watson's critical political-economy interpretation [P10, pp. 5–16] restores imperial power and enslaved labor to a textbook narrative that can conceal them. This is a necessary contextual challenge. However, his stronger suggestion that the numerical example cannot demonstrate mutual gains is not accepted here: the explicit exchange calculation above does so under its assumptions. Historical asymmetry challenges an application or policy inference, not that arithmetic. Claims about Ricardo's deliberate intentions likewise require more than a mismatch between abstraction and history.

These accounts agree that actual commerce was politically organized. They differ over how far that observation licenses a single causal story of Portuguese failure. Neither power's existence nor the treaty's contingent survival identifies the magnitude of its long-run effect.

### Reconstructed growth and the resource curse

Palma and Reis [P07] reconstruct Portuguese GDP per capita for 1527–1850. Their account places sustained growth before the mid-eighteenth-century reversal, rather than uninterrupted decline from 1703. It treats maize, commercial wine production, and empire as expansion mechanisms. The estimates combine regional prices and wages, labor-supply adjustments, agricultural demand, and assumptions connecting occupational structure to non-agricultural output. They are reconstructed national accounts, not direct annual censuses of production. The article expressly leaves fundamental causes open.

Kedrosky and Palma [P08] subsequently make a causal case for Brazilian gold. Archival price baskets for four regions indicate real appreciation; an augmented synthetic control with a 1694 treatment date and six European donors estimates Portuguese GDP per capita about 40% below its counterfactual by 1800 [P08, “A Counterfactual Portugal,” Figure 7 and following discussion]. Actual Portugal was a first-stage recipient of Brazilian gold. Their counterfactual makes Portugal a second-stage recipient, like the donor countries; it is not a world without gold. They regard Methuen as a transmission mechanism rather than the ultimate cause. Important limitations include reconstructed outcomes, six-country donor support, possible spillovers, concurrent shocks such as war and the 1755 earthquake, extrapolation, and incomplete sectoral output data. The supplement and code were not replicated here. Their reported robustness checks do not turn the counterfactual into an observed fact.

Pedreira's earlier structural history [P11, pp. 563–575, 591–596] documents manufacturing, limited technical adoption, surviving enterprises, and constraints on diffusion. It gives greater weight to colonial-market disruption and the early-nineteenth-century crisis, but explicitly refuses a generalized industrial-destruction narrative and identifies surviving branches and enterprises [P11, p. 575]. It also rejects simple entrepreneurial-incapacity explanations: commercial investors did attempt industrial projects within difficult incentives, market, transport, and institutional conditions.

The apparent conflict partly concerns measurement. A national per-capita decline does not imply every manufacturing branch disappeared. Conversely, named surviving workshops and improving export figures do not establish aggregate real growth. Nominal revenue, real output, employment, and productive potential are not interchangeable. The newer macro reconstruction is important counterevidence to an older late-eighteenth-century prosperity narrative; the enterprise evidence remains sufficient to reject literal absence of an industrial base.

The proposed resource-curse mechanism is a change in relative incentives: a windfall can raise demand and costs in domestic non-traded activities, weaken competing tradables, and redirect resources. Larger independent royal revenues can also reduce incentives to bargain with representative institutions. These are mechanisms to test, not synonyms for personal improvidence. The synthesis supports taking Dutch disease and political incentives seriously. It does not identify the effect of “choosing wine” separately from bullion inflows, war, institutional change, markets, or technological opportunities. Those are related but distinct treatments. A retrospective comparison between two countries cannot by itself attribute their divergence to different saving habits.

## Wine was an economic system as well as a crop

Martins [P09, pp. 391–402, 427] reports growing regional wine production and an export takeoff predating Methuen, together with crises and regulatory responses. She records wine taxes financing Porto public works from 1763, Douro navigation works in 1780, and roads from 1788 [P09, p. 392, notes 4–6]. Her series' systematic regional production registrations begin in 1772. Production rose from approximately 152,000 hectolitres in 1772–74 to 374,000 in 1800–04 [P09, p. 396, note 25], but these are regional totals, not fixed-acreage yields. Boundary and registration changes must be considered before treating them as productivity measures.

Duguid's archival study [P12, manuscript pp. 8–10, 18–19, 25–28] provides a more discriminating account of capture. Portuguese stockholders and brokers possessed capital and earned returns; British exporters depended on their stocks, credit, and expertise. The argument identifies access to distribution, regulation, and specialized knowledge, not just capital scarcity. Later branding and integration shifted control and weakened some local intermediaries. The evidence draws on selected surviving firms, correspondence, accounts, and customs records; it is not a representative experiment or a complete profit census.

These findings expose several category errors in the proposed vineyard/loom comparison. A barrel sold is an output. A vineyard, cellar, inventory, commercial relationship, brand, or transport improvement can be an asset. A yard of cloth is also an output rather than the loom that produced it. Selling either output can finance consumption or investment. The historically meaningful comparison is between the returns, risks, learning opportunities, and control rights associated with particular assets and positions, not between “wine” and “capital.”

The sentence “A vineyard in 1780 produced roughly what a vineyard in 1710 produced” remains **unresolved**. No matched vineyard, fixed-hectare, labor-productivity, or total-factor-productivity series spanning those dates was recovered. An increase in acreage or regional output would not refute stable yield per hectare. Equally, biological limits or a familiar landscape do not prove stable economic productivity. Quality, marketability, wastage, storage, labor inputs, and transport can change independently of grape yields.

“Wine doesn't compound” must therefore be disaggregated:

1. If it means unchanging physical yield at a fixed vineyard, it needs the missing evidence.
2. If it means revenue cannot finance durable productive assets, it is an untenable economic proposition.
3. If it means wine offered fewer productivity spillovers than particular mechanized industries, that is a comparative empirical hypothesis, not established by the vineyard sentence.
4. If it means geography constrains a designated-origin product's quantity, that is compatible with valuable capital, product differentiation, and changing returns.

The text also confuses scarcity with monopoly. France and Spain were actual wine competitors; the treaty itself presupposes French wine. A legal privilege within a market, a differentiated product with limited close substitutes, and exclusive possession of a necessary input are different propositions. A Portuguese vintner did not own “Portugal's wine” collectively or inherit an automatic claim on an entire nation's trade gains.

## What explains British mechanization

The woollen manufactures named in 1703 should not silently become an already mechanized cotton industry. Allen's high-wage interpretation [P13, manuscript pp. 9–13] emphasizes whether labor-saving spinning machinery paid at prevailing labor and capital costs. It also distinguishes the later diffusion of power weaving. This is an investment-incentive account, not evidence that Portuguese cloth purchases uniquely financed the machines. Its wage reconstructions and profitability assumptions are debated.

Styles [P14, pp. 45–48, 60–68] supplies a different emphasis: product quality, fashion, materials, and domestic/Atlantic markets. Customs and excise records, theft-trial evidence, and surviving textiles connect demand for particular fabrics to innovations in cotton spinning. This discriminates among kinds of cloth rather than treating all textile output as homogeneous. Surviving objects and prosecuted thefts are selective evidence, not a comprehensive consumption sample. His account also retains the importance of enslaved labor in expanded cotton supply.

Palma's monetary interpretation [P15, sections I, III–V] argues that American precious metals supported English monetization, market participation, and state capacity. It combines money-stock reconstruction, coin-denomination evidence, and historical mechanisms, rather than tracing a particular export payment into a particular machine. The argument acknowledges two-way money-growth causation and identifies the counterfactual conditions it requires. It offers a possible connection between overseas bullion and development, not proof of the draft's simple reinvestment chain.

The common ground is that investment operates through a set of complements and incentives. The disagreements concern their relative importance and measurement. Technology, product development, finance, political privileges, labor organization, and access to supplies cannot be reduced to the virtue of one country's investors. The narrow fact of British textile mechanization is secure. The exact story “Portugal's wine receipts were consumed; England's cloth receipts built the machines” remains unsupported as a comparative accounting or causal statement.

Likewise, “workshop of the world by 1817” is a broad retrospective description, not a sufficiently precise industrial benchmark. It should not be made to imply that every major production process had already mechanized or that the relative roles of different textile branches were fixed. A safe chronology names the specific technology, sector, and period instead of using Ricardo's publication year as an all-purpose endpoint.

## Safe argumentative use and downstream dependencies

Three different propositions must remain separate: gains from a particular exchange, the evolution of productive capacity, and who receives income or control. The historical evidence can motivate investigating all three. It cannot collapse them into a single morality tale.

For R-03, carry forward the repaired comparative/absolute distinction, the explicit assumptions behind the arithmetic, and the factor-mobility boundary. An analogy is not a production function. For R-05 and R-15, examine particular complementary assets and control arrangements rather than assigning “ownership” to the successful country and “labor” to the unsuccessful one. For R-06, P12 is useful counterevidence: historically valuable local knowledge need not retain the same organizational role after standardization. Its applicability to AI remains a question, not a historical prediction. For R-12, keep firm prosperity, national per-capita income, consumer gains, investment, and distribution separately measurable.

An especially important break is aggregation. Portugal and Britain contain heterogeneous owners, workers, traders, consumers, and rulers. Their relationship also involved Brazil and other markets, including coercive colonial production. Treating each nation as one representative vintner or mill-owner suppresses precisely the distribution and control questions the book wants to raise. A prosperous export house does not demonstrate national prosperity; national divergence does not show that every exporter chose badly.

A second break is the counterfactual. Showing that Portugal later lagged Britain does not establish that rejecting Methuen would have generated British-style industrialization. Nor does evidence that gold distorted incentives establish a policy whereby every Portuguese merchant could profitably escape those incentives by buying machines. A lesson about maintaining investment options is plausible, but its desirability and feasibility cannot be read straight off the outcome.

**Research-safe uses:** an illustration of relative costs under explicit assumptions; an historically contingent example prompting questions about productive development and distribution; or a bounded account of how wine-sector knowledge, capital, market access, and control interacted. **Unsafe uses:** a natural human monopoly proved by Portugal; vineyard stagnation presented as measured fact; a binary consumed/invested national history; or a theorem that ownership guarantees superior capture.

## Remaining evidence gaps and stopping boundary

1. **Vineyard productivity:** Recover matched estate or parish records for 1710–1780, with area, vine age, labor, weather, quality, and boundary definitions. Distinguish grapes, wine, saleable fortified wine, and value added. No such estimate is asserted here.
2. **Use of receipts:** Testing the draft's saving claim would require comparable household/firm investment and consumption accounts, separated by class and activity. Public works and stockholding refute universal noninvestment, but do not estimate its economy-wide rate.
3. **Treaty-specific causality:** Separate preferential tariffs from preceding wine-trade trends, bullion shocks, military alliance, and subsequent institutional developments. Existing macro counterfactuals do not isolate Methuen alone.
4. **British finance:** Trace sources of machinery finance at firms and industries, rather than equating export revenues, bullion, money supply, retained profits, and fixed capital. No marginal contribution of Portuguese demand to British industrialization is estimated here.
5. **Historical heterogeneity:** The treatment of small growers, laborers, enslaved producers, privileged firms, and consumers requires distributional evidence beyond national accounts and selected business archives.
6. **Textual precision:** A facsimile/critical treaty edition should precede any long diplomatic quotation; the exact 1817 first-edition text should be checked if that edition, rather than the 1821 revision read here, is quoted.
7. **Treaty termination:** The policy endpoint is established above, but a formal termination date requires the diplomatic instruments, later treaty provisions, and notice history. The 1831 debates show competing legal readings; 1860 is not a substitute answer.

These gaps bound the safe-use verdict; they do not justify postponing the corrections already established. This component has mapped the admitted Portugal assertions to verified facts, disagreements, analogy breaks, or explicit unresolved evidence. It does not claim to have resolved Portuguese economic history or completed the entire R-18 program.

### Additional leads and limits

The full texts of H. E. S. Fisher's *The Portugal Trade* (1971), the collective *O Tratado de Methuen (1703)* (2003), and Costa, Lains and Miranda's *An Economic History of Portugal, 1143–2010* (2016) were not read directly. Their claims were not promoted from citations in later studies into independent evidence. A. D. Francis's 1960 article and Robert Dimand's 2018/2019 Smith article were located at restricted publisher pages; metadata/abstracts only were inspected. They remain further-reading leads, not source-count substitutes.

Stefan Estreicher's 2025 “Wine and Portugal: A Brief History” was inspected for wine-history passages and source leads, but the verdicts above rely on the more directly relevant primary texts and specialist studies. Recent publication is not itself a reason to prefer a broad survey over treaty wording or underlying archival scholarship.

For this review, formal gains-from-trade reasoning is a conditional analytical result; treaty wording is documentary evidence; customs and firm records establish particular historical observations; reconstructed GDP and synthetic controls are model-dependent measurement and causal inference; generalizing to contemporary AI is an additional analogy. Keeping those evidentiary levels separate is the substantive outcome of the audit.
