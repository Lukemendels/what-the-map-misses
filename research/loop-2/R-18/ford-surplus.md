# R-18 subreview: Ford, work time, rebound, and the proposed surplus sequence

**Research date:** 4 October 2026. **Status:** research contribution for integration and quality review, not an author-adopted thesis or a remotely checkpointed program. **Claim scope:** C-044 and C-045; downstream R-01, R-04, R-05, R-08, R-09, R-11 and R-12. Source identifiers below are unique to this subreview (`R18-F01` etc.).

## Questions and admitted basis

The relevant passages are the assembly-line and gray-beard sections of `source/retired/03-portugal-didnt-have-to-lose.md`, the historical-script and Jevons sections of `source/drafts/cognitive-factory-floor.md`, and the senior-stock/training claims in `source/essays/02-dont-eat-your-seed-corn.md`. These are hypotheses and analogies to audit, including a retired and a predecessor draft, not canonical book doctrine. The stable ledger identifies C-044 as the Ford cluster and C-045 as the universal capital–labor–consumer sequence and expanding-demand cluster. This review does not alter either the source corpus or the accepted but revisable D-001/D-002 decisions.

The tests are deliberately separate: did the historical event occur; what does the relevant measurement cover; which mechanism is supported; what alternatives remain; and what, if anything, travels to contemporary AI? Verification of the first does not settle the last.

## Findings in brief

Ford supplies strong evidence that production redesign, lower product prices, changed job content and an unusually high compensation package can coexist. It does not supply a general wage law, a causal estimate of the assembly line's contribution to household prosperity, or a forecast that AI checkpoint jobs will fund a good life. The small task-time denominator and the multiple dates matter.

The contemporary veteran-engineer story has a recoverable reporting origin and a narrower defensible core than many viral retellings. It is evidence of a company's reported effort to combine expertise, training and automation. It is not an identified experiment showing that AI layoffs caused the quality crisis or that the company rebuilt an otherwise extinct apprenticeship pipeline.

The historical record supports possible demand expansion, complementary skills, consumer benefits and persistent rents. It does not establish that those outcomes occur in an invariant order. A claim that every major technology first gives nearly all gains to capital, then to labor, then eliminates abnormal profits in a consumer windfall is not established by the examined histories; its implied clean succession conflicts with observed coexistence of benefits and rents.

## 1. Ford chronology: what the famous numbers measure

### 1.1 A sequence of changes, not one installation date

Ford and Samuel Crowther's contemporary account is unusually useful because it specifies the operation. Experiments began with flywheel-magneto assembly in April 1913; chassis arrangements were subsequently changed. The account reports stationary chassis assembly at **12 hours 28 minutes**, an intermediate rope-and-windlass arrangement at **5 hours 50 minutes**, and **1 hour 33 minutes of labor time per chassis** after elevating and subdividing the line in early 1914. It explicitly distinguishes chassis assembly from attaching the body. This is the primary-source basis for the familiar rounded 12½-hours-to-93-minutes story. [R18-F01, ch. V]

The narrow numerical claim survives. The number is not the elapsed time to manufacture every component, acquire materials, make a complete vehicle, inspect it and deliver it. Nor is it necessarily the interval between finished cars: many workpieces can occupy a line concurrently. Calling this an eightfold improvement in *all automobile production* would change the denominator. The calculation 748/93 ≈ 8.04 is a ratio of the reported labor-time measures, not a measured eightfold rise in economy-wide total factor productivity.

There is an additional analogy break. The source describes moving skill into planning, tooling and experimental work while reducing production workers' discretion. It does not describe each station as an expert judgment checkpoint. A cognitive workflow may resemble a conveyor spatially or organizationally while allocating skill in the opposite way. Moreover, the antecedent was not simply one unchanged team of master craftsmen making an entire automobile from raw materials. Procurement, interchangeable parts, specialized equipment and successive changes to assembly were already transforming production. “Craft becomes station work” is a useful stylized contrast only if the intervening industrial system is not erased.

### 1.2 Prices fell before the moving chassis line

The touring-car series in the same account is preferable to splicing isolated museum prices without specifying year or body style:

| Ford/Crowther period | Nominal touring-car price |
|---|---:|
| 1909–10 | $950 |
| 1910–11 | $780 |
| 1911–12 | $690 |
| 1912–13 | $600 |
| 1913–14 | $550 |
| 1914–15 | $490 |
| 1915–16 | $440 |
| 1916–17 | $360 |
| 1917–18 | $450 |
| 1918–19 | $525 |

The source also records substantial production expansion and identifies the final two rows as war-work years. [R18-F01, ch. X, touring-car price/production table]

This chronology both supports and disciplines the story. The long-run reduction exceeded half, but much of the decline preceded the moving chassis line; it did not occur wholly between its introduction and the January 1914 wage announcement. Subsequent price increases disprove a monotonic nominal-price account. Quality, model configuration, cost accounting, economies of scale, vertical integration and demand were changing together. An observed price–quantity path is not a demand curve estimated with a supply instrument, and cannot by itself identify elasticity or allocate the causal credit among innovations.

The price decrease is consistent with productivity allowing a larger market. That statement should not be upgraded to “the line alone halved prices, which caused the five-dollar day.” Affordability and wage setting are different dependent variables.

### 1.3 The compensation announcement belongs to January 1914

The January 5 announcement associated with Henry Ford and James Couzens combined high pay with shorter daily hours. The well-known comparison is approximately **$2.34 for nine hours versus $5 for eight**, but the new total included conditional profit-sharing. It was not an unconditional doubling for every employee. The archival museum treatment records both the queues for jobs and the domestic conditions attached to qualification. [R18-F02–F03]

Ford's own description specifies eligible family-support categories and acknowledges intrusive administration; it also states that the 1914 change meant a **48-hour week**. [R18-F01, ch. VIII] The transition to the company's five-day, 40-hour arrangement belongs to **1926**, not 1914. [R18-F07]

Source criticism is necessary even with institutional pages. The Henry Ford's Sociological Department exhibit contains a caption assigning a 40-hour week to 1914, while Ford's account and the wider chronology distinguish the dates. The exhibit is valuable for its documents and intrusive welfare practices; that caption should not overrule the primary chronology. [R18-F03]

## 2. What explains the five-dollar day?

### 2.1 Distinguish an enlarged feasible surplus from a wage-setting mechanism

The claim “productivity made the pie larger” is an enabling proposition. It does not identify why an employer offered this particular compensation package rather than retaining additional profits, reducing prices further, expanding capacity, distributing dividends or changing recruitment. A complete explanation needs a mechanism connecting compensation to behavior, bargaining or the employer's objectives.

Raff and Summers' archival case study supports a broad efficiency-wage interpretation but is more qualified than its famous abstract is often made to sound. It documents job queues and sharp falls in turnover, yet finds that ordinary replacement-training savings are too small to explain the wage increase. Improved effort, coordinated production, morale and rent sharing matter; recession and simultaneous technical/personnel changes complicate causal attribution. Its productivity regressions use trends, seasonal controls and post-policy indicators, not randomized wages. [R18-F04]

Raff's later article explicitly emphasizes threatened collective action and rent sharing over narrow efficiency-wage mechanisms. The publisher's abstract and notes establish that this is a real disagreement in interpretation, not a consensus that the case has one simple solution. The article's full text was not recovered here, so the detailed evidentiary work rests on the accessible earlier study rather than a claim to have audited the later argument. [R18-F05]

The distinctions matter:

- **Recruitment:** a wage can be high enough to attract applicants without maximizing reliability after hiring.
- **Retention and coordination:** a cheaply trained individual can still occupy a station whose absence disrupts expensive complementary assets and other workers.
- **Effort and bargaining:** greater individual replaceability does not rule out collective leverage or costly production interruptions.
- **Compensating differences:** undesirable conditions can require a premium; the presence of job queues does not itself tell us which applicants understood the job, nor how welfare compares with their actual alternatives.
- **Paternalism and publicity:** motives can coexist with profitability; a founder's retrospective account is not a neutral measurement of his utility function.

The broad efficiency-wage claim means that paying above an outside option may improve the employment relationship's net return. It should not be reduced to “turnover was costly, therefore doubling pay necessarily paid for itself.” Nor should a before/after productivity increase be treated as the experimental effect of wages when the factory itself was being redesigned.

### 2.2 Why “pay always tracks replacement cost, never created value” fails

A statement of universal wage determination is much stronger than the historical evidence. Replacement options constrain bargaining, but the proposition must accommodate productivity, monitoring, contract enforcement, worker cooperation, collective action, institutional wage floors and the firm's capacity to pay. The question is not whether value creation guarantees capture; it plainly does not. The question is whether replacement cost alone is sufficient. Ford is an especially awkward case for that reduction because the economically interesting feature is precisely a compensation premium despite ample applicants.

For the book, a safer distinction is between **productive contribution**, **outside options**, and **the institutions or contracts that determine compensation**. Those variables can move in different directions. R-05/R-12/R-15 must investigate their relative weight rather than importing a wage ceiling from an assembly-line metaphor. High productivity can coexist with low wages; high wages can be a productive input; and a component of compensation can be a negotiated share of joint gains.

The five-dollar package also cannot establish a benign welfare bargain for every worker. Conditional eligibility and surveillance are not decorative context. They affect who receives the money, what autonomy is surrendered, and which households are excluded. The analogy should neither erase real income gains nor treat the employer's welfare narrative as a complete account of worker welfare.

## 3. Household prosperity, industrial prices and shorter hours

### 3.1 A falling automobile price is not general deflation

BLS's historical all-items CPI-U annual averages are **9.9 in 1913, 10.0 in 1914, 10.1 in 1915 and 20.0 in 1920**, on the 1982–84=100 base. These numbers contradict the claim that the new five dollars bought extra because industrialization was then driving down everything else a household purchased. General prices rose, especially during the war. [R18-F06]

A small arithmetic distinction prevents overcorrection. Five dollars is already about 2.137 times $2.34. Using the national 1913–14 CPI change produces an illustrative real ratio of roughly **2.115**, still above two. Thus the literal possibility of “more than double” purchasing power is not refuted by 1% inflation; its proposed *general-deflation explanation* is. This is an illustrative comparison of stated daily rates, not a reconstruction of Detroit families' actual budgets, eligibility, days employed or annual real earnings.

The stronger sentence about a house, car and supported family during the greatest broadly shared prosperity requires a defined period, population, counterfactual and outcome measure. A favorable conditional daily wage is inadequate evidence for annual employment security, housing access or the distribution of gains across the working class. If later twentieth-century prosperity is intended, it must not be silently backdated to 1914. The review therefore preserves the modest affordability claim while leaving the universal household-prosperity claim unresolved and unsuitable as a forecast.

Relative-price declines can raise real living standards even during inflation. Conversely, aggregate deflation can occur in a depression without indicating prosperity. “Deflationary” must therefore specify the affected service's quality-adjusted price, not serve as shorthand for all beneficial technical change.

### 3.2 The workweek has economic and institutional causes

Whaples' historical synthesis finds a long decline predating electrification. His research on the World War I period, covering 274 cities and 118 industries, treats growth and wages, reduced immigration, electrification, union strength and government rules as jointly relevant. He gives substantial weight to growth rather than a unions-only account. He also separates the eight-hour day from the five-day week and emphasizes discontinuities between historical hours series. [R18-F07]

This challenges two opposite simplifications. “Technology created the 40-hour week” ignores collective choices and law; “law alone created shorter work” ignores demand for leisure, fatigue, wages, labor supply and equipment utilization. Neither a chronological overlap with electrification nor one employer's policy identifies the national effect of the technology. Longer machine operating hours can also coexist with shorter individual shifts, so capital utilization need not require longer hours for the same person.

The contemporary BLS account of the Fair Labor Standards Act specifies overtime thresholds of **44 hours from October 24, 1938; 42 from October 24, 1939; and 40 from October 24, 1940**. Above-threshold work was permitted with at least time-and-a-half pay. Coverage was limited, including exclusions; this was not a universal legal ban on working more than forty hours. [R18-F08] The Department of Labor's legislative history documents the contested New Deal path and preceding government-contract standards. [R18-F09]

These sources support a conditional mechanism for AI: productivity might make shorter hours feasible, but its translation into leisure depends on preferences, bargaining, organization, rules and complementary costs. More output in a fixed workday is one possible choice. It is not the only equilibrium, nor a law of technological progress. The “creation of the standard forty-hour week during industrial electrification” should be rewritten as a multi-cause historical development, with separate dates for factory policy and legal standards.

## 4. The 2026 gray-beard story: verified core and inference limits

Keith Naughton's Bloomberg report, dated June 25, 2026, is the recoverable origin. Its public opening and an openly published Portuguese Bloomberg syndication report **350 veteran engineers hired over three years**, including former employees and supplier veterans. Executives describe mentoring younger staff, technical reviews and improving AI/automated tools. They attribute shortcomings partly to undervaluing experienced engineers. The evidence is management's reported account, not audited personnel histories. [R18-F10]

J.D. Power independently reports Ford leading mass-market brands in its 2026 Initial Quality Study, at 152 problems per 100 vehicles. It combines a **90-day survey of 78,514 purchasers and lessees of 2026-model vehicles** with **dealer repair-visit records covering hundreds of thousands of events**. This mixed early-quality measure is not lifetime durability, recall incidence or the causal effect of hiring. [R18-F11]

Four distinctions prevent the anecdote from becoming stronger with each retelling:

1. **Hiring is not necessarily rehiring after an AI layoff.** A former employee may have retired, moved voluntarily or left for other reasons. Supplier recruitment is not return of a dismissed Ford employee. The count should not become “350 people fired for AI and then brought back.”
2. **A management explanation is not an identification strategy.** Product mix, model redesigns, suppliers, manufacturing, software, inspection and reporting can change concurrently. A better quality ranking does not isolate one intervention's effect.
3. **Immediate correction and apprenticeship are different outcomes.** Reviews and mentoring may serve both, but the report does not measure juniors' retained judgment, future independent performance or the counterfactual training pipeline.
4. **The case also limits the strongest seed-corn thesis.** External hiring and use of veterans to improve automated systems make portability and codification empirical questions. They do not support a structural claim that useful knowledge can never be acquired from outside or partly incorporated in a tool.

Safe use: a dated, attributed example of reported complementarity between experienced personnel, junior development and automated quality systems. Unsafe use: proof of a universal inability of AI to learn tacit knowledge, a general law of irreversible expertise collapse after layoffs, or a measured return on rehiring seniors. A personnel-level chronology and independently measured quality/training outcomes remain missing. This is a resolved provenance lead with explicitly unresolved causal extensions, not an unresolved question about whether any reporting exists.

## 5. Jevons: attribution survives; universal exponential demand does not

### 5.1 Recover the original proposition

In *The Coal Question* (first published 1865; the inspected text is the 1866 second edition), chapter VII, Jevons argues that more economical industrial coal use can expand profitable applications and total consumption. He distinguishes domestic conservation and discusses both within-industry expansion and cross-industry effects. His historical mechanism is recognizable: cheaper effective power can enable previously uneconomic activities. It is not an econometric estimate of a universal elasticity, nor a theorem establishing exponentially growing demand for every cheaper good. [R18-F12]

At Ford, lower car prices and higher vehicle sales are ordinary market expansion. A strict resource-rebound claim additionally specifies which input is used more in total despite being needed less per unit: labor hours, steel, energy or capital services. A wage increase is a price of labor, not the quantity of that input. The sequence “cars became cheaper, sales grew, therefore the five-dollar day is the Jevons paradox” crosses these variables without supplying the missing equations or evidence.

### 5.2 What modern rebound research establishes

The UKERC synthesis reviewed more than 500 studies using evaluation, econometric, substitution-elasticity, general-equilibrium and growth approaches. It finds material rebound, considers backfire plausible for some pervasive production technologies, and stresses weaker evidence for economy-wide effects than for particular end uses. It does not make backfire inevitable. [R18-F13]

Gillingham, Rapson and Wagner place greater weight on credible causal estimates and distinguish a costless efficiency breakthrough from a costly policy-induced change in a product. Their review finds little convincing support for general backfire while retaining substantial uncertainty around induced innovation and macroeconomic growth. Its conclusions are not equivalent to “rebound never exists.” The disagreement concerns magnitude, scope, identification and appropriate counterfactuals. [R18-F14]

Both perspectives oppose treating a historical co-trend in efficiency and resource use as sufficient proof. The relevant counterfactual is resource use *without* the efficiency improvement. More use than last year does not imply more use than that counterfactual. In addition, efficiency in one input can alter other inputs, and a cheaper service can be limited by time, infrastructure or complementary resources.

### 5.3 A transparent conditional model for cognitive work

Consider a deliberately simple illustration, not an estimate. Let each useful completed task initially require `a` human hours, so human hours are `H = aQ`. If a workflow halves `a` while task volume grows 20%, `H` falls to 60% of its previous level. If volume triples, `H` rises by half. Both outcomes are consistent with more demand after lower cost; only the second exceeds the original human-hour total.

With fixed wages, full cost pass-through, no other input costs and constant-elasticity demand `Q = Kp^(-ε)`, labor-saving efficiency raises total human hours only if ε exceeds one. These are demanding assumptions. Real AI workflows mix model charges, setup, verification, rework, accountability and scarce decision-makers; cost reductions may not reach customers, and task definitions and quality may change. Token demand, reports produced, decisions improved, employment and wages can therefore diverge.

“Exponential” is an additional time-path assertion. Even elasticity above one says nothing about exponential growth over time unless one also specifies a sustained exponential price path and stable demand parameters. Cheap analysis may open new uses, but neither Jevons nor Ford establishes an unlimited appetite for reports or an unchanging supply of attention to act on them. R-01 should measure useful-task demand at the appropriate margin; R-12 should analyze general-equilibrium distribution separately.

## 6. Testing the universal capital–labor–consumer phase story

### 6.1 The categories are not a fixed pot passing between three recipients

The draft slides between factor income, producer surplus and consumer surplus. Wages are a production cost in the usual firm's producer-surplus calculation; workers may receive labor rents relative to their outside options, but their entire wage bill is not automatically a subdivision of a firm's producer surplus. Consumer surplus concerns willingness to pay relative to payment, not an observable national-accounts income share. Owners and workers are also consumers. Changes in product quality complicate comparisons further.

A fall in price can increase buyers' surplus while a firm's profit rises because quantity, cost and complementary revenue change. Complementary workers can gain at the same time as substituted workers lose. Therefore even convincing evidence of consumer gains would not establish that producer gains have disappeared or that labor necessarily enjoyed a preceding stage. R-11's accounting definitions should constrain R-12 before the sequence is reused.

### 6.2 Printing: entry, knowledge and political institutions, not one clock

Dittmar and Seabold analyze early European printing using roughly 299,000 known editions and merchant-manual data, with printer deaths as a source of variation in competition. They find economically consequential variation in content and prices; local oligopoly and collusive arrangements persisted even as the technology spread. Master printers combined capital, technical knowledge and commercial relationships. [R18-F15]

This is not a measurement of printing-sector labor's surplus share, much less proof that owners first captured virtually everything. It gives a stronger alternative causal picture: entry conditions, transport costs, local market structure and the content being transmitted shape who benefits. Technical availability does not imply effective competition, and human skill does not appear only after a purely capital-dominated stage. A study of cities' growth and religious institutions cannot be repurposed as a panel of wage, profit and consumer-surplus shares.

### 6.3 Steam and early industrialization: the strongest resemblance remains conditional

Allen's reconstruction and calibrated model describe British wages lagging productivity before the mid-nineteenth century, followed by catch-up as capital accumulation adjusts. The account depends on reconstructed wage/output/factor-share series, a production function and savings behavior; it explicitly disputes alternative historical estimates. It is not an experimentally identified sequence and does not establish a later disappearance of producer rents. [R18-F16]

Crafts' growth accounting finds steam's largest contribution much later than Watt's invention and little early aggregate effect. It distinguishes capital deepening from own-sector productivity and does not estimate a complete no-steam counterfactual. [R18-F17] This directly qualifies the preliminary premise that each paradigm's arrival immediately produces a TFP “spike.” An invention date, adoption date, diffusion interval and period of peak aggregate impact are different clocks.

The resemblance to an early capital-accumulation phase is worth retaining as a hypothesis. The book should not convert one contested British trajectory into a law for every technological regime, or treat all industrial change as the steam engine's effect. Delayed gains, uneven welfare and alternative distributional institutions belong in the analogy.

### 6.4 Electrification and computers: complementarities change work, but labor is heterogeneous

Electrification's relation to work time is covered above; detailed productivity chronology belongs in R-04. The safe bridge is that complementary redesign and institutional adjustment can matter, not that electricity mechanically transfers a fixed surplus to labor.

Bresnahan, Brynjolfsson and Hitt find firm-level evidence consistent with complementarities among IT, decentralized work organization, skills and new output. Their common panel has 1,331 firm-years; organization is measured in a 1995–96 management survey near the end of the period, and the authors caution about unobserved complements and causal interpretation. [R18-F18]

That evidence allows gains for some complementary workers while others face substitution. It does not establish a phase in which “labor” as an undifferentiated group claws back a predetermined share. It also challenges the assumption that useful redesign always turns workers into narrowly constrained stations: decentralization can be part of the productive package. Mainframes, personal computers and enterprise IT cannot be assigned one capture trajectory simply because all involve computation. The particular assertion that early mainframe owners captured nearly all producer surplus remains unmeasured in the inspected evidence and should not be presented as established fact.

### 6.5 Internet and smartphones: consumer benefits coexist with durable market power

The digital-choice evidence and valuation limits are consolidated in [R-11](../R-11/README.md#62-consequential-choices-improve-measurement-without-solving-every-problem). A valuation exercise is not evidence of a mandatory historical order in which different groups receive gains. [R18-F19]

The UK's 2022 CMA mobile-ecosystem study simultaneously recognizes useful, trusted products and identifies sustained market power and supra-competitive returns. Its evidence includes formal information requests, financial analysis, consumer research and developer evidence; it identifies network effects and control of distribution among the mechanisms. This is an agency's reasoned market assessment, not a worldwide experiment. [R18-F20]

These findings are compatible rather than contradictory: users may receive large benefits while gatekeepers retain rents. Widely available software and low marginal replication costs do not guarantee that distribution, switching, complements, brands or interfaces are contestable. Competition can move the bottleneck rather than eliminate it.

Apple's 2025 filing is an additional check against a loose story that mature smartphone-era corporate profits simply vanished: it reports $112.010 billion net income. Accounting profit alone is **not** proof of an abnormal economic return; it neither deducts a full opportunity cost of equity nor cleanly isolates smartphones from other activities. [R18-F21] The CMA's return analysis is the more relevant evidence for the stronger rent claim.

### 6.6 Broad pass-through does not rescue the universal chronology

Nordhaus estimates a small present-value capture rate for innovators in U.S. nonfarm business over 1948–2001. His well-known 2.2% result is model-dependent, using an appropriability estimate together with assumed depreciation, discount and growth rates; the paper notes international spillover problems and an unsuccessful dynamic specification. [R18-F22]

This is evidence worth confronting if the book overstates permanent owner capture. It is not evidence that every inventor begins by receiving almost all gains and then loses them in the same order. An economy-wide historical average, a firm's accounting income and a regulator's sector-specific return estimate have different denominators and counterfactuals. Treating them as mutually exclusive slogans would repeat the very measurement error the review is intended to prevent.

**Adjudication:** several mechanisms in the phase story are plausible and individually supported in some settings. The compulsory order, universality, initial near-total capital appropriation and inevitable terminal destruction of abnormal profits are not. “Eventually” without a horizon or observable transition criterion makes the final stage unfalsifiable. A conditional framework of innovation, diffusion, complementary investment, bargaining, competition and control rights is empirically defensible; adopting that framework as the book's thesis remains Luke's choice.

## 8. Implications, remaining gaps, and stopping conditions

### What is safe to carry forward

- A redesign can change task content and the scale of production; wages and consumer prices need not move in lockstep.
- Scarcer complementary expertise can become valuable, but productivity alone does not decide compensation.
- Expert knowledge can support present error correction and training; these benefits require separate measurement.
- Cheaper useful services can create new applications, subject to demand and complementary constraints.
- Consumer gains, worker gains and owner rents can coexist; history does not assign each a compulsory era.

### What the analogy cannot currently carry

A forecast of AI wages, hours, owner margins or employment needs contemporary evidence. A claim that station operation inevitably grows expert foremen needs longitudinal evidence on learning and promotion, not an attractive job-ladder image. A claim that seniors are irreplaceable after departure needs evidence about knowledge specificity, transfer, documentation and successor training. Contemporary Ford's supplier recruitment is a reason to investigate those margins, not assume them away.

### Evidence gaps that matter downstream

1. **R-09:** recover Ford's personnel history and distinguish rehires, retirement returns, outside recruitment and internal promotions; measure mentoring and retention separately from current defect correction.
2. **R-08:** compare station/checkpoint participation with deliberate practice and supervised consequential work on retained judgment and transfer. Neither historical Ford nor the 2026 report identifies these effects.
3. **R-01/R-12:** estimate response to lower *effective cost of useful completed work*, with quality, verification and scarce complements included; distinguish task volumes, token use, human hours, earnings and welfare.
4. **R-04:** examine electrification's productivity mechanisms independently of the workweek's legal and bargaining history. Do not presume that all lags were caused by physical layout.
5. **R-05/R-11/R-12/R-15:** define the income/surplus measures, identify bottlenecks and control rights, and specify the conditions under which rents are competed away rather than relocated.

The historical cluster is sufficiently researched to decide that the broad phase law and the immediate wage/Jevons bridge are unsafe. Unknown exact causal weights do not justify retaining a universal claim. Further archival work could improve the detailed history, but no unresolved source here licenses a deterministic AI forecast. Whether to retain a carefully narrowed Ford vignette, replace the phase narrative or remove it is an author decision; no chapter prose or thesis replacement is made in this review.

### Ford and work time

**R18-F01.** Ford, Henry, with Samuel Crowther. *My Life and Work* (1922). [Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7213), [full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7213/pg7213.txt). Read substantial passages in chs. V, VIII, IX and X, including assembly experiments, wage qualifications and touring-car table. **R18-F02.** Anderson, Matt. “Ford’s Five-Dollar Day.” The Henry Ford, January 3, 2014. [Article](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/fords-five-dollar-day). ### Limits of the review as a whole

This is a targeted historical/mechanism literature review, not a preregistered systematic review or meta-analysis. Searches deliberately covered confirming and disconfirming mechanisms and recovered original sources behind popular retellings. Evidence is strongest for U.S./British industrial history and large-firm/digital-market examples; it is not a representative global sample of all technologies. No archival records were privately inspected, personnel microdata acquired, paywalls bypassed, or source estimates replicated. Source-reported causal claims are distinguished from this review's counterfactual criticism. Identified access failures and abstract-only items remain labeled. The universal claim fails without requiring the review to reconstruct every industry's complete history; exact shares and contemporary AI forecasts remain subjects for the later programs.
