# R-10 Specialization and acquisition cost

**Research date:** 4 October 2026. **Purpose:** analytical literature review for *What the Map Misses*, covering C-020, C-021, C-022 and C-048. **Standing:** independent quality review passed; research judgments for author review, not a chapter, adopted thesis, or amendment of D-001 or D-002.

## 1. Main conclusion and admitted questions

AI can broaden the range of useful work undertaken with assistance, and there is credible evidence of narrower improvements in learning. Neither result establishes a general collapse in the cost of acquiring a second specialty. The strongest version of the seed's argument combines several different propositions: declining production costs, cheaper access to other people's knowledge, improved human learning, transferable judgment, changes in team composition, and a shift in economic rewards. These require different tests.

The evidence supports a conditional version of **C-020**: acquisition burdens are one cause of specialization; particular AI arrangements can reduce some of those burdens. The claim that specialization primarily reflected cost *rather than* cognitive constraints is poorly specified, because cognitive constraints help determine acquisition costs. **C-021** is plausible as a developmental design hypothesis, with partial evidence for its components, but building something with AI does not itself establish retained expertise. **C-022** has a strong pre-AI institutional precedent and an economically coherent mechanism; the scale and durability of AI-enabled community-builder diffusion remain unresolved. **C-048** should retain Jones as a theoretical reference while abandoning historical priority as an established fact.

The admitted inputs are the protected claim ledger; S-05, `source/drafts/essay-4-seed-simultaneous-specialization.md`; S-10 §6; and the accepted, revisable [D-001](../../../decisions/resolved/D-001-middle-seat-apprenticeship.md) and [D-002](../../../decisions/resolved/D-002-ownership-dominance.md). S-10's formulation that specialization *partly* reflects prerequisites is materially narrower than S-05's exclusion of cognitive explanations. This review preserves that evolution. D-001's foundational understanding, informed tool use and supervised judgment remain author intent, not experimentally established curriculum parameters. D-002's distinction between contribution and compensation is essential to interpreting an alleged integrator advantage.

The historical facts and selected-biography problems are already audited in [R-18 §3–4](../R-18/biography-electricity.md#3-jones-what-was-actually-measured-and-what-remains-an-extension). That review's Jones evidence capsule is incorporated by reference rather than reproduced here. The new task is to test the mechanism against economics, learning research, organization studies and contemporary AI evidence.

## 2. What would count as a partial reversal

### Outcomes and units of analysis

The review uses six distinct outcomes. **Access** means finding or receiving an explanation. **Assisted performance** means completing a specified task while the tool is available. **Retained learning** means changed human capability demonstrated without the relevant assistance after an appropriate interval. **Transfer** means successfully applying knowledge under a meaningful change in problem, context or representation. **Integration** means combining complementary contributions into a working result. **Frontier innovation** means producing validated novelty relative to an existing field, not merely undertaking an unfamiliar task personally.

These distinctions are not an argument that unaided performance is always the right production standard. A competent operator of a dependable human–tool system need not reproduce every component manually. Such system competence can be economically real. But it must be described as system competence, with its dependencies, rather than quietly entered into an account of the individual's human capital. Conversely, a learner may gain substantial personal understanding while producing nothing commercially useful. The two can move together or apart.

“Adjacent domain” also needs a definition. Professional labels can exaggerate distance: marketing and engineering staff in the same product organization may share customers, terminology, incentives and institutional history. Two tasks within one nominal profession can require very different representations or verification methods. A useful study should characterize prerequisite overlap, task novelty, availability of external feedback and consequence severity, rather than count occupational labels.

### A cost account that permits contrary results

For purposes of testing the book, total cost of acquiring reliable capability should include information search, prerequisite instruction, practice, corrective feedback, evaluation, and maintenance of knowledge. Productive access through a tool has a different account: tool provision, prompting, task specification, verification, integration, rework and continuing dependence. Both accounts require opportunity cost, including the time of teachers, specialists and users. They should not count a course fee in one arm while ignoring unpaid experimentation in the other.

This decomposition is this review's analytical framework, not a published estimate. It generates several falsifiable possibilities. AI may lower search costs while increasing time spent evaluating plausible errors. It may make an unfamiliar project affordable without changing the learner's unaided competence. It may reduce the effort needed for basic proficiency but increase the frontier's complexity, leaving the journey to frontier expertise longer. It may enlarge one person's productive scope while expanding the number of specialists needed elsewhere to build, maintain or validate the system.

A **partial reversal** therefore needs a specified margin. Less time to reach a fixed, independently assessed competence threshold would be a learning-cost reversal. More quality-adjusted tasks completed by an individual with stable support would be a productive-scope expansion. Fewer human handoffs at equivalent lifecycle quality would be an organizational change. These are valuable outcomes, but none alone establishes that society has reversed a secular trend toward specialization. That stronger proposition requires repeated cohorts, stable measurement and evidence about the changing frontier as well as the user.

## 3. Economic explanations do not predict one inevitable direction

### Acquisition and coordination

**R10-S02 evidence capsule.** Becker and Murphy's division-of-labor model treats specialist productivity as partly produced by concentrated skill investment. Coordination problems limit further division, including communication failure, incentives and hold-up. Its workers are initially identical by assumption, so specialization itself can create comparative advantage. General knowledge affects the productivity of skill acquisition; the model does not make market size the sole determinant. This is a theoretical construction rather than an identified decomposition of actual specialization. Its equal-task and complementarity assumptions are useful simplifications, not measurements of every occupation. [S02, pp. 1138–41.]

**R10-S03 evidence capsule.** Garicano separates the cost of acquiring solutions from the cost of consulting knowledgeable people. In his knowledge-hierarchy model, cheaper acquisition expands workers' own problem-solving scope; cheaper communication can instead make it economical for them to learn less and refer more questions. Time spent helping constrains specialist capacity. The model explicitly discusses expert systems as an acquisition-cost technology and permits overlapping knowledge acquired through work. It sets incentive problems aside and uses stylized problem distributions. Its comparative statics are conditional theoretical predictions, not evidence that every information technology decentralizes expertise. [S03, introduction, §§II–V.]

The contrast gives the book a sharper hypothesis than “AI dissolves boundaries.” An AI tutor and an AI answer service need not change human specialization in the same direction. A firm may rationally buy cheaper external answers rather than invest in each worker's knowledge. Alternatively, good instruction may make broader internal competence worthwhile. A tool that supports both routes leaves the outcome dependent on work design, incentives and evaluation.

Jones adds a dynamic question: what happens when the stock that must be mastered grows? His already-audited evidence and model do not license treating acquisition cost as independent of finite learning capacity. For R-10, the important extension is a race among easier acquisition, easier delegation and newly expanded demands. “Standing on more shoulders” can mean learning their content, relying on a larger infrastructure, or both. The fact that several margins can offset each other is precisely why task-level gains cannot identify the direction of the long-run trend.

There is no logical inconsistency between cheaper learning and continued specialization. If everyone becomes more productive in several activities, relative opportunity costs can still favor division of labor. If AI makes specialists far better at their own work, the opportunity cost of learning a second domain can rise even when the hours required fall. If a generalist can satisfy a small local need without coordinating several paid specialists, broader self-provision can nevertheless become attractive. These are contrasting implications of relative costs and scale, to be reconciled with R-03 and R-12 rather than collapsed into a single historical law.

## 4. Prerequisites, cognitive load and transfer

**R10-S04 evidence capsule.** Cohen and Levinthal frame absorptive capacity as the ability to recognize, assimilate and use external knowledge, drawing on prior related knowledge and organizational communication. They distinguish an organization's capacity from a sum of individual capacities and discuss a trade-off between shared understanding and diversity. Their paper combines conceptual development with firm R&D analysis; its individual-cognition discussion is not a randomized intervention on novices. Relevant prior knowledge is therefore a plausible complement to access, not a measured fixed prerequisite syllabus for AI users. [S04, pp. 128–35; empirical sections consulted for level of analysis.]

**R10-S05 evidence capsule.** Sweller distinguishes goal attainment from schema acquisition. His theory and computational model connect demanding means–ends search with attention unavailable for learning. A reported experiment involved 24 Sydney Year 10 students already introduced to trigonometric ratios. Conventional and nonspecific-goal groups did not differ significantly in total solution time; the latter made fewer errors on several subsequent reproduction measures. This small, constrained dual-task experiment cannot establish a universal superiority of any curriculum. It does challenge the assumption that more effort on execution necessarily produces more learning. [S05, especially pp. 277–84.]

**R10-S06 evidence capsule.** Gick and Holyoak separate noticing an analogy from applying it after a hint. Their experiments manipulate story examples and schema support before transfer problems. Comparing multiple analogs could aid transfer; even understood examples were not automatically retrieved when useful. The design relies heavily on a small family of laboratory problems, and the authors discuss limitations of the sequential hint procedure and use of earlier no-analogy controls. It establishes a mechanism for supported analogical transfer, not general professional judgment that transfers freely between domains. [S06, pp. 10–13, 22–23, 28–29, 34.]

Together these perspectives make the acquisition-versus-cognition dichotomy untenable. A prerequisite can be expensive because it takes time to form a usable representation, because prior knowledge is missing, because useful feedback is scarce, or because the learner is attending to the wrong aspects of successful execution. Merely supplying more information does not distinguish these mechanisms.

The same research also prevents a blanket defense of manual effort. If a tool removes extraneous search and exposes the causal structure of a problem, assistance may improve learning. If it removes the need to generate, test or explain that structure, it may impair learning. “Builder” and “reviewer” are consequently insufficient treatment descriptions. The relevant contrasts concern what the person actually predicts, attempts, diagnoses, explains and revises, and what feedback they receive.

D-001 can be operationalized without pretending research has selected a universal sequence. Foundational understanding should be assessed through the capabilities it enables: detecting a violated assumption, explaining a failure, choosing a valid test, recovering when support changes, and knowing when specialist help is needed. How much unsupported practice is necessary, and when support should be reduced, remain empirical questions. A commitment to understanding does not identify a number of years, a specific programming language, or a requirement to recreate every obsolete process.

## 5. Integrators have predecessors and their returns are conditional

**R10-S07 evidence capsule.** Hargadon and Sutton's IDEO ethnography links cross-industry contacts to organizational routines for acquiring, storing and retrieving technical knowledge. The study focused on designers and support staff in Palo Alto, used observations and interviews, and examined projects and artifacts rather than inferring innovation solely from executive slogans. Building and examining prototypes helped turn exposure into usable knowledge. This is a selected successful firm and a mechanism-building ethnography, not an experiment comparing randomly assigned generalists and specialists. It supplies a pre-LLM example of organized recombination; it does not establish universal brokerage rents. [S07, methods, pp. 718–22; acquisition/storage discussion, especially pp. 732–35.]

**R10-S08 evidence capsule.** Lazear models entrepreneurship as requiring a balanced bundle of skills and specialist employment as rewarding a stronger individual skill. His 2002 manuscript uses Stanford MBA alumni histories and course records; the survey response rate was about 40%. Breadth of previous roles and less specialized study predict entrepreneurial entry. The paper explicitly accommodates both selection of broadly skilled people and purposeful investment by would-be entrepreneurs. Elite alumni, retrospective histories and occupational self-selection prevent interpreting this as a causal training experiment or evidence that generalists earn more everywhere. The 2005 journal publication is bibliographically identified, but the inspected substantive version is the earlier manuscript. [S08, model; manuscript pp. 27–35.]

The meaningful integrator hypothesis is thus not that integration becomes valuable for the first time. It is that a new technology changes which integrations are feasible, how cheaply they can be attempted, and who can perform them. This formulation is compatible with genuine novelty without erasing earlier technical managers, designers, toolkits and user innovators.

The compensation inference is separate. An integrator can create value while being paid an ordinary wage. A specialist can acquire integration capability. A platform can automate parts of coordination. Competitors can copy a useful workflow. Lower entry costs can enlarge the number of successful builders while reducing the margin available to each. None of these possibilities makes integration unimportant; they make “returns migrate to integrators” a proposition about scarcity, demand, bargaining and competition, not a direct implication of task performance. R-05 and R-15 must determine the persistence and ownership questions under D-002.

The proposed one-judgment-specialty/many-execution-arms hybrid should remain a carefully bounded description. Some judgment can be portable, such as disciplined hypothesis testing or recognizing the need for a counterexample. Deciding which test is valid, which omission is dangerous, or which standard applies can require substantial domain knowledge. A single “judgment layer” is therefore not demonstrated merely by an individual's ability to produce work under several disciplinary labels.

## 6. Direct AI evidence on productive breadth and its limits

### P&G cross-functional innovation capsule

**R10-S10.** The published 2026 *Cybernetic Teammate* study reports 791 P&G workshop completers (776 complete surveys); note 9 excludes 35 nonrandom late/senior entrants. Quality regressions use 550 individual/team submissions. The preregistered 2024 experiment crossed solo versus commercial/R&D pairs with AI access; AI arms used GPT-4/4o and also received one hour of instruction. Participants were experienced employees working on their business units' early-stage problems. Relative to solo/no-AI, quality gains were 0.245 SD for pairs/no-AI, 0.373 for solo/AI and 0.392 for pairs/AI. Functional differences in proposal orientation diminished with AI. These outcomes assess assisted proposals, not subsequent unaided expertise or launched-product returns. Exploratory decomposition found better generated ideas without better selection of the best idea. Pairs/AI raised top-decile probability relative to solo/no-AI, but the comparison with pairs/no-AI was not significant (Table 9, p=.190). One firm, one-day virtual collaborations, selected professionals and bundled instruction restrict generalization. P&G employees are coauthors; the earlier manuscript discloses P&G gifts to Harvard and past consulting compensation. [S10, §§3–6, Tables 2 and 9, Figure 8, notes 8–15; source-version note below.]

### BCG task-boundary capsule

**R10-S11.** The published 2026 *Jagged Technological Frontier* article reports a preregistered GPT-4 experiment with 758 BCG consultants: 385 in a product-development arm and 373 in a business-analysis arm. Following an unaided baseline, treatments were no AI, AI, or AI plus an overview. On the selected 18-task inside-frontier sequence, AI increased completion and speed; final human-rated quality gains were 29.9% and 33.9% by AI arm, not the older headline of over 40%. For the deliberately outside-frontier case, correctness was about 84.5% without AI, 70.6% with AI and 60% with AI plus overview. The combined shortfall is approximately 19 **percentage points**, despite the abstract's percent shorthand. The failure involved integrating interview evidence with numerical data. Expert regrading supports the positive results. This is causal evidence for those treatments and selected tasks, not their prevalence in all work, a general prompt-training effect, or retained learning. Corporate collaboration and an elite sample constrain transport. [S11, §§2–5; Tables 4, 7, 10 and 12.]

These experiments should constrain the book's terminology. Greater breadth of a deliverable is an important productive result even if the user does not internalize every contributing skill. But a claim about acquiring a specialty requires a separate human outcome. The appropriate comparison is not only an unaided individual: it includes a specialist with AI, an experienced cross-functional team with AI, an existing software service, and a learner receiving equally costly non-AI support. Otherwise an experiment may establish improvement over one baseline while leaving the book's proposed advantage over real alternatives untested.

Nor should “boundary crossing” mean absence of a boundary. A portfolio of projects can include easy-to-check outputs and costly-to-check failures. The expected economic result depends on their mixture and consequences. Counting how many domains a person attempts, or how many artifacts an AI produces, can reward expansion without distinguishing reliable capacity from unsupported reach. R-02's lifecycle evidence and R-07/R-13's verification and authority analyses must therefore accompany any productive-scope claim.

**R10-S18 evidence capsule.** Doshi and Hauser randomized access to GPT-4 story ideas for 293 online writers, with 600 evaluators assessing eight-sentence stories. Assistance improved assessed novelty and usefulness, particularly for lower-scoring writers on a prior divergent-association measure, while assisted stories became more similar to one another. The intervention offered bounded initial ideas rather than unrestricted iterative collaboration. Ratings and embedding similarity measure distinct outcomes; neither measures a change in writers' retained creative skill or the rate of scientific discovery. This provides a useful warning against equating improved individual output with greater collective diversity. [S18, main experiment, results and limitations.]

Quality dispersion, conceptual diversity and scientific novelty are different dependent variables. A treatment could improve all submissions while making their ideas more alike; it could widen the quality distribution without discovering new principles. Thus evidence about the upper tail of assessed proposals cannot by itself settle whether the expanding knowledge frontier ultimately intensifies or relaxes specialization.

## 7. Learning evidence supports design dependence

**Shared evidence, R10-S12.** The campaign's source capsule is in [R-09, AI-mediated transfer and its limits](../R-09/README.md#ai-mediated-transfer-and-its-limits). Here the analytical question is whether evidence of within-occupation adaptation can establish cross-domain competence; it cannot do so without an additional transfer comparison.

**Shared evidence, R10-S13.** See [R-08, answer provision and task completion](../R-08/README.md#answer-provision-and-task-completion) for the Bastani study's evidence and limitations. R-10 uses it to distinguish an easier production route from an effective acquisition route.

**Shared evidence, R10-S14.** See [R-08, designed tutoring](../R-08/README.md#designed-tutoring-supplies-counterevidence) for the Kestin study's evidence and limitations. The cross-domain implication depends on which elements of an instructional package can travel to another setting, not on assigning the entire result to cheap querying.

**R10-S15 evidence capsule.** Prather and colleagues observed 21 introductory-programming students using generative AI through think-aloud sessions, interviews and eye tracking. Twenty completed the assigned problem, but successful completion coexisted with persistent metacognitive difficulties and apparent overconfidence among some students. More capable students could use suggestions selectively. The course already modeled AI use; participation was voluntary, the sample was small and the comparison with prior non-AI work was not concurrent randomized assignment. The study supplies process evidence and hypotheses, not a causal estimate of learning loss or population-wide inequality. [S15, §§3–5.]

The differences across these studies should not be reduced to a contest over whether AI “helps learning.” The treatments, learner histories, feedback, incentives and measured outcomes differ. Short-run practice scores, a same-session examination, a two-lesson post-test and job performance during an outage are not interchangeable estimates of human-capital accumulation. A favorable test result can establish more than mere artifact production without establishing durable transfer. An unfavorable result in one design cannot establish that assisted learning is intrinsically ineffective.

For C-021, the key unresolved causal comparison is **deliberately structured AI-assisted building versus a comparably supported alternative path to the same competence**. It is insufficient to compare motivated builders with passive reviewers and attribute all differences to their stance. Prior ability, available time, teacher access, motivation, task choice and survival through early failures can jointly select who becomes a visible builder. The author's admitted experience can illustrate a pathway and motivate this test; this review did not seek private validation or turn the testimony into a controlled result.

A further implication is that learning may occur outside the seed's builder category. Applying advice, observing the consequences, and revising behavior can be developmental even where the worker is not constructing software. Conversely, building can mean repeatedly accepting complete solutions. A stronger account should define developmental activities directly, rather than treating occupational identity or a self-description as the mechanism.

## 8. Historical novelty and the community-builder mechanism

**R10-S09 evidence capsule.** VanLehn's 2011 review distinguishes answer-level from step-level computer tutoring and compares them with human tutoring. It finds substantial learning benefits from pre-LLM systems under specified STEM comparisons. Its own method section acknowledges a truncated formal search, a nonreplicable informal search and single-coder selection; where multiple assessments existed it reported the largest effect. Content-matched comparisons also exclude some prominent classroom packages. It is useful evidence of predecessors, with clear limitations, rather than a universal effect-size benchmark or a proof that computerized tutoring can replace teachers. [S09, pp. 204–07 and 212–14.]

**R10-S16 evidence capsule.** Von Hippel's *Democratizing Innovation* explains why local knowledge of needs can favor user development, and why tools and communities can reduce its cost. His toolkit account explicitly enables users to design within bounded solution spaces while manufacturers retain other capabilities. Trial-and-error feedback, modular contributions and diffusion arrangements matter alongside access. This pre-LLM scholarship documents a predecessor mechanism for user-led construction; its selected cases and synthesis do not establish the population incidence or full costs of current AI-generated software. [S16, chapters 4–5, 7 and 11, especially pp. 147–50.]

The word “first” would require a demanding historical comparison. Lower search costs, reusable abstractions, remote instruction, expert systems and user toolkits can all ease components of acquisition or delegated production. A claim that generative AI combines these functions more flexibly is different from a claim that no predecessor lowered them. Conversely, identifying predecessors does not prove that earlier technologies reversed the *net* knowledge burden. The appropriate judgment is that unique historical reversal has not been established, not that novelty or substantial future effects have been disproved.

R-18 already provides the reasons not to use Rogan or Leonardo as causal identification. A biography selected for conspicuous breadth cannot tell us how common the proposed route is, how many equally supported attempts failed, or which component of skill transferred. Naming remains the author's rhetorical decision; selected success does not become representative evidence because the example is vivid or controversial.

The community-builder proposition is more precise if it describes a reduction in the need to transfer local requirements to a distant production specialist. Consider, as an analytical example rather than an observed case, a community organization with an idiosyncratic scheduling need. A person who knows the users might combine existing components and AI assistance, repeatedly test the result with them, and solve a problem too small to support bespoke procurement. The implied advantage concerns the joint cost of requirement discovery and implementation, not possession of frontier expertise in all the component disciplines.

That example has several potential failure conditions. Need knowledge can be incomplete or parochial. The intended users may not adopt the tool. A prototype may fail when records change, permissions matter, the original builder leaves, or a dependency changes. Lower construction costs do not supply ongoing support, institutional permission or succession. These are testable lifecycle requirements rather than reasons to dismiss small-scale construction.

**R10-S17 evidence capsule.** De Jong and colleagues examine 176 validated consumer innovations in Finland, drawing on a population-based survey and an additional likely-innovator sample in 2012–13. Innovators judged many creations useful to others, but reported actual commercial or peer diffusion for only 19%. The authors connect limited diffusion effort to benefits accruing elsewhere, while acknowledging that self-assessed general value and social-desirability bias could weaken the market-failure inference. This observational, mixed-product evidence separates creating something useful to oneself from diffusing it. It cannot estimate AI-community software adoption. [S17, §§3–5.]

The next research question is therefore not simply whether a nonprogrammer can make an application. It is whether intended users obtain a sustained improvement at lower full cost than a realistic alternative, and whether that result can be reproduced by other builders. The relevant denominator includes attempts abandoned before publicity. Free revealing, downloads and continued effective use must be measured separately. R-11 should then distinguish nonmarket benefits, own-account assets and recorded expenditures; R-05 should examine who is compensated for teaching, integration and support.

## 10. Evidence gaps and tests capable of changing the judgment

A discriminating study should start with a competence standard fixed before treatment, rather than measure how ambitious participants become. Recruit people with measured starting knowledge and assign equally resourced paths: conventional learning/support, generic AI assistance, structured AI tutoring, and AI-assisted projects with explicit feedback. Include relevant specialists with and without AI. A generalist-only design cannot identify whether the technology favors generalists relative to specialists.

Measure separately: assisted task quality; time and money spent by all participants and supporters; explanation and diagnosis; delayed unaided performance; performance with a different tool; transfer to unfamiliar cases; calibration of confidence; and maintenance or correction after deployment. Retention intervals should fit the actual claim. A same-day test cannot settle a multi-year apprenticeship argument. Where tool dependence is acceptable, evaluate reliable operation during normal use and recovery from changed or unavailable assistance rather than demanding unaided reconstruction of everything.

For a community-builder study, follow a cohort from initial problems through prototypes, deployment, adoption, incidents, maintenance and handover. Record projects that never ship and organizations that decline participation. Compare with ordinary software, low-code tools, volunteer help and professional contracting. Track whose time and infrastructure subsidize the apparently cheap project. The target outcome is useful sustained service, with distributional and access consequences, not simply code generated or a demonstration completed.

For the Jones extension, longitudinal evidence needs stable categories of validated inventive breadth, training time and team dependence. Patent classifications, citation practices and inventor naming change and are incomplete measures of expertise. A tool can simultaneously reduce individual prerequisites and accelerate frontier growth. Separating these channels would require more than observing smaller teams or younger founders after AI adoption; financing conditions, sectoral composition and selection into invention are competing explanations.

For integrator advantage, distinguish entry into broader work from returns conditional on entry. Record task quality, wages or fees, failure costs, bargaining conditions and durability under imitation. Test whether AI users become better at diagnosing cross-domain dependencies or merely better at generating persuasive specifications. These are future research designs proposed by this review, not results claimed to exist.

### Author-only questions

Luke must decide whether “simultaneous specialization” names supported productive breadth, retained multi-domain human expertise, or a deliberately broader human–tool arrangement. Those meanings have different evidentiary burdens. The review recommends making the unit explicit, but does not choose the thesis or rename the concept. Whether to retain the Renaissance and Rogan framing is likewise an author choice after factual corrections. The quantity and sequencing of foundational practice remain research and curriculum questions within the accepted D-001 position.

## 12. Review method and remaining limits

This is a purposive analytical review, not a preregistered exhaustive systematic review or a meta-analysis. Searches followed the admitted mechanisms: knowledge burdens; division of labor and coordination; prior knowledge and transfer; technology brokering; AI cross-functional work; tutoring, retention and novice programming; and user innovation/diffusion. Public primary papers, author manuscripts and scholarly syntheses were favored over commercial descriptions. Search-result summaries were used to discover sources; where search retrieval exposed substantive article sections, that access mode is explicitly recorded above.

Selection favored evidence that can distinguish mechanisms. Thus one-day workplace experiments are compared with education experiments and process observations, while historical organization research supplies alternatives to novelty claims. These heterogeneous designs are not pooled into one effect size. No source is treated as measuring multi-domain mastery merely because its title uses “expertise,” “learning,” or “creativity.” Failed retrievals were preserved as access limits; they did not license inference from an abstract alone.

The largest unresolved gap is longitudinal adult acquisition of adjacent-domain competence under sustained AI-assisted projects, measured against realistic alternatives and with transfer, verification and maintenance costs. Research on students or employees within an established role informs mechanisms but does not close that gap. Evidence on grassroots AI builders' long-term community use is also missing from this reviewed set. This means the relevant proposition remains open, not that such work does not exist anywhere.

The review's defensible stopping point is a qualified mechanism with explicit tests: AI can change the costs of access, production, learning and coordination in different directions. Broader useful work is already observable in bounded settings. The further claims about retained specialization, population diffusion, historical uniqueness and economic rewards require their own evidence and author interpretation.
