# R-02 — Software threshold and full lifecycle cost

**Research date:** 4 October 2026. **Status:** substantive literature review accepted after independent quality review; remote durability verified separately after publication. **Scope:** C-002, C-006, C-007, C-022, C-049, C-050, C-051 and the software component of C-053. This review neither establishes a canonical thesis nor changes the protected author corpus.

## Finding

The most defensible proposition is a **conditional reduction in the threshold for useful custom software**, not a common percentage increase in developer productivity. Controlled experiments establish that assistance can reduce implementation time or increase completed work in particular settings. They also establish a real counterexample among experienced maintainers. Public usage and session-outcome records and small end-user studies make broader participation plausible, but do not identify how many additional tools remain useful, secure, accessible and maintained after their creators stop experimenting.

The distinction matters in both directions. A disappointing average result on incumbent developers' existing tasks cannot rule out worthwhile software that previously would not have been attempted. Conversely, impressive output on newly attempted tasks does not show that the output was worth its opportunity cost, that it displaced an expensive procurement, or that a deployed system has become economical over its full life.

Three corrections are particularly important for the book. Its provisional OpenAI figures come from two different June 2026 releases, with different measures. METR's later work does not cleanly reverse its early randomized result: changed selection and time measurement obstruct that inference. Finally, a categorical opposition between proprietary visual platforms and portable agent-generated code is untenable. Interface, hosting, licensing, dependency and maintenance choices are distinct.

## Minimum viable software is a lifecycle threshold

C-002 is about an extensive margin: which tools can justify existence, and who can initiate them. It is not equivalent to asking whether an already-employed developer completes a fixed issue faster. For this review, a minimum viable software threshold means the smallest expected stream of useful service sufficient to justify the resources and risks of making, using and eventually retiring a particular tool. This is an analytical definition, not an estimated parameter. “Minimum efficient scale” has a more specific cost-curve meaning; R-03 should prevent that phrase from being used interchangeably without explanation.

A useful decision comparison is:

> Expected incremental benefit over the best available alternative must exceed specification, construction, verification, integration, operation, revision, risk and retirement costs.

The alternative might be doing nothing, continuing a manual procedure, changing the procedure without software, using a spreadsheet, configuring an existing platform, purchasing a service, commissioning conventional development, or combining these. A development-team budget is therefore not the universal counterfactual. Nor is the creator's unpaid time economically free. R-11 handles the accounting and welfare distinctions; here they enter as costs that a plausible threshold test cannot omit.

There are at least four separate thresholds:

1. **An executable demonstration:** enough behavior to test whether an idea is intelligible.
2. **A useful local instrument:** sufficiently correct for bounded use by someone who understands its limitations.
3. **An operational service:** other people can rely on it, with appropriate access, support, accessibility and recovery.
4. **An institutional system:** it is integrated with authoritative records, decision rights, replacement personnel and continuing obligations.

These are not inevitable stages through which every script must progress. A disposable file-conversion script may create all its value in one afternoon. Giving it a five-year support budget would be as misleading as giving an institution-critical case-management service none. The relevant question is whether obligations match exposure and intended use. A small user count does not imply small risk when the tool handles sensitive records or controls consequential actions.

AI could lower the first threshold substantially while lowering the third only modestly. It could also lower later thresholds by helping write tests, migration tools, documentation and diagnostics. The review therefore rejects both “the prototype is the product” and the opposite presumption that every downstream cost is fixed or immune to automation. The empirical task is to measure the whole cost vector and service quality, not merely append a cautionary paragraph to a generation-time statistic.

A further benefit is the option to learn cheaply. An unsuccessful prototype can be valuable if it reveals an invalid requirement before an expensive commitment. But this is a benefit of an informative experiment, not of accumulating unfinished applications. Counting trials, code volume or newly created repositories cannot distinguish learning from waste. C-002 should be assessed using accepted service and avoided mistakes, alongside creation and modification costs.

## Recovering the OpenAI lead

### The June figures are not one statistic

**R02-S01: the June 2 announcement and companion report.** OpenAI's *Codex is becoming a productivity tool for everyone* reports **more than five million weekly active users** and **about 20 percent knowledge-worker users**. The accompanying *The Next Era of Knowledge Work*, pp. 4–5, separates those knowledge workers from personal users, reported as another category exceeding five percent. It describes coding among knowledge workers, but is a promotional report without a reproducible sampling/classification appendix or confidence intervals for these headline counts. Thus “about one-fifth nondevelopers” loses its category definition: the source says knowledge workers, not the complete complement of developers. These are company-reported adoption measures, not an occupationally representative survey or verified software-production outcomes. The June 25 URL in the draft is not the appropriate sole citation for them. [Announcement](https://openai.com/index/codex-for-knowledge-work/); [report](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/the-next-era-of-knowledge-work.pdf).

**R02-S02: the June 25 paper and release.** Johnston and colleagues report that **25.6%** of sampled individual users in May 2026 made at least one request exceeding eight hours of estimated experienced-human work. The denominator is a **0.1% random sample of individual accounts permitting training use**, not all Codex users, requests or workers. February/March data are partial. GPT-5-mini estimates hands-on time; it excludes waiting, approvals, builds and AI runtime. Appendix F validates ordering against 1,000 Codeforces problems; correlations are 0.65 with fastest-five-human mean times and 0.69 with difficulty. This does not validate eight-hour calibration on general work. Section 3's personas are behavior-based, assigned from the modal request label over 30 days; nondeveloper combines knowledge-work and personal personas. OpenAI's own workforce is explicitly unusually favorable and unrepresentative. The paper measures requested work and usage, not successful completion or audited savings. [Paper, §§3, 4.2, Fig. 6, Appendices D/F](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/5d1e1489-21c0-43e4-9d42-f87efdbf0082/the-shift-to-agentic-ai-evidence-from-codex.pdf); [release](https://openai.com/index/how-agents-are-transforming-work/).

The inference permitted by these records is narrower but still relevant: the platform is being used beyond its initial programming audience, and some users request large pieces of work. The following steps remain separate and unmeasured in those headlines:

- a request produces a working artifact;
- the artifact is accepted by someone other than its creator;
- it remains useful after deployment;
- it is additional to what another available tool would have supplied;
- benefits exceed all costs and risks.

A persona inferred from coding behavior is especially awkward for testing whether people *outside coding occupations* have become programmers. Successful cross-domain use can itself change the behavior used to classify the user. Occupational background, usage category and newly acquired capability therefore need separate measures. The simple inference “nondeveloper usage demonstrates nondeveloper software production” would be circular if a classification rule labels substantial coding activity as development.

### The author's browser tools remain testimony

The admitted V3 account says an economist without traditional software-engineering training built **more than a dozen** functioning browser tools, that first useful versions often appeared in hours, and that some took weeks to harden. Its statements about the tools' existence, timing and usefulness are first-person testimony. Its assertion that they otherwise would not have existed is a counterfactual judgment.

This review has no independent public inventory, time records, user evaluations or deployment audits sufficient to validate that account. No private origin, personal account, internal tool or unadmitted workspace was inspected. The appropriate treatment is to preserve the account as authored, then distinguish it from independent evidence of a wider mechanism. A public demonstration, if later authorized and supplied, could corroborate existence; it would not alone establish prior infeasibility, lifecycle cost or representativeness. R-04 separately handles the AutoReviewer account.

## Controlled gains, real slowdowns, and incompatible denominators

### What the positive experiments identify

**R02-S03: Peng et al.** The 2022 Upwork experiment randomized 95 professional programmers, 45 to Copilot access and 50 to control. Thirty-five in each group completed the task and survey. For a JavaScript HTTP server, completion meant passing twelve visible tests. Conditional on completion, mean times were 71.17 versus 160.89 minutes, a **55.8% time reduction** (reported 95% interval 21–89%). The completion-rate difference was not statistically significant. Five assigned users did not finish Copilot setup. This is a short, incentivized task, mainly among freelancers in India and Pakistan, with a supplied skeleton and finite test suite; it is not a lifecycle-quality comparison. The conditional timing endpoint and attrition matter, even though success rates do not show a clear treatment difference. GitHub/Microsoft affiliations and sponsorship context are explicit. [Full paper, Study Design, Results and Discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06590v1).

**R02-S04: Cui et al.** Three employer-run trials in 2022–23 yielded **4,867 analyzed developers**: Microsoft 1,521, Accenture 316, anonymous company 3,030 after exclusions. The June 2025 manuscript's enrollment table is not that denominator. Randomization varied across individuals, teams and rollout dates; adoption was incomplete. The preferred precision-weighted IV estimate is **26.08% more weekly completed pull requests**, SE 10.3 percentage points, for access-induced adopters, not a universal intention-to-treat effect. Microsoft review proxies are broadly reassuring; Accenture's build-success estimate worsens. Proprietary code was unavailable for direct quality inspection. One additional disrupted Accenture experiment is excluded from the preferred pool and analyzed separately. Trials were business-driven and postregistered; two authors are Microsoft researchers. The published February 2026 abstract confirms the headline, while detailed inspection here uses the accessible author manuscript. [Manuscript, §§2–4, Tables 1–4, Appendices A/B/E](https://demirermert.github.io/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf); [published record](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535).

These studies strengthen a real efficiency mechanism, but neither estimates the book's exact extensive margin. The first supplies a task that all recruits are expected to attempt; the second observes employees within existing engineering organizations. Neither randomly offers domain specialists a choice between building a previously unaffordable tool and their best non-AI alternative, then follows that tool's benefits and costs through its useful life.

Even the positive percentages have different units. A reduction in minutes per completed task and an increase in weekly pull requests are not interchangeable productivity measures. Pull requests can vary in size, difficulty and economic importance. A trial can identify the effect of access on its recorded outcome without identifying a comparable percentage change in customer value, quality-adjusted output or labor demand.

### METR: the early RCT and the later selection problem

**R02-S05: early-2025 METR RCT.** Sixteen experienced maintainers completed 246 real issues in familiar mature repositories during February–June 2025, mainly with Cursor/Claude 3.5 or 3.7 when AI was allowed. Issues were randomized before work; the endpoint includes implementation and post-review effort. The estimated effect was **19% longer time**, interval **+2% to +39%** in METR's later recap, despite participants expecting gains. This is genuine contrary evidence in its setting, not an estimate for all programmers. The July 25 arXiv **v2** uses HC3 intervals and compares developer-clustered alternatives and a wider hierarchical bootstrap, which includes zero, in Appendix D. This supersedes older methodological wording still visible in the blog FAQ. Recruitment was selective, repositories unusually familiar and demanding, and compensation $150/hour. Mechanism investigations implicate generation review and repository context but do not randomize those mechanisms. [Paper v2, §§2–3, Appendices C/D/G](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09089v2); [dated release](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/).

**R02-S06: February 24, 2026 update.** The follow-up began August 2025: 57 developers, including ten returnees, 143 repositories and 800-plus tasks; pay fell to $50/hour. Returnees' time-change estimate was **−18%**, interval **−38% to +9%**; newcomers' **−4%**, interval **−15% to +9%**. Both intervals include no effect. Refusal to participate without AI, withheld high-expected-benefit tasks, differential completion and parallel-agent time accounting undermine interpretation. Surveys indicated 30–50% withheld some tasks. METR regards the signal as unreliable and suspects larger current benefits, but does not identify their size. Importantly, expected gains can select participants and tasks without accurately measuring actual gains. The authors' suggested downward bias is plausible, not a formally identified lower bound under all these simultaneous problems. [Full methodological update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/).

**R02-S07: the subsequent survey.** METR's May 11, 2026 report analyzes 349 technical workers from 359 respondents after quality filtering, collected February–April. Convenience recruitment and approximately 2% email response rates limit representation. Median self-reported value multipliers range 1.4–2 across questions; the speed median is 3. These are beliefs, not a replacement experiment. Importantly, the study asks whether AI changes *valuable contribution*, rather than only time on whichever tasks users now choose. Its quality flags include some declining reported uplift trajectories; robustness checks do not solve external calibration. The survey's distinction is useful; its magnitudes cannot close the lifecycle question. [Methods, Results and Data quality appendix](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/).

The later measurement difficulty is substantively important for C-002. If users change which tasks they attempt, a fixed-task comparison can miss precisely the new activity the book cares about. But it does not follow that refusing a no-AI condition proves a productivity gain. People can value enjoyment, reduced effort, flexibility, learning, or relief from tedious work even when measured time does not fall. Those are potentially genuine benefits, but require their own evidence and should not be relabeled as output.

Nor should the review narrate “AI initially slowed experts, then became faster” as an identified trend. Models, participants, repositories, compensation, willingness to submit tasks, completion behavior and concurrency all changed. A time series with those shifts cannot isolate technical progress. Conversely, treating the early result as permanently dispositive would hold technology and workflow fixed against the evidence that both evolve.

### Longer-run codebase evidence

**R02-S08: He et al., MSR 2026 version.** The January 26 v3 studies **806** visibly adopting repositories and 1,380 matched controls, using January 2024–August 2025 data. Older versions say 807. A committed Cursor rule file proxies adoption; control repositories can use other AI. Preferred staggered-DiD results show transient velocity gains and higher warning/complexity levels, but Appendix B's alternative estimator gives negative, insignificant quality effects. Codebase-size dynamics explain much of warning growth. The design needs parallel-trend and covariate assumptions; adoption intensity, model choice and unobserved project changes remain uncertain. Static metrics are not incident counts or maintenance dollars. The study is useful evidence against assuming persistence, with the authors' estimator sensitivity preserved. PDF Tables 2/6 were visually checked because HTML/text extraction incorrectly displayed significance stars. Discussion prose also contains a complexity percentage inconsistent with Table 2, so that number is not used here. [v3, §§3–5 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04427v3).

**R02-S20: writing versus shipping, revised September 2026.** Demirer, Musolff and Yang combine public GitHub activity, Microsoft telemetry and marketplace records. Their cumulative cross-generation summary reaches roughly **240% more commits versus 30% more releases**. These combine generation estimates; separate async-agent release effects are unavailable. Preferred estimates concern weeks 21–30. Prior-year controls require cross-calendar assumptions; adoption is not randomized, agent-only output partly uses interrupted time series, and release samples require pre-period releases. Entry covers four marketplaces; usage analysis covers three, excluding SourceForge. Usage concerns **new-entry cohorts’ first three months**, measured by downloads/users or iOS ratings. Disproportionately weak cohort uptake does not prove stagnant overall software use or welfare; production versus discovery/demand bottlenecks remain unresolved. September supersedes smaller-sample May headlines. [Working paper, §§4–7, Tables 5–6, Appendices C–F](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35275/w35275.pdf).

The unresolved comparison is not simply “experiments versus anecdotes.” Different designs trade off control, ecological validity, follow-up and measurement quality. Laboratory tasks supply unusually clear correctness criteria. Employer trials supply real workflows but limited visibility into downstream defects. Maintainer experiments supply contextual quality demands but narrow recruitment. Repository panels observe longer trajectories while relying on adoption proxies and strong counterfactual assumptions. Product telemetry has breadth but selected participation and no untreated comparator. A substantial review should keep these evidence types in conversation rather than average their percentages.

There is also a genuine possibility of coexistence: more people create useful small tools, while some incumbent projects face more review or complexity costs. That combination would support a bounded threshold shift and undermine a blanket engineering-efficiency claim at the same time. It is not a contradiction that must be solved by choosing one study to believe.

## Nondeveloper diffusion and the no-code alternative

**R02-S19: Anthropic's June 2026 expertise study.** Hitzig and colleagues analyze 398,198 interactive sessions from 234,751 users, excluding internal usage, third-party IDE/SDK and headless activity. Occupation is inferred separately from coding activity; about 30% is unclassified. Among code-producing sessions, transcript-defined verified success is approximately 34% for software/math occupations versus 29% for others. Expertise gradients persist with controls, but expertise and success both use the same interaction record: verification/correction behavior helps define expertise, creating shared-measurement concerns. Success includes such signals as tests, commits or explicit affirmation, not independently observed deployment or retained learning. Appendix validation chiefly compares classifiers with another model, supplemented by limited human review and internal telemetry checks. These vendor observational data strengthen evidence of cross-occupation participation while leaving task selection, causal skill effects and lifecycle value unresolved. [Report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-code-expertise); [sampling, classifier prompts, validation and regressions](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/4zrzovbb/website/a94728142a45694292336165947f8d6e3e1a357e.pdf).

**R02-S09: Weber's end-user feasibility study.** Industrial-engineering students at a German university built survey applications using AI, HTML/CSS/JavaScript, Google Sheets and Apps Script. Thirty-three usable individual responses included 21 master's and twelve bachelor's students; 24 reported functional completion. Participants received an example and introductory explanation. Grading, voluntariness and collaboration differed across groups; results are self-assessed, without randomization or a no-AI comparator. Backend integration was the most frequently reported difficulty. The accessible December 2025 preprint describes conference acceptance while disclaiming version-of-record improvements. This supports feasibility for selected technically adjacent novices, not a population completion rate, independent security validation, or cheaper maintenance than a conventional form builder. [§§3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.05666v1).

**R02-S10: Rao et al.'s LowCoder study.** Twenty participants recruited within a technology company completed four short ML-pipeline tasks each, using a visual interface with natural-language retrieval or keyword search. Eight self-identified as ML novices; these were not uniformly nonprogrammers. Natural language helped discover unfamiliar operators, while visual composition made structure inspectable. Completion across conditions was 66/80 tasks; knowledge gaps still obstructed novices. Administrators could nudge participants. The comparison tests complementary interfaces, not raw-code agents against all no-code products, and its main outcome is a trained pipeline returning a score rather than production validity. [§5.2, Tables 2–3 and discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.20015v1).

**R02-S11: open-core no-code counterexample.** Baserow describes a self-hostable no-code product with an MIT-licensed core and proprietary paid add-ons. Its own deployment comparison assigns server administration, updates and backups to self-hosters. Those are documented product arrangements, not independent estimates of low cost or guarantees of compliance. Nevertheless, they disprove the assertion that a visual/no-code interface necessarily entails an entirely proprietary hosted runtime. Open core is not the same as every feature being open. [Business model](https://baserow.io/blog/baserow-business-model); [deployment comparison](https://baserow.io/blog/baserow-cloud-vs-self-hosted-deployment).

**R02-S12: visual programming and operational responsibility.** Node-RED's official documentation describes an open-source, flow-based system with importable/exportable JSON, JavaScript extensions and Git-backed projects. Its security guide warns that the editor is unsecured by default and distinguishes editor/API protection from HTTP-node protection. This is low-code, not proof that every task is achievable with literally no code. It illustrates the relevant combination: visual abstraction can be portable, yet deployment still needs technical care. [About](https://nodered.org/about/); [projects](https://nodered.org/docs/user-guide/projects/); [security](https://nodered.org/docs/security).

The evidence supports treating “no-code versus AI coding” as a design comparison, not a civilizational sequence. Natural-language generation can produce source files, visual-flow definitions, spreadsheet formulas or calls to a managed platform. A visual representation can provide constraints and inspectability that help a novice supervise the result. Conversely, a platform may prevent a necessary customization or make exit expensive. Which is preferable depends on the task, the person's skills, shared infrastructure and anticipated changes.

The predecessor's “English is now the highest-level programming language” is defensible as a metaphor for an interface, not as a specification guarantee. Natural language can omit assumptions about time zones, identity, retries, cancellation, negative cases and concurrent edits. The generated implementation selects interpretations. Running code tells us that some interpretation is executable; it does not demonstrate that it is the intended one.

The same separation applies to portability. Possessing source code can ease inspection and migration. It does not make a database, cloud identity configuration, proprietary API, model behavior, trained staff or data rights automatically portable. A creator may replace a visual-platform dependency with several less visible dependencies. Conversely, a managed system can amortize operations and security expertise across many small tools. Its subscription can be the cheaper way to cross the operational threshold even where direct source generation is cheaper for the first screen.

C-022's community builder is thus a credible organizational possibility, not an established demographic outcome. Local knowledge can improve problem selection and user contact can expose misunderstandings quickly. Neither substitutes for every engineering skill. A sustainable arrangement might be a domain builder supported by shared deployment, review and maintenance capacity. The studies reviewed do not establish whether that arrangement is scalable, how scarce its support is, or whether it distributes benefits widely.

## Full lifecycle: what a working screen does not settle

### Security evidence cuts both ways

**R02-S13: Perry et al.** A 2023 CCS paper reports a study of codex-davinci-002 assistance: 54 recruited participants became 47 analyzed, 33 assisted and fourteen controls, after screening/withdrawals. Six assigned security tasks became five analyzed tasks after one proved underspecified. The sample was predominantly students. Some task-level comparisons show worse security and users can overestimate safety, but the pooled adjusted group coefficient in Table 3 has **p=0.056**; it would be wrong to translate the headline into uniform statistically significant harm across every task. Manual security coding and prompt inspection illuminate failure mechanisms. The historical model, small unequal groups, multiplicity and constructed tasks limit any current vulnerability-rate inference. [Full paper v3, §§3–6, Tables 3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.03622v3).

**R02-S14: Sandoval et al.** The USENIX 2023 study randomized 105 signups; 58 supplied analyzable code for a C linked-list assignment with eleven functions to implement. Assistance used code-cushman-001. Manual audits and functional tests found no evidence of a large relative security penalty in this setting. The authors' noninferiority margin was 10%, a chosen tolerance, not a safety standard. Figure 8 reports p=0.04 for severe issues per line in compiling functions and p=0.06 for test-passing functions; “no new security risk” overstates what follows. Both groups could still produce insecure code. Attrition, student recruitment, one language/task and an early model limit transfer. [Study design, §4.3, Fig. 8 and limitations](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity23-sandoval.pdf).

Read together, these experiments establish neither that AI necessarily makes software insecure nor that human comparison groups certify safety. Relative performance and acceptable absolute risk are different questions. Security properties also differ from ordinary functionality: a program may perform its intended operation for a benign input while permitting an unauthorized one. A test suite can check the examples it contains without covering a missing threat model.

### Operational standards identify obligations, not a fixed overhead percentage

**R02-S15: NIST SSDF 1.1.** The 2022 framework treats security across preparation, software protection, secure production and vulnerability response. Relevant practices include role assignment and tooling (PO.2/PO.3), design review, code analysis and executable testing (PW.2/PW.7/PW.8), secure defaults (PW.9), and continued vulnerability identification and remediation (RV.1–RV.3). Automation is explicitly part of the approach. This is technical guidance, not a causal study, a universal law or an estimate of AI's security costs. [SP 800-218, framework table](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-218.pdf).

**R02-S16: WCAG 2.2.** Conformance concerns full pages and complete processes, alongside criteria such as keyboard use, focus, labels, errors and accessible authentication. A visually attractive initial screen cannot establish that a complete workflow works for relevant users. The Recommendation also does not claim to meet every disability-related need. This review uses the technical standard, not a jurisdiction-specific legal conclusion. [Recommendation, criteria and §§5.2.2–5.2.4](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/).

The following is this review's proposed cost inventory. It is not a set of measured percentages or a claim that every tiny script requires an enterprise department.

| Cost or obligation | What should be measured | Why generation speed can mislead |
|---|---|---|
| Problem selection and requirements | User discovery, assumptions, acceptance criteria, rejected ideas | It may be cheap to implement the wrong workflow |
| Construction and revision | Human attention, elapsed time, compute, failed attempts, tool learning | Agent runtime and worker time overlap; neither alone is total cost |
| Functional verification | Independent examples, edge cases, regressions, usability and acceptance | Code and tests can share the same misunderstanding |
| Security and data handling | Access rules, secrets handling, dependencies, abuse cases, incident exposure | A locally successful operation may be unauthorized or unsafe in context |
| Accessibility | Complete task success with relevant input and assistive methods | Appearance is only one part of use |
| Integration | Identity, schemas, migrations, API changes, reconciliation and failure recovery | A standalone demonstration omits boundary behavior |
| Deployment and operation | Hosting, monitoring, backups, restore tests, on-call effort, outages | The bill continues after the code is written |
| Maintenance and succession | Change effort, documentation, dependency updates, handover to another maintainer | Cheap creation can create many continuing obligations |
| Governance and retirement | Ownership of decisions, audit needs, support limits, deletion/export and replacement | Abandoned tools can retain data or remain unofficial authorities |

Several costs are correlated. A poorly understood implementation can simultaneously increase review effort, debugging time and succession risk. Their sum should not be estimated by multiplying a single failure probability by a single repair cost and assuming independence. Equally, shared libraries, centrally maintained templates and a supported hosting environment can spread fixed costs across applications. A serious threshold account needs those economies of shared support, not just isolated creators.

Inherited controls deserve examination rather than automatic retention. For example, a duplicated approval could be unnecessary bureaucracy, a segregation-of-duties control, or a workaround for an unreliable data feed. The redesign question is what failure it addresses, who bears the loss, and whether a better replacement actually covers that failure. This is an analytical method for respecting C-051's caveat; no study here proves that every inherited control contains a wise lesson.

### Maintenance evidence that is not ready for a headline

**R02-S17: Liu et al., March 2026 preprint, screened with reservations.** This repository-mining study attributes changes using AI traces in Git metadata and applies static analyzers to selected public Python/JavaScript/TypeScript projects, then checks issue survival at HEAD. Its apparent breadth does not supply a human counterfactual, realized remediation cost or comparable follow-up duration. More seriously, §5.1 reports issue-bearing commits as 8.7% overall while the abstract says every included assistant exceeds 15%; the accessible replication link is a placeholder. These unresolved denominator/recovery problems prevent using its headline incidence or survival rate as a book fact. It remains a research lead, not support for a quantified maintenance burden. [Methods, Table 2/Fig. 6, limitations and replication statement](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28592v1).

The cost issue therefore remains open quantitatively. The record supports making maintenance an explicit outcome, not assigning it an invented constant or asserting that it always consumes initial gains. A tool with rapidly changing external dependencies may be expensive to keep alive; one with stable inputs and a bounded purpose may need little further work. The population distribution, including abandoned and unsuccessful tools, is what a threshold claim ultimately needs.

## Endogenous construction: a genuine loop with an unmeasured net effect

**R02-S18: OpenAI's harness-engineering account.** Ryan Lopopolo's February 11, 2026 report describes a five-month internal product effort using agent-generated code, tests, developer tools, repository-management scripts and release infrastructure. Humans specify intent, translate user feedback and validate outcomes. The account describes recurring corrective work and explicitly leaves multiyear architectural coherence unresolved. Its claimed acceleration is an internal counterfactual estimate, not a randomized comparison. It corroborates the feasibility of agents helping construct the machinery that manages agents; it does not establish a general lifecycle return. [Full account, particularly agent-generated artifacts, autonomy, cleanup and limitations](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/).

C-007 is stronger than a claim that developers use a coding autocomplete. A model can help create a wrapper, evaluator, integration or recovery mechanism that changes what subsequent model use can accomplish. That is an endogenous *software-construction* loop: model-assisted changes alter the environment in which future model-assisted work occurs. It need not involve retraining model weights, persistent personal development or autonomous recursive intelligence improvement. Those are separate questions for R-14/R-16.

The sign of the loop is not guaranteed. An improved test harness can make future errors easier to detect; an incorrect shared assumption can instead spread through generated implementation, generated tests and generated review. Better context may lower repeated explanation costs; more accumulated code may raise comprehension and maintenance costs. Faster repair may increase useful experimentation; it may also encourage deployment before consequences are understood. All are mechanisms, not measured inevitabilities.

An adequate comparison would hold intended service and acceptance standards fixed while varying whether AI helps build its surrounding infrastructure. It would record the initial investment, repeated-use savings, rejected outputs, incidents and later revisions. It should include human-built shared infrastructure and existing managed platforms as alternatives. Otherwise an apparent reinforcing loop could simply be an expensive one-off reimplementation of infrastructure already available elsewhere.

This yields a conditional implication for C-006: if both cognition and some complementary software changes become cheaper, system-level redesign is a sensible object of analysis. It does not follow that redesign is always worthwhile, that every bottleneck is software, or that authorization and organizational agreement become cheap at the same rate. Production can remain constrained by physical processes, scarce review, conflicting incentives or legitimate limits on action.

### Search and inclusion boundaries

The search began with the admitted OpenAI and METR URLs, then followed their primary papers and later updates. Additional searches targeted controlled professional coding, nondeveloper/end-user construction, visual-language complementarity, security experiments, maintenance trajectories and official lifecycle documentation. Search summaries were leads only. DORA and SPACE were inspected as leads but their report bodies were not sufficiently examined for inclusion here; their headline claims are not used. An additional proposed agent-code sustainability study describing expected results was not treated as completed evidence. A broader systematic review could identify further studies; this is a focused analytical review, not a preregistered exhaustive meta-analysis.

No common effect size is calculated. Outcome units, selected populations, intervention vintages, assignment mechanisms and follow-up windows do not justify pooling. Author affiliations, corporate incentives and safety-research aims are potential interpretive considerations, not reasons to presume dishonesty or discard a result.

## Evidence gaps, next tests and synthesis dependencies

1. **Direct extensive-margin identification.** Recruit domain specialists with real unmet needs; specify their best alternatives before access; randomize assistance or rollout; measure useful accepted tools, failed/abandoned attempts and user benefit. Counting generated apps without a counterfactual cannot establish additionality.
2. **Longitudinal lifecycle cost.** Follow projects long enough to observe meaningful revisions, dependency changes, recovery and personnel handover. Record the distribution, not just successful showcase projects. Standardize exposure and intended service rather than imposing one arbitrary maintenance horizon.
3. **Nondeveloper representativeness.** Distinguish occupation, formal training, domain expertise, prior coding, AI familiarity and platform-provided support. Existing evidence overrepresents technically adjacent or unusually enabled users.
4. **A genuine alternatives comparison.** Compare conventional development, configured existing tools, managed no-code, open visual systems and AI-written code at the same acceptance standard. Without this, the assertion “otherwise uneconomical” rests on an artificially expensive alternative.
5. **Independent safety and accessibility.** Measure residual defects and complete-task accessibility, including cases creators do not know to request. The absence of a security incident in a brief observation window is not a reliable rate estimate.
6. **Concurrency and task substitution.** Separately record human attention, elapsed completion, agent runtime, simultaneous tasks and quality-adjusted contribution. Record tasks that would never be attempted without AI, rather than excluding them or pricing them at an implausible manual rate.
7. **Selection after adoption.** The most enthusiastic adopters may refuse randomized withdrawal. More attractive compensation, shorter experiments and developer-level rollout can help, but no design removes all tradeoffs. Reasons for refusal and noncompletion belong in the evidence.
8. **Maintenance and learning are connected but distinct.** A local creator's inability to hand over a tool could reflect documentation, organizational ownership, skill retention or simply a short intended lifespan. R-08/R-09/R-10 should not infer a learning mechanism from construction success alone.
9. **Control and capture remain separate.** The ability to modify files does not establish authority to deploy them, a right to take them on departure, or bargaining power over surplus. R-05/R-15 must retain these distinctions; D-002 is not empirically proven by a working repository.
10. **Feedback-loop efficacy.** Compare repeated tasks with and without AI-built evaluation, recovery and integration infrastructure. The relevant evidence is improved accepted outcomes after the investment, not the presence of a self-referential architecture.

The immediate author-facing choice is rhetorical rather than a missing empirical permission: whether to center the bounded threshold proposition or preserve the predecessor's stronger platform/ownership prescription as an explicitly examined earlier view. This review recommends no automatic manuscript substitution. It supplies a clear research boundary: **custom software can become viable for additional purposes and creators while secure, durable institutional software remains a coupled engineering and organizational achievement.** Its extent, distribution and lifetime net value remain empirical questions.
