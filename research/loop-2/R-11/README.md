# R-11 Own-account capital and welfare measurement

**Project:** *What the Map Misses*  
**Research date:** 4 October 2026  
**Admitted claims:** C-023, C-024 and C-025; accounting constraints on R-12, with links to R-02, R-05 and R-15  
**Status:** Substantial literature review accepted after independent quality review. Research judgments are not author adoption; remote durability is verified separately after publication.

## Finding

The defensible proposition is that useful software need not have a separately priced sale. The more ambitious proposition that AI is creating a large, previously unmeasured stock of household capital remains unestablished. Those are different claims. Existing accounting already accommodates some production without a sale, while other useful activity lies outside its principal production boundary. Moreover, being inside a conceptual boundary does not guarantee that statistical surveys and estimation procedures measure an activity well.

C-023 is **supported within a conditional, mechanism-level scope** and **unresolved as a claim about the scale of AI-induced diffusion**. C-024 is **supported**, provided that “capital-service value” is distinguished from the total benefit obtained by an asset's users. C-025 is **supported as a conditional proposition**, with its quantitative macroeconomic and distributional implications **unresolved**: a divergence between software revenue and welfare is possible, but its size and the direction of GDP effects cannot be inferred from a zero price. No conclusion about inevitable deflation, employment replacement or equitable access follows.

The review's central analytical contribution is a sequence of questions: who produced the software, for what use, under what rights and remuneration arrangement, for how long, using which scarce inputs, and compared with what alternative? “Free software” supplies none of these answers by itself. [R11-S01–S17]

## 1 Admitted argument and research boundary

The claim ledger's R-11 program asks for distinctions among household and volunteer software, firm own-account production, maintenance, opportunity costs, capital services, consumer surplus and GDP. The admitted synthesis, `source/synthesis/2026-09-11-current-author-synthesis.md`, §7, proposes software that needs little monetary investment, produces no software-development purchase or subscription, and nevertheless expands productive capacity or welfare. It explicitly calls the macroeconomic implications hypotheses. The review preserves that caution rather than importing the stronger surplus sequence from predecessor texts.

The protected ledger states the three propositions as follows:

- C-023: “Low-cost bespoke software can supply productive services or welfare without a conventional software purchase, startup or subscription.”
- C-024: “Production cost, capital-service value and welfare value are distinct.”
- C-025: “Bespoke software and abundant cognition may expand output or welfare without corresponding measured market revenue; macro and distribution outcomes are unsettled.”

The relevant historical correction has already been made in [R-18](../R-18/README.md): expenditure, services, income and welfare are separate objects. This review does not reproduce its historical capsules. It asks how those distinctions work when the object is a reusable program, a free copy, a household activity or an unpaid contribution. The source corpus and historical claim ledger remain unchanged.

The evidence combines accounting standards, actual U.S. compilation guidance, experimental accounts, software valuation exercises, consumer valuation experiments and macroeconomic measurement critiques. Their evidentiary roles differ. An accounting manual defines a convention; it does not estimate the social return to AI. A cost model estimates resources under assumptions; it does not reveal willingness to pay. A deactivation experiment estimates a particular counterfactual for participating users; it does not measure the productive contribution of all software. The review therefore does not average incompatible estimates into a synthetic “true value.”

The international baseline is the 2008 SNA and its adopted 2025 successor. The implementation baseline examined here is the United States. European or other national implementations are not presumed identical. Publication of a new international standard is not a revision to every country's historical GDP series.

## 2 What must be kept separate

For the book's purposes, six objects should be named rather than collapsed into “value.” These are an analytical vocabulary, not a proposed new national-accounts classification.

1. **Cash outlay:** the amount the particular builder or user pays. Subsidies, gifts, employer-provided resources and free licenses can make this small without making resource use small.
2. **Production resources:** the labor, purchased inputs and existing equipment services used to make a functioning asset. A national-accounts valuation of production is one disciplined way of assigning money values to those resources; an individual's opportunity-cost calculation can differ.
3. **Asset stock:** the surviving resource available after production. Counting a repository, a file or a successful first run is not evidence of a durable, usable asset.
4. **Capital-service input:** the services the asset supplies during a period. In growth accounting this is an input into production; it is not the entire output of the workflow using the asset.
5. **Operating and distribution outcomes:** output quality, time saved, wages, profits, fees and who bears risk. These are outcomes of a joint production system, not attributes discoverable from its purchase price alone.
6. **Welfare change:** the benefit of the changed situation relative to a specified alternative, including nonmarket uses of time and relevant harms. This needs an explicit valuation and aggregation framework.

There is also a distinction between a **level** and a **growth rate**. An indispensable tool may have a large value in a world where it suddenly disappears but contribute little additional growth this year if nothing about it changed. A stock accumulated over many years cannot be inserted into an annual GDP flow. Likewise, a one-off level gain cannot be counted as new growth again every year. A durable tool can legitimately supply recurring annual service or welfare benefits relative to a continuing feasible counterfactual; that is a flow of benefits, not repeated increments to the growth rate. This discipline will be essential in R-12.

## 3 The production boundary is not the cash register

### 3.1 Three forms of output already exist in the accounts

The December 2024 BEA *NIPA Handbook*, chapter 2, distinguishes market output, output retained for own final use and nonmarket output. Its own-use examples explicitly include software developed or improved in-house. Comparable market prices or production costs can supply valuations. Government and nonprofit nonmarket output is also included, generally on a cost basis. Imputations can recognize production without an observable price or transaction. Thus “no sale” is an invalid general test for GDP exclusion. [R11-S01, pp. 2-4–2-5]

This matters for the book's proposed community builder. “There is no software market for this task” may mean no vendor can profitably sell a specialized product. It does not mean that all inputs, resulting output or capital formation lack an accounting location. An employee making an internal scheduling tool and a person making the identical tool during unpaid leisure need not generate the same entries. The distinction concerns the institutional activity and the production boundary, not whether one program is intrinsically more useful.

### 3.2 Firm own-account software is an existing investment category

BEA chapter 6 includes purchased and own-account software in investment, with fixed assets used in production for more than one year. New or significantly enhanced in-house software is valued by compensation, intermediate inputs and capital services. Estimation uses selected occupations, industry wages and occupation-specific time-use factors, with adjustments against double counting custom software. Software originals for sale or incorporation into products are instead included in R&D. Own-account volume estimates use the custom-software deflator. National accounting need not follow a firm's expensing choices. [R11-S02, pp. 6-2–6-5, 6-37–6-39]

Two separate measurement risks follow; neither has been quantified here. First, if software-making spreads to occupations omitted from an estimator, its coverage may deteriorate. Second, if useful output per input changes rapidly, a historical input/price relationship may become less informative. The opposite error is also possible: counting more automatically generated text or code as proportionally more usable software could overstate production. Therefore nondeveloper participation and code volume are research signals, not ready-made corrections to GDP.

The relevant empirical question is not whether statisticians have ever heard of internal software. It is whether their current identification, time allocation, quality adjustment and service-life assumptions continue to describe the new activity.

### 3.3 A software original and its copies are different accounting objects

The 2008 SNA connects economic ownership to benefits and associated risks. Its software asset criterion requires use in production beyond one year; in-house valuation uses an estimated basic price or production costs. Crucially, §10.101 records no owner-to-recipient flow when copies are distributed free, while an original can remain an asset if the owner expects benefits. The same standard's nonprofit satellite-account discussion imputes volunteer labor at comparable workers' remuneration, not volunteers' personal opportunity costs. [R11-S03, §§3.26–3.31, 10.100–10.111, 23.34–23.36]

This makes “the software is already counted” an incomplete rebuttal to every measurement concern. Counting creation of an original does not provide a complete account of every beneficiary's use. But “millions of free copies are uncounted” is equally incomplete: multiplying the full historical development cost by every download does not establish millions of separate equivalent development investments. Reuse is precisely the mechanism that makes the distinction consequential.

### 3.4 The current standard clarifies who the producer is

The UN adopted the 2025 SNA in 2025; the searchable text inspected remains labelled **pre-edit**. Its §§7.45 and 22.71–22.75 distinguish employed/commercial open-source production, including commercial unincorporated household enterprises, from unpaid independent volunteers. The former can be own-account investment; the latter's originals are outside the integrated production boundary. A developer can own an asset without license revenue. Substitution toward free copies can reduce measured user investment even when supplier output remains recorded. Extended accounts for free platform services are encouraged; Annex 4 §A4.118 calls the free-products treatment a clarification rather than a conceptual change. Separately, §A4.32 adds a net return to capital in nonmarket cost-based output, unlike the BEA handbook vintage below. [R11-S04]

The important distinction is therefore not “firms versus every person classified in the household sector.” Household-sector entrepreneurs can be market producers. Nor does “open source” mean “unpaid”: a license describes permitted uses, not the financing of every contributor's work. The review consequently rejects a categorical claim that all open-source output is missing from GDP.

### 3.5 Government and nonprofit production require their own treatment

BEA chapter 9's current nonmarket government valuation includes labor, materials and consumption of fixed capital. Its discussion treats depreciation as a partial capital-service measure and assumes zero net return on general-government fixed assets. Real government consumption is generally input-based, which restricts measured productivity change. Government own-account investment is separately recognized. [R11-S05, pp. 9-2–9-5 and methodology tables]

These conventions are important for public-service software. A better tool may improve service quality without a proportionate change in an input-based volume measure. Conversely, a costlier failed system may use measurable resources without improving service. Neither observation makes the entire government sector invisible. It identifies a particular problem of measuring outcomes and productivity. For a volunteer-supported nonprofit, the analyst must separately establish which paid inputs, asset production and donated work are represented; incorporation alone is not evidence that every volunteer hour has been imputed.

### 3.6 Standards and implementation are different vintages

The BEA handbook landing page says its chapters reflect the 2024 annual update and do not incorporate the 2025 update's potentially temporary source-data changes. More decisively, BEA's March 2026 data-assets working paper describes NIPAs as implementing SNA 2008 and presents **experimental**, proposed estimates for the 2025 standard's data-capitalization change. It identifies a 2029–2030 international implementation target window. [R11-S06, introduction; R11-S02 access record]

This review does not claim to have audited every 2026 NIPA release. It reports the public methodological vintages actually inspected. No experimental series below is silently relabelled official headline GDP. This is especially important for later claims about machine “experience”: neither a new asset label nor a technical demonstration of persistence establishes a recognized, measured asset in an already published national series.

## 4 Cheap to make is a hypothesis about a cost function

### 4.1 Which cost has become cheap

The synthesis's phrase “production cost can approach zero” needs a denominator and a boundary. Possible claims include a low price for one generated draft, a low incremental cost given existing equipment and subscriptions, a low cost of a deployable application, or low total cost over the application's useful life. These are increasingly demanding propositions.

An analytical lifecycle ledger should record discovery and requirements, construction, verification, deployment, data preparation, integration, documentation, user training, ongoing operation, updates, incident response and retirement. Existing C-051 already recognizes testing, security, maintenance, accessibility and governance. R-11 adds that these activities should not all be assigned to the initial software asset by verbal fiat. Some create or substantially improve an asset; some keep it running; some are complementary capabilities. Exact capitalization follows the relevant accounting rules, while the decision to adopt needs the whole economic comparison.

This distinction allows the cheap-software thesis to survive without assuming away its difficult parts. If AI compresses construction, a previously unaffordable project may become worthwhile even if maintenance does not fall. But the viable set will not expand without bound. If maintenance, integration or supervision is the binding cost, removing first-draft expense may change little. If lower construction cost encourages many experimental tools, abandonment and duplication may rise as well. That is not necessarily failure: cheap experiments can be useful. It does mean that starts and surviving productive assets cannot be treated as the same statistic.

### 4.2 Opportunity cost is real but not always a wage

For a volunteer, “unpaid” specifies a cash flow, not the value of forgone alternatives. Yet valuing all personal time at the person's professional wage is also a strong assumption. The person may not be able to sell another hour at that wage; building may itself provide enjoyment or learning; the actual alternative may be leisure, care or another volunteer activity. A replacement-cost valuation asks a different question: what would comparable purchased work cost? Neither automatically measures the user's willingness to pay for the finished result.

The 2026 BEA household-production working paper offers a concrete extended-account model: labor and durable-capital services form value added; materials are added for gross output and expenditure. Its 2003–2024 component series and extended use tables integrate household services with market counterparts. Allocation remains assumption-dependent; some multi-use inputs are split mechanically, and personal computers are excluded from allocated household-production capital because of substantial other uses. This is an experimental measurement framework, not an estimate of household-created software. [R11-S08, §§2–3]

The methodological lesson is that a credible extension needs a consistent production account, not just a large imputed wage bill. If the analyst adds home-produced output while retaining all inputs as unrelated final consumption, the same resources may be represented twice. If the analyst imputes a higher wage to the same task solely because a highly paid professional performs it, comparisons may reflect earnings inequality rather than task output. For the book, a sensitivity range over plausible shadow values of time is usually more informative than a single impressive total.

### 4.3 Services, depreciation and the asset's full benefit

The OECD *Measuring Capital* manual distinguishes productive stocks and capital services from wealth stocks. Its user-cost treatment includes financing/opportunity cost, depreciation and expected revaluation, rather than depreciation alone. It explains why rental charges can also contain labor and overhead and why endogenous versus exogenous returns create different assumptions. These are measurement conventions for an input into production, not a universal measure of social benefit. [R11-S07, chapters 5–8, especially pp. 63–72]

Consider an invented example, not an estimate. A reusable program eliminates twenty hours of scheduling work per month. That does not make the program's annual capital-service input equal to every dollar earned by the organization during those saved hours. The gain may depend on a knowledgeable scheduler, clean records, complementary systems and continued use. If the worker has no additional demand to serve, the main gain may be less unpleasant work rather than additional sales. If errors fall, quality may improve even when neither hours nor revenue moves. The same technical program can thus have different benefits in different organizations.

Software can also become economically obsolete without wearing out physically. A changed interface, abandoned dependency, revised rule or missing maintainer can end its useful life. A working copy's continued existence is insufficient evidence that yesterday's service flow remains available. Conversely, a mature tool can remain useful after its accounting value has declined substantially. These are reasons to observe use, failures and replacement, not to infer a service life from the word “digital.”

## 5 What open-source measurement actually establishes

### 5.1 Cost-based reconstruction measures production effort

Santiago Calderón and coauthors' July 2022 BEA working paper constructs an OSS investment measure from over 7.75 million licensed, original, nondeprecated GitHub repositories. It converts code additions through a software cost model, links contributors to countries/sectors and applies resource costs. Its U.S. 2019 estimate is $36.2 billion. This is a modeled annual development-cost estimate, including activity outside the ordinary accounts, not a newly observed GDP increment. Coverage excludes some repositories/licenses; country/sector assignment is incomplete; generated non-code material can inflate counts; code quality is not measured. The paper explicitly does not propose changing BEA's investment definition. [R11-S09, §§5–8]

The study is relevant because it makes invisible work more observable through a different data source. It does not eliminate valuation uncertainty. Its central bridge runs from recorded development artifacts to inferred resources. AI may alter that bridge: the relationship between additional lines and human effort can change, while shorter code or deletion can improve a system. For R-02 and R-11, repository traces should be linked to deployment and outcomes rather than adopted as a sufficient production measure.

### 5.2 Replacement at each user measures a different counterfactual

Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou's January 2024 HBS working paper combines inward-facing software-composition evidence and outward-facing website technology observations. A COCOMO-based reconstruction estimates about $4.15 billion to reproduce the sampled widely used OSS once, and $8.8 trillion if each using firm had to recreate its dependencies internally. The data select widely used packages and observable firms, not all software. Linux Foundation support and participating software-composition vendors are disclosed. The large figure is a global replacement scenario for existing code, not annual value added, realized savings, a market price or experimentally estimated consumer surplus. [R11-S10, §§2–4 and appendix]

Its value for the book is the contrast between creation and reuse. Its limitation is the assumed alternative. If OSS disappeared, firms could buy common proprietary substitutes, pool development, change designs, use less functionality or exit particular activities. Rebuilding every dependency independently is not the unique feasible response. The estimate therefore cannot be read as an identified loss in an equilibrium without OSS. Missing packages can make a dataset incomplete while an expensive replacement assumption can still overstate the relevant economic counterfactual. “Incomplete coverage” alone does not establish a lower bound on every meaning of value.

The two OSS studies do not disagree merely because their totals differ. They use different populations, units, horizons and valuation objects. One estimates a development flow; the other investigates reconstruction of a stock and repeated private replacement. Their coexistence supports C-024's demand for distinctions. Neither provides an estimate of the incremental contribution of generative AI or a count of new household applications that remain useful after a year.

## 6 Welfare beyond revenue and the limits of valuation

### 6.1 A welfare framework is not an accounting correction by assertion

Brynjolfsson, Collis, Diewert, Eggers and Fox's GDP-B framework uses expenditure-function reasoning and reservation/marginal valuations to incorporate new and free goods into welfare-growth measures. The accessible ABS/RBA conference text distinguishes a total-income construction from a Hicksian welfare-change construction and explicitly focuses on changes rather than simply adding all existing benefits to a GDP level. Its examples concern consumer services and smartphone features. The final journal publication is October 2025, but its full text was not accessible in this review; no conference-version number is presented as a verified final estimate. [R11-S11, §§2–5]

This is a substantive response to the absence of positive prices: use additional evidence on preferences instead of equating a zero payment with zero benefit. But it changes the evidentiary task. The researcher must identify the good, time horizon, substitutes and population, and establish how a valuation maps into the chosen welfare concept. A number denominated in dollars is not thereby commensurate with expenditure-based GDP. A household may place a high value on access to a tool without producing an equivalent amount of exchangeable income.

### 6.2 Consequential choices improve measurement without solving every problem

Brynjolfsson, Collis and Eggers' 2019 PNAS study combines incentive-compatible Facebook/laboratory choices with larger hypothetical category surveys. For Facebook, a randomly selected respondent's choice could be implemented and non-use verified. The paper reports a median monthly Facebook compensation near $48. Its annual category surveys, with 64,940 responses across 2016–2017, were not incentive-compatible and asked about losing all services in a category. These are distinct designs; the latter's large category valuations must not be described as observed payments or as results of the Facebook enforcement experiment. Internet-only participation and sensitivity of whole demand curves to extreme responses limit generalization. [R11-S12, “Methods and Results,” table 1]

For a bespoke community tool, a feasible evaluation could compare real access choices or adoption offers with observed usage and task outcomes. A survey asking what users would demand to surrender the only working system after becoming dependent on it measures a different counterfactual from whether building it was worthwhile initially. Both may be informative, but they should not be substituted silently. The first can incorporate disruption, switching and network coordination; the second must consider development and adoption alternatives.

### 6.3 Stated preferences require aggregation discipline

Coyle and Nguyen's 2023 article uses Great Britain online-panel surveys in February 2020, May 2020 and February 2021, with 10,000, 1,600 and 10,000 respondents respectively. These are non-incentivized WTA exercises; some respondents recur. Plausible changes during lockdown and preference rankings support informational usefulness, but the authors emphasize aggregation problems, skewed valuations, time constraints and the absence of a monetary budget constraint. Lockdown supplies contextual variation, not a randomized treatment identifying each product's causal welfare effect. [R11-S13, §2 and conclusion, pp. 30–31, 42–43]

The review's implication is methodological rather than dismissive. Large WTA values may show that a service matters, but summing them across overlapping goods can fail to describe a feasible choice. Losing a browser, a search service and a local application can each destroy the same activity. Adding three separate loss valuations may count its benefit repeatedly. Nor does equal access imply equal welfare: digital literacy, available time, disability access, language, suitable devices and local support can change use and gains. Distribution needs observation, not an inference from a posted price.

### 6.4 Revealed attachment is not an exhaustive welfare criterion

Allcott and coauthors' November 2019 author manuscript, published in 2020, studies four-week Facebook deactivation before the 2018 U.S. midterms. It recruited 2,743 users via Facebook advertisements; randomization covered the 61 percent willing to deactivate for less than $102. Deactivation improved a subjective-well-being index, reduced news knowledge, and lowered later usage and valuations. Random assignment strengthens causal inference within the eligible sample, but willingness-selected users and one electoral period limit generalization. Funding by Sloan and Knight is disclosed, as are relevant author industry ties. [R11-S14, §§1–3, 5–6]

This does not show that useful software is harmful, nor that monetary valuation should be abandoned. It shows why adoption, stated attachment, task performance and experienced well-being are different outcomes. A workflow could save time while increasing surveillance or vulnerability; a personal tool could be enjoyable to build even when its operational savings are small. Evaluations should make these outcomes visible rather than force them all through one enthusiastic revenue-versus-surplus story.

## 7 The mismeasurement debate is a debate about magnitudes and boundaries

Ahmad and Schreyer's 2016 OECD analysis separates conceptual-boundary questions from practical compilation problems and argues that much digital activity fits the existing framework. Its free-household-asset discussion identifies unpaid creation, ownership and valuation difficulties, while recognizing costed free assets. Its broad Linux/Wikipedia framing should not be imported as a current claim that every contribution is unpaid or uncounted: the later standard and sector-sensitive evidence above are more discriminating. This is a useful methodological defense of production accounts, not proof that every contemporary digital activity is fully measured. [R11-S17, §§3–6, 9–10]

The strongest challenge to a sweeping “the gains are merely missing” argument is empirical. Byrne, Fernald and Reinsdorf reconstruct alternative ICT price and growth-accounting measures for the pre- and post-2004 United States. Correcting mismeasurement does not erase the slowdown: pre-slowdown errors and increasing import penetration matter. Their 2016 paper separately treats many consumer digital benefits as nonmarket gains. This is a product-level accounting investigation with assumptions about prices and production shares, not a randomized test and not evidence about the 2020s generative-AI wave. [R11-S15, §§2–6 and tables]

Syverson's June 2016 manuscript tests the slowdown explanation using cross-country ICT intensity, the scale of omitted output required, producing-sector arithmetic and the GDP–GDI discrepancy. The combination challenges a dominant mismeasurement explanation for that historical slowdown. Country correlations and counterfactual calculations are not causal identification of one alternative cause. The 2017 publication's metadata were checked, but the substantive text accessed was the earlier manuscript; its numerical vintages are not mixed with the final version. [R11-S16, §§2–6]

These critiques and the welfare literature can both be right. A production-growth slowdown need not be a statistical illusion for important nonmarket benefits to exist. Nor does evidence of nonmarket benefits establish that their *acceleration* matches a shortfall in market productivity growth. R-12 must specify whether it is explaining a level of welfare, a change in welfare, a production level, an observed growth slowdown or a forecast of future growth. Switching the explanandum after encountering inconvenient evidence would make the argument impossible to test.

There is a further accounting trap. If a business uses a free input to produce more measured final output, some benefit can appear downstream rather than as revenue of a software producer. If a household substitutes its own activity for a purchased service, the classification may move in the opposite direction. If the input is imported, user benefits and domestic production can again diverge. These are different mechanisms. “GDP misses software” bundles them into a slogan and prevents the reader from seeing which evidence is needed.

## 8 Practical applications and explicit counterfactuals

The following are constructed examples, not reported empirical cases or official statistical estimates. They apply the distinctions above while avoiding invented population effects.

### An internal business tool

A small firm assigns an employee to build an inventory-checking program. The fact that it pays no external software developer does not settle the accounting question. The investigation should record whether the work creates a reusable asset, the development resources, continued use and economic control. For evaluation, compare it with the actual next-best option: a purchased tool, a manual process or no inventory check. Do not assume the alternative is an expensive custom-development contract the firm would never have purchased.

Suppose the program takes ten hours to make, saves two hours a month, requires one hour of upkeep each month, and is abandoned after six months. Ignoring discounting, it saves twelve hours and uses sixteen. An attractive demonstration is not yet a time saving. If it also prevents substantial losses or teaches a transferable skill, the project could still be beneficial; those benefits need separate evidence. This simple identity exposes why gross time saved and net gains are different.

### A household reminder tool

A person builds a reminder application for personal errands using an already-paid subscription. The incremental cash outlay may be zero. That does not establish zero resource cost, and an assumed hypothetical commercial replacement price does not establish what the household would have spent. The empirical record should distinguish building time, enjoyment, avoided errors, continued use and resources shared with other activities. Calling it “productive capital” in ordinary language can be useful, but formal national-accounts asset status needs the relevant production and use tests.

### A community tool maintained by volunteers

A volunteer publishes code for a neighborhood equipment-lending group. One version needs extensive installation, training and recurring moderation; another works with existing records and minimal support. Identical license prices hide very different adoption costs. The appropriate denominator is successful, maintained use by the intended community, not repositories published or theoretical potential users. A free copy may also shift support work to recipients. The social account should identify who supplies that work and whether the apparent saving simply moves it to someone with less bargaining power or less spare time.

### Public and commercial complements

A publicly funded project releases useful code; a business sells hosting and support around it. The original, the free copies and the paid complementary service are different objects. A commercial price can partly reward convenience, reliability or risk-bearing rather than exclusion from the underlying code. Conversely, a new zero-price entrant may replace a paid product while demanding more user time. Neither nominal revenue change can determine the welfare comparison on its own.

## 10 Investigated gaps and the next useful measurements

**AI-created household and community software.** Searches centered on official software investment methods, OSS valuation, household production and digital welfare did not recover a representative longitudinal estimate joining AI-assisted creation, full costs, actual deployment, survival and beneficiary outcomes. This is a specified evidence gap, not proof that no such activity exists or that no relevant study can be found. The OSS studies cannot fill it by changing the label on their populations.

**Classification in borderline cases.** The standards establish major distinctions but not a turnkey answer for every mixed arrangement: an unpaid contributor who anticipates later commercial benefits, shared volunteer/employee authorship, ownership split across countries, or a short-lived prototype that becomes a maintained service. The book should describe the concrete activity before claiming an accounting result. Legal ownership questions belong in R-15; economic ownership for statistics is not a complete legal analysis.

**Output quality and asset survival.** The main measurement opportunity is a panel linking development inputs to deployment, independent quality checks, continuing users, dependency changes, incidents and retirement. A useful asset count needs a survival concept. Small tools may have short lives because needs change, or long lives with little maintenance. No uniform rate is established here.

**Nondeveloper estimator coverage.** A practical test would compare activity-based survey data with the occupations/time factors used in official own-account estimates. Include failed projects and software made outside designated technical jobs. Distinguish new asset formation from ordinary operation and incremental maintenance. A discrepancy must be reconciled against existing R&D and software entries before it is labelled omitted investment.

**Welfare and opportunity costs.** Collect time use alongside adoption and valuation, including whose time is saved and whose time is added. Use consequential choices where feasible and ethical; report hypothetical responses separately. Compare access to the bespoke tool with actual substitutes, rather than removal of an entire digital category. Repeated measurement before and after use would help detect adaptation and switching costs.

**Counterfactual production without a market.** Some projects would otherwise not happen. For those, avoided commercial expenditure is the wrong benefit measure. Measure the newly feasible outcome. For projects replacing existing purchases, distinguish resource saving from a transfer between suppliers, workers and buyers. For projects replacing leisure, include the value of that leisure rather than assuming displaced time had no value.

**Source-version gaps.** The 2025 GDP-B final publication and 2017 Syverson final article were identified, but accessible substantive versions differ, as documented below. No current numerical claim depends on pretending that the final texts were inspected. The exact national-account implementation of 2025 SNA changes after the documented BEA methodological vintages requires release-specific verification before later quantitative use.

These gaps do not prevent a bounded conclusion on C-023–C-025. They prevent stronger conclusions about aggregate size, causal AI effects, permanent capital accumulation and distribution.

## 11 Constraints for R-12 and author-only choices

R-12 should carry forward the following requirements without repeating the source capsules:

- State the outcome: market revenue, domestic value added, real consumption, net income, wealth, time use or welfare. Declare nominal versus real and annual flow versus asset stock.
- Trace both production and use. A gain can arise at a user rather than a software seller; creation and consumption may occur in different countries.
- Keep the counterfactual feasible. A world in which each firm reconstructs every shared dependency is not the only world without current OSS.
- Do not sum avoided expenditure, producer gains and consumer valuations mechanically. Transfers, overlapping benefits and intermediate inputs must be reconciled.
- Separate a boundary change from a measurement improvement inside a fixed boundary. Revising a standard is not an observed technological gain in that year.
- Reconcile gross and net effects, including replacement and obsolescence. A burst of construction can coexist with little durable accumulation.
- Model reallocation. Saved resources can support more output, less work, more leisure, unsuccessful experimentation or other spending; the sign is not predetermined.
- Establish distribution independently. A zero license fee is neither universal effective access nor evidence about who controls complements and captures income.
- Treat the historical mismeasurement debate as a constraint on reasoning, not a timeless numerical bound on AI's possible effects.

For R-02, the minimum deliverable is evidence on useful completed and maintained work, not just generation speed. For R-05, benefit creation does not establish a durable rent. For R-15, a copyable artifact, statistical asset and legally transferable right need not coincide.

The author must decide whether the book uses “capital” broadly for any durable capability or narrowly for recognized produced assets, and whether a welfare argument is about preference satisfaction, experienced well-being, autonomy or a plural set of outcomes. Research can expose the consequences of these choices but does not silently make them. The empirical claim requiring no such choice is narrower: useful software can exist without a separately priced software sale, and assessing its significance requires more than inspecting that sale price.

## 13 Method limits and publication checks

This is a focused analytical review, not a preregistered systematic review. Searches deliberately paired measurement-expansion proposals with official accounting treatments and mismeasurement counterarguments. They covered production boundaries, own-account software, household/volunteer activity, capital services, OSS replacement models, digital WTA, experienced welfare and productivity measurement. No study count or search-result ranking is claimed to measure the prevalence of a view.

The major conclusions rest on inspected substantive text. Unavailable versions are documented rather than reconstructed from abstracts. Local extraction occasionally produced two-column reading-order artifacts in the SNA 2008 PDF; paragraph numbering and adjoining text were used to restore the relevant sequence. The review does not reproduce source tables, extensive quotations or proprietary data. Source-derived capsules are kept compact and shared by reference; the claim ledger and downstream constraints add judgments rather than duplicate empirical capsules. Other programs should cite the relevant R-11 section when using these same findings.

All practical examples are explicitly invented. They are accounting/decision illustrations, not measured AI productivity effects. Their arithmetic is elementary and checkable. No historical statement from R-18, empirical causal claim from another program or author's personal experience is offered as privately validated evidence.

**Completion boundary:** the accounting distinctions, major measurement approaches, opposing evidence, claim-level judgments and research gaps required by R-11 have been supplied. Final acceptance still requires independent substantive checking, cross-program reconciliation and the coordinator's verified publication. No canonical thesis, chapter or normative author choice is established by this review.
