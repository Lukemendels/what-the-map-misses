# R-04 — Redesign mechanism and electrification

**Program:** R-04, redesign mechanism and electrification.  
**Research and access date:** 4 October 2026.  
**Admitted claims:** C-005, C-006, C-008, C-043 and C-053.  
**Authority:** [Loop 2](../../../loops/02-eighteen-literature-reviews.md).  
**Status:** Analytical literature review accepted after independent quality review; coordinated publication checkpoint. Research judgments do not revise the protected corpus, adopt a thesis, or establish remote durability.

## 1. Finding and exact scope

Organizational redesign is a supported, conditional mechanism for making a technological capability useful. The evidence does not establish a general premium to making the largest organizational change, nor that a model's weaknesses are usually symptoms of bad layout. Task acceleration, elimination of a handoff, better allocation of judgment, and creation of a previously unavailable service are different interventions. Each can be valuable. Their relative value depends on the output sought, existing constraints, errors, implementation costs and feasible alternatives.

Electrification helps explain why the productive unit can be larger than the new device. R-18 has already shown why its history cannot supply an invariant waiting period or a single explanation of productivity growth. This review takes that completed audit as its historical foundation and extends the question into organizational economics, detailed production studies and contemporary AI workflows. It does not repeat the historical source capsules or treat the analogy as an estimate of AI's effects.

The strongest modern evidence considered here establishes particular improvements in bounded writing, computational problem solving, screening procedures and retail transactions. These outcomes are not interchangeable. Most designs identify the effect of an implementation package relative to its actual control, rather than separately identifying the contributions of the model, deterministic code, human review and organizational redesign. Public independent corroboration of the specific AutoReviewer episode was not located. Its appropriate status remains an admitted first-person account, not a disproved account and not a general treatment-effect estimate.

### Authored claims versus auxiliary tests

The [historical claim ledger](../../../evidence/claims/CLAIM-LEDGER.md) makes C-005 a possibility claim: adding capability **can** preserve bottlenecks, and redesign **can** remove them. Its scope note expressly permits useful acceleration and says redesign is not always warranted. This review preserves those quantifiers. The relevant auxiliary tests compare the marginal gains from an actual model upgrade, a specified workflow change, and their combination. They do not replace that possibility claim with an invented universal.

C-006 combines cheaper cognition, cheaper software rearrangement and the coupled production system as the relevant object of redesign. R-04 can assess the last link and its organizational mechanism; R-01 and R-02 must establish the changing costs across useful tasks and full software lifecycles. C-008 concerns one named project and approximately three weeks within its first architecture. C-043 concerns historical chronology and attribution. C-053 is a composite thesis that also depends on human capability and institutional reliability, outside this program's sole competence.

The specific corpus examined is S-03's historical 'The stack' passage; S-04, especially “Software was always the factory floor,” “I spent weeks building the wrong factory,” “Better AI does not automatically mean better work,” and the human-authority discussion; and S-10 §§2–4, 8–9 and 18. S-04's “Most organizations” and “often” formulations carry prevalence claims stronger than the ledger's **can**. Neither a selected case collection nor the studies below identifies the proportion of all organizations trapped in an inherited workflow. That gap should remain visible rather than being silently solved by changing “most” into “some.”

## 2. What the electrification audit contributes

The detailed chronology, engineering evidence and competing productivity findings belong in [R-18's electrification analysis](../R-18/biography-electricity.md#5-electrification-the-mechanism-survives-the-simple-delay-story-does-not). The cross-industry and investment comparison belongs in [R-18's buildout analysis](../R-18/buildout.md#electrification-as-complementary-change-rather-than-a-universal-timetable). Those modules distinguish Devine's engineering reconstruction, David's interpretation, the David–Wright synthesis, the cross-country challenge from Ristuccia–Solomou, and the local exposure design of Fiszbein and colleagues. They also distinguish electricity-use benefits from patent-based measures of generality. They are complementary research approaches, not independent repetitions of one layout experiment.

Three historical clocks must remain separate in the book: the first technically interesting invention, a commercially feasible application, and adoption or reorganization at a particular establishment. A fourth clock is the visibility of effects in an aggregate statistic. Confusing these clocks can turn diffusion into apparent managerial delay. Conversely, evidence of an early successful application does not mean the application was feasible everywhere at that date. This is the conceptual repair inherited from R-18, rather than a new historical estimate.

For R-04, the important question is what is being held fixed in each comparison. A replacement power source in an existing plant differs from a new plant with different machinery, buildings, supply arrangements, workers and product demand. A comparison between these bundles may reveal a better productive arrangement without revealing how much improvement came from floor layout alone. The same identification problem appears when a new model is installed together with retrieval, interface changes, trained users, revised approval rules and better evaluation. Attributing the entire difference to “redesign” is defensible only if redesign denotes the whole intervention package; it cannot simultaneously mean a separately measured causal component.

The electricity analogy also contains a selection problem. A successful new plant demonstrates what could work under its conditions. It does not reveal the return to rebuilding every installed plant, including those near retirement or facing uncertain demand. The AI counterpart is a successful greenfield application compared with the most awkward legacy workflow. That comparison can identify an opportunity but overstate the return to migration. A fair alternative includes improving the existing process, buying an established product, changing staffing, or doing nothing until replacement is economically warranted.

A useful inheritance is therefore a set of questions, not a historical script:

- Which inherited constraint does the new input relax?
- Is that constraint currently binding on useful output?
- What complementary investment is required to exploit the relaxation?
- Is the relevant delay technical, organizational, financial, institutional or statistical?
- What surviving observations would distinguish these explanations?

The buildout audit adds a further discipline: the size of an investment commitment cannot answer these questions. Construction expenditure, functioning capacity, adoption, organizational learning, useful services and final welfare must not be used as substitutes for one another. A large AI buildout is compatible with large eventual benefits, expensive mistakes, uneven adoption, or a mixture. That is why R-04 studies completed processes rather than interpreting expenditure as proof that the redesign thesis has already prevailed.

## 3. Complementarity: mechanism, evidence and measurement

### 3.1 A system can matter without every component being indispensable

The economics of complementarities asks whether doing more of one activity raises the return to another. It does not say that every fashionable practice belongs in the same package, that all old arrangements are incoherent, or that coordination is free. R-03 owns the formal treatment of Milgrom and Roberts, including the important 1995 correction [S11]. R-04 uses the framework to formulate a contingent organizational question, not to derive a universal result about prices or the superiority of a named organization form.

For empirical work, three propositions require different evidence. **Co-adoption** means practices occur together. **Performance association** means a bundle predicts an outcome after specified controls. **Causal complementarity** means the incremental effect of one intervention changes because another intervention is present. A prosperous firm could purchase more technology and training because it anticipates demand growth. A capable manager could introduce several useful changes together. A survey interaction can be informative while failing to isolate any single marginal contribution.

**S01 evidence capsule.** Bresnahan, Brynjolfsson and Hitt analyze 1987–1994 firm data; the common panel has 1,331 firm-years and organization is surveyed in 1995–1996. Demand and productivity regressions support an IT/organization/skills cluster, but workplace measures are temporally coarse, hardware measures exclude software, and interactions are imprecise. Sales-based productivity includes differentiation; unobserved complements restrict component-level causality. This supports evaluating bundles rather than estimating a randomized redesign premium [S01, §§III–V, pp. 347–355, 366–369; this source is also R18-F18, now cross-linked here].

**S02 evidence capsule.** Bartel, Ichniowski and Shaw's published study examines 212 surviving US valve plants, with 2002–2003 interviews recalling 1997–2002 changes. Product-specific, first-difference models link IT-enhanced equipment to shorter setup, run and inspection times in the relevant production stages. Customization, skills and workplace practices also change. Crucially, the product-specific models do not find significant IT-by-training interactions; teams likewise do not explain those time reductions, although plant-level practices co-vary. This narrows, rather than negates, complementarity. Within-industry and within-plant-stage comparisons reduce heterogeneity, but time-varying shocks, retrospective measures, nonresponse and survivor selection remain. Setup savings can support product variety rather than merely more identical units. The result is not randomized adoption or an estimate of total plant welfare [S02, §§IV–VII; Table III; pp. 1745–1746, note 19].

These studies are useful together because their units differ. The broad firm-level approach makes organizational interdependence visible but measures its components coarsely. The narrowly defined production study better locates where time is saved, while potentially missing benefits outside the measured product run. A null interaction at the latter level cannot establish that organization never matters; a positive bundle association at the former cannot establish that every local efficiency improvement requires it. The same reconciliation will be needed when an AI tool improves a narrow task but a firm has yet to change measured output.

There is a further distinction between **changing a process** and **changing the product**. Faster production of an unchanged artifact is one outcome. A larger range of customized outputs, a better service, or a newly feasible activity is another. Holding the old product definition fixed can miss genuine innovation. Abandoning any stable product definition, however, makes every redesign appear successful by changing the scoring rule. The appropriate response is to report both a like-for-like comparison and the separately valued new output, with its costs and acceptance criteria.

### 3.2 Implementation costs are real even when accounting misses them

**S03 evidence capsule.** Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson formalize a productivity J-curve in their January 2020 working-paper revision, subsequently published in 2021. Resources used to create unmeasured intangible investment can depress measured productivity initially; omitted capital services can later exaggerate measured growth. They combine growth accounting with market-valuation estimates of complementary intangibles. The procedure depends on efficient-pricing, aggregation and correlated-capital assumptions, and does not identify the effect of a particular workflow change. Their own analysis says the mere presence of complementary intangibles does not guarantee a J-curve. This is a conditional measurement mechanism, not evidence that disappointing projects must eventually succeed [S03, §§III–V and VIII; footnote 22; Appendix].

A book about movable machinery should therefore keep two accounts. The **resource account** includes effort actually spent discovering, implementing and repairing the new arrangement. The **measurement account** asks which of those efforts create a durable asset and which appear as current expense or disappear from a chosen statistic. Reclassifying effort as investment does not reimburse it. Nor does an accounting explanation for weak measured output show that the organizational change was wise.

For a prospective AI redesign, the relevant comparison includes discovery and failed prototypes; specification; data cleaning and permissions; integration; migration and parallel operation; staff learning; evaluation and review; incident response; maintenance; model or supplier changes; and retirement of the system. Some costs are fixed, some scale with volume, and some arise only in exceptional cases. A large saving per routine item can coexist with an uneconomic project at low volume. A slower routine process may be preferable if it prevents rare, severe or hard-to-reverse failures.

These are analytical cost categories, not measured universal cost shares. Their purpose is to prevent the disappearance of costs between the model benchmark and the business case. R-11 owns the accounting treatment of capital and welfare; R-05 owns evidence about management interventions and persistence. R-04's contribution is to require the redesign comparison to include transition and operating costs together, with a stated horizon. Repeated redesign can create useful adaptations, but it can also create churn, incompatibility and abandoned learning. A capacity to change is not evidence that the optimal cadence is continuous change.

## 4. Bottlenecks, completion and the false choice between acceleration and redesign

### 4.1 Define the intervention at the right level

The contrast between “make the task faster” and “move the machinery” is illuminating only if its scale is explicit. Four levels are distinguishable:

1. **Within an artifact:** change the order of planning, drafting, checking and editing.
2. **Within a workflow:** eliminate re-entry, change routing, make state authoritative, or move review earlier.
3. **Across an organization:** change ownership of work, staffing, incentives, interfaces or control rights.
4. **Across products or markets:** offer a service that was previously unavailable or uneconomic.

These levels overlap. A tool introduced as assistance can change the user's internal task sequence without changing formal job boundaries. A substantial software replacement can leave the actual approval bottleneck untouched. “Redesign” should therefore name an observed change in allocation or dependency, not confer higher status on a project because it is larger.

The central mechanism is conditional: removing work that no longer serves the objective, or assigning a component to a better-suited resource, can reduce total effort or improve accepted output. But acceleration can also relax the binding constraint. If a task already constitutes a useful final service and its inputs are adequately specified, faster completion may be exactly the desired improvement. The literature should test these alternatives symmetrically.

### 4.2 Two illustrative calculations, not empirical findings

Consider a serial job requiring 60 minutes of drafting and 40 minutes of checking and delivery. If drafting alone becomes twice as fast, total time falls from 100 to 70 minutes, a 30% reduction; jobs per labor-hour could rise by approximately 43% if demand, quality and the remaining work are unchanged. This is the review's arithmetic illustration, not an estimate from an AI experiment. It shows both that partial acceleration can matter and that its headline speedup does not transfer one-for-one to the whole job.

Now consider a pipeline with capacity for 100 drafts per hour and only 10 completed reviews per hour, with every draft requiring review. Doubling draft capacity leaves the steady-state reviewed-output ceiling unchanged under these stipulated conditions. It may simply increase the backlog if arrivals rise. But reducing the review time, improving the draft's first-pass acceptance rate, or routing only a valid subset for review can change the ceiling. Whether any such change is permissible depends on quality and authority requirements. The numbers are constructed; they are not evidence that human review is generally the binding constraint.

These examples also expose missing outcomes. Faster drafting may reduce individual workload or deadline risk even before final throughput rises. Conversely, more final outputs may be unwanted, duplicative or harmful. An organization may rationally use time savings to improve quality, recover slack, train staff or absorb variable demand. Failure to observe a headcount reduction is not failure to observe any benefit. Equally, self-reported time saved is not enough to establish higher organizational productivity.

### 4.3 Completed, quality-adjusted work is a measurement discipline

The term should not become a new universal score. For each application, specify the unit, the acceptance boundary, the relevant defect classes, and the observation window. A document could be syntactically valid but factually wrong; accepted today but costly to correct next month; useful to one reviewer but unusable by the receiving system. A screening examination, a correctly answered arithmetic question and a purchase are not commensurable units merely because each appears at the end of a process.

An evaluation should report a vector of outcomes:

- completed and accepted units, including abandoned cases in the denominator;
- quality or error measures tied to the intended use, with serious defects reported separately;
- total human effort, including supervision, specialist rescue and downstream rework;
- elapsed time and its distribution, not only average active task time;
- compute, purchased services and amortized implementation costs;
- subsequent corrections, reversals, complaints or other relevant consequences.

Combining these into a weighted index can be useful, but the weights encode judgments about value and harm. They must be disclosed and should not let many trivial successes offset an unacceptable serious failure. Measuring human authority, retained skill or legal compliance may require additional outcomes even when those features lower short-run speed. Those choices cannot be settled by a productivity statistic alone.

## 5. Contemporary evidence: what is actually completed?

### 5.1 Bounded writing is a real result with a narrow boundary

**S04 evidence capsule.** Noy and Zhang's author-hosted manuscript, linked from the author's current research page, matches the published study's 453-person sample. In a preregistered 2023 online experiment, experienced college-educated professionals completed short occupational writing tasks with randomized ChatGPT access. Average time fell 40% and evaluated quality rose 18%. Independent occupational peers graded the work. The tasks deliberately avoided precise factual accuracy and context-specific knowledge; control-group AI use and differential attrition require care. Little editing benefit was observed. These are completed experimental artifacts, not maintained organizational outputs or subsequent business outcomes. The study supports valuable bounded assistance without establishing a need for organization-wide reconstruction [S04, author manuscript pp. 3–7, Figures 1–2; publication metadata separately verified].

This is material counterevidence to dismissing task acceleration as merely cosmetic. It is not counterevidence to C-005's **can** proposition. The distinction is causal and practical: an intervention can improve the outcome measured in its experiment while leaving a different organization's bottleneck unresolved. Calling the first outcome “shallow” should not make it disappear; calling it “productivity” should not expand it into unmeasured factual accuracy, organizational throughput or retained expertise.

### 5.2 Deterministic execution can repair a specific failure class

**S05 evidence capsule.** Gao and colleagues' PAL uses a language model to translate a problem into a program and a Python interpreter to execute it. In the ICML 2023 paper, Table 1 reports GSM8K solve rates of 72.0% for PAL versus 65.6% for chain-of-thought using Codex; these are benchmark accuracies, not employee productivity. Same-model comparisons and interpreter ablations make the allocation mechanism more informative than a comparison of model sizes alone. Success still depends on a correct program and appropriate representation. The study does not evaluate human acceptance, secure integration or lifecycle cost. Model versions, few-shot prompts and selected arithmetic/symbolic tasks bound transport. Table 1 and nearby prose differ on GSM-HARD values; this review does not use those disputed numbers [S05, §§3–6, Table 1, Appendix H].

The relevant inference is local: move an operation whose correctness can be specified to an execution mechanism suited to that specification, while testing the translation into it. This does not create a clean permanent boundary between “creative model work” and “mechanical code.” Generated code is itself an uncertain artifact; deterministic execution can reliably execute a mistaken instruction. The same caution applies to a calculator, a database query or a document comparator. It is the explicit contract between components, not determinism as a label, that makes errors diagnosable.

PAL is therefore a useful mechanistic analogue for AutoReviewer, but not corroboration of that project. It tests a different problem class and does not establish the incremental effect of the human decision stage. Conversely, failure to prove a general productivity effect does not erase a measured improvement in a defined computational task. The evidentiary level is a technical experiment, between an illustrative architecture and a field study of completed organizational work.

### 5.3 A redesigned clinical pathway is a package, not an isolated model score

**S06 evidence capsule.** Hernström and colleagues' 2025 MASAI report covers 105,934 randomized women at four Swedish screening sites, with 19 excluded. Transpara 1.7.0 routed examinations to single or double reading and supplied detection support; radiologists retained recall decisions. Compared with standard double reading, the intervention reduced the number of screen readings by 44.2% and raised detected cancers from 5.0 to 6.4 per thousand, without a statistically significant increase in false positives. This is actual workflow randomization, not retrospective model accuracy. However, reading counts are not total staff hours, costs or mortality; one mammography vendor and one AI vendor within this screening context bound generalization. These were secondary outcomes, not the trial's final primary endpoint [S06, Methods, Table 2, Discussion].

**S07 endpoint update.** The 2026 primary report is available here only through its complete public abstract and institutional record. It reports interval-cancer-rate noninferiority using a prespecified 20% margin: the proportion ratio is 0.88, with 95% confidence interval 0.65–1.18. The interval does not establish superiority on this endpoint. It also reports improved sensitivity and equal specificity. Thus the older report's “primary endpoint awaited” statement is no longer current, but the update is not a mortality or full cost-effectiveness result [S07, abstract Methods/Findings].

For the book, the productive feature of this example is the correspondence between an explicit routing rule and an actual service process. It demonstrates why testing an integrated procedure is more informative than citing a stand-alone model's accuracy. It does not isolate whether routing, detection assistance, reader behavior or their combination produced each effect. A factorial comparison would be needed to apportion those components. Nor is a diagnostic model a generative writing model: differences in input standardization, ground truth, regulated practice and error consequences restrict the analogy.

A further lesson concerns the endpoint. Making fewer observations of a patient cannot itself define success; the resulting decisions and later missed disease matter. In office work, the corresponding discipline is to follow the downstream consequence rather than count fewer reviewers or faster drafts as self-validating improvements. This is an evaluation analogy, not a clinical recommendation or a claim that the two kinds of consequence have equal stakes.

### 5.4 Retail experiments move closer to business outcomes

**S08 evidence capsule.** Fang and colleagues' June 2026 v6 studies seven selected workflows in one cross-border retailer during 2023–2024; granular data cover five, with company summaries for two. Randomized consumer/product experiments produce heterogeneous sales effects, including nonsignificant advertising estimates. The main chatbot control offers no live service, so its gain is not superiority over human service. Appendix C1's AI-first/human-escalation versus human-only comparison estimates 11.53% higher sales, but only at p<.1 (30,467 observations). Non-significance in a separate AI-only/human-only comparison is not equivalence. Available returns and ratings do not deteriorate, but observation and selection limits remain. Development/deployment costs are unquantified. The current title is *Sales Productivity*, replacing *Firm Productivity*; older TFP language should not be imported. Authors include company consultants and an employee [S08, §§2–3 and 5; Tables 2, 4, C1 and C9].

This evidence strengthens the move beyond self-reported minutes and artifact ratings, while simultaneously sharpening the counterfactual problem. Supplying a missing service is an extensive-margin intervention; replacing existing support is a substitution experiment; escalating unresolved cases is a routing intervention. Pooling these under “AI productivity” conceals the different mechanisms. The supplementary hybrid comparison is suggestive, not a sufficiently precise or fully costed demonstration that a general human–AI hierarchy dominates.

A transaction is also not the end of every relevant causal chain. More sales can result from better matching, persuasion, timing shifts or diversion from another seller. They are business outcomes, but not automatically net social output or consumer welfare. The source's version change is consequently substantive, not cosmetic: a careful review must retain the measured sales object rather than revive an older interpretation through an outdated abstract.

### 5.5 Reconciliation with the other programs

The comparison should include the existing [R-09 customer-support capsule](../R-09/README.md#ai-mediated-transfer-and-its-limits), [R-10's P&G and BCG experiments](../R-10/README.md#6-direct-ai-evidence-on-productive-breadth-and-its-limits), and [R-05's Danish evidence](../R-05/README.md#the-danish-test-work-changes-before-pay). Their detailed results and source-version audits are not repeated here. They address different completion boundaries and identification problems; adding their percentage headlines would not produce an aggregate redesign estimate. R-02 supplies the coding and maintenance comparison and should control the current METR/OpenAI evidence used by S-04.

The human–AI review by Vaccaro and colleagues explicitly distinguishes improvement over humans from improvement over the better stand-alone component [S10]. R-17 owns its fuller treatment. For R-04 the implication is a comparator requirement: assistance, synergy and low-cost adequacy are distinct achievements. An arrangement can be preferable under an authority constraint even if it does not maximize a narrow accuracy score. That preference should be defended as such rather than mislabeled as empirical synergy.

Across these literatures, the disagreement is therefore not a simple contest between “models matter” and “organizations matter.” Both interventions and outcomes differ. A task within a model's competence can benefit immediately. A poorly specified or unmeasured downstream task can remain costly. A reallocated workflow can improve one outcome while incurring new costs elsewhere. The studies give reasons to inspect the coupled system, while leaving the relative payoff to any proposed redesign to be established in its setting.

## 6. AutoReviewer: testimony, technical plausibility and missing identification

### 6.1 What is and is not corroborated

C-008 and S-04 say the first design required a model both to revise prose and to encode every change in specialized notation; after roughly three weeks, the revised design separated clean revised text, deterministic comparison and human acceptance. The author also says much of the previous machinery became unnecessary. These are the account's commitments. There is no admitted numerical failure rate, sample size, saved-hour estimate or controlled comparison to recover from that wording.

The public search pass used exact combinations of “AutoReviewer,” “Luke Mendelsohn,” “Lukemendels,” “tracked changes,” and a broader author/document-review query. No independently inspectable public release history, before-and-after evaluation or third-party replication of this specific episode was located. A public author post about process translation surfaced, but did not identify the episode or validate its timing or results; it is excluded as corroboration. Search-engine coverage is incomplete, and name collisions are possible. The result is a bounded negative search, not a claim that no public evidence exists anywhere.

No private origin, unrelated workspace, internal tool deployment or nonpublic document was inspected. The testimony remains usable as an attributed account of the author's experience. Its illustrative force comes from the diagnostic question it raises; its epistemic status does not improve simply because the architecture sounds sensible.

### 6.2 Why the proposed separation is plausible without being a complete proof

**S09 technical documentation.** Python's `difflib` documents deterministic sequence comparison, difference representations and reconstruction of either input from a delta. It also explains that its differences need not be minimal and that matching heuristics can produce surprising alignments. These are documented software behaviors, not a study of AutoReviewer, Word-document fidelity or reviewers' accuracy [S09, “Diff generation,” `Differ`, `SequenceMatcher`, `restore`].

The architecture separates two contracts: propose a new text, and represent the difference between two authoritative texts. That separation makes a useful invariant possible: accepting every proposed textual change should recover the revised text, and rejecting every change should recover the original. This is an analytical test specification, not a test performed on the author's system. It can expose malformed edit notation without deciding whether the proposed prose is true, appropriate or authorized.

Other contracts remain. Text extraction and reconstruction must preserve the intended document objects; changes need to remain understandable; the comparator's chosen alignment must not hide a consequential alteration; and reviewers must have enough context and time to decide. A text-only reconstruction can succeed while tables, footnotes, comments, formatting, cross-references or tracked moves are mishandled. These are prospective defect classes for a document system, not allegations about defects in AutoReviewer. Similarly, accepting or rejecting some changes can produce a third text whose coherence requires separate checking.

The arrangement can also shift rather than eliminate work. A model allowed to rewrite freely might generate a larger comparison and a heavier review burden. A more restrictive editing interface might be slower to produce but easier to audit. The right question is not which design emits cleaner machine-readable text in isolation; it is which design delivers an acceptable reviewed document for lower total effort and acceptable risk under the intended workload.

Several rivals could explain an observed before-and-after improvement: accumulated debugging, improved prompts, a changed model, easier documents, a reduced feature scope, increased user familiarity or better tests. A historical reconstruction would need dates and versioned artifacts to distinguish them. A prospective comparison could hold these factors fixed, but would test the new experiment rather than prove the recollected three-week sequence. Public synthetic documents could support such a test without disclosing private working material.

## 7. An auxiliary evaluation capable of changing the judgment

The following is a proposed design, **not** an authored universal claim, an experiment conducted for this review, or a requirement that every small tool run a clinical-style trial.

For a representative document-review workload, compare:

- a competent no-model baseline using established editing and comparison tools;
- model-generated revisions plus model-generated edit notation;
- model-generated clean revisions plus deterministic comparison;
- the best feasible hybrid with routing, exception handling and human acceptance.

Use identical source documents and explicit quality requirements. Randomize or counterbalance order so that familiarity does not advantage the later condition. Match model versions, available context, tool permissions and effort budgets. Evaluate both an initially configured system and a mature configuration; separate setup costs from operating costs instead of excluding either. Record all attempted cases, including recoverable failures and abandoned work. Analysts should not select only outputs that reached the reviewer.

The primary outcomes should be final accepted documents, substantive errors, preservation failures, human editing and review time, elapsed completion time, and rework after acceptance. A blinded assessment of writing quality is useful, but should be supplemented by source-grounded factual checks where accuracy matters. Round-trip reconstruction checks test the comparator contract; they cannot replace semantic review. Separate serious defects from stylistic disagreements and measure whether reviewers actually notice the consequential changes.

To identify the layout mechanism, compare conditions with the **same model**. To assess whether better models substitute for or complement redesign, repeat the comparison with specified model versions. To assess the human contribution, compare review arrangements where legally and ethically feasible, keeping the acceptance standard fixed. A full factorial experiment may be too costly; an explicitly incomplete design is preferable to claiming it identifies interactions that it never varied.

Adoption over time needs another comparison. Log migration effort, exception rates, changes in the case mix, maintenance and reversals. Stop treating every early failure as evidence against a system that has not finished setup, but also specify what would count as failure after setup. Otherwise “the gains come after redesign” becomes unfalsifiable: good outcomes confirm the thesis and bad outcomes merely demand another redesign.

These principles generalize as measurement questions, not as a single experimental protocol for every domain. The crucial missing evidence is comparative, fully costed completed-work performance under stable acceptance rules. No paper in this review supplies that complete comparison for AutoReviewer or for the book's entire coupled-production-system thesis.

## 9. Investigated gaps, implications and author-only choices

### What remains unresolved

The missing evidence is not simply another list of successful implementations. It is evidence on **comparative returns to redesign**, relative to feasible modular improvements, after implementation and maintenance costs. We also lack a representative estimate of how often handoffs, model limitations, poor data, user knowledge, demand or authority constraints are the binding obstacle. The present studies cannot rank these obstacles for organizations generally.

The exact AutoReviewer chronology, its old/new failure distributions and its full document outcomes remain publicly uncorroborated. The technical tests described above establish what could be measured, not what was observed. The mammography example has a later primary-endpoint publication, but full-text access to that report was not recovered here; its abstract does not close cost or mortality questions. The retail evidence is version-sensitive and business-outcome-specific. A proposed mechanism can remain useful while these gaps remain open.

### Downstream implications

- **R-01:** price per model operation is not the cost of a delivered outcome; changes in verification and workflow volume belong in the denominator.
- **R-02:** lower prototyping or modification costs do not establish lower migration, security or maintenance costs. Keep the software lifecycle and nondeveloper evidence distinct from this organizational inference.
- **R-03:** retain the distinction between a workflow description, a feasible technique and a formal production function. Treat complementarities and relative allocation precisely, including the corrected manufacturing theorem.
- **R-05/R-12/R-15:** a productive intervention is not proof of persistent advantage, compensation, aggregate welfare or ownership-based capture. A change in control rights is also not merely another software edit.
- **R-07/R-13:** test whether the human can exercise authority and whether component failures are detectable and recoverable. A syntactically valid trace or a passing deterministic test does not certify the whole decision.
- **R-08/R-09:** current completion and future capability stocks must both be measured when redesign removes practice or changes supervision. This review does not identify an optimal apprenticeship arrangement.
- **R-14/R-16/R-17:** improved workflow performance alone does not establish durable learning, actor development or pair-specific productivity. Identify the retained state and update mechanism; unchanged base weights do not rule out system learning through artifacts or a learned controller.

Luke's decisions concern how much explanatory work the factory metaphor should do, whether to retain an attributed AutoReviewer account, and how to balance speed, human capability and legitimate authority in the argument. Those decisions cannot establish historical dates, representative prevalence or causal effects by preference. The evidence does not require abandoning the coupled-system thesis; it requires presenting it as a conditional account with observable mechanisms, rather than letting a vivid metaphor certify its scale or inevitability.

### Review limitations and publication boundary

This is a purposive analytical review, not a preregistered exhaustive search or a meta-analysis of redesign effects. Selection favored sources that distinguish mechanisms, units, credible comparisons and consequential outcomes, including findings that weaken a simple redesign narrative. Search snippets served as discovery leads; access-limited material is labeled where used. No empirical effect size was inferred from a simulation, a software architecture diagram or an unpublished test plan. No external model benchmark was rerun.

Important nonclosures remain explicit: component-level causality within bundled interventions; full implementation costs; representative prevalence; comparative human contribution; long-run maintenance and distribution; and independent public corroboration of AutoReviewer. These limits are part of the result. They do not prevent the book from using the evidence responsibly, and they should not be concealed by calling the metaphor a law.
