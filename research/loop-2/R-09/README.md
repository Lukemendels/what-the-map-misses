# R-09 — Labor, senior knowledge stocks, and incentives to train

**Research date:** 4 October 2026.  
**Scope:** C-017 and the labor/training implications of C-044.  
**Status:** substantive literature review accepted after independent quality review; not an author-adopted thesis.  
**Reading convention:** R09-S01–S17 identify sources in the recoverable access appendix. Evidence capsules carry the source-specific results; other programs should link them rather than reproduce their figures. Relative links assume this review is published as `research/loop-2/R-09/README.md`.

## 1. Result and admitted questions

The defensible seed-corn argument is a **conditional investment and organizational-design problem**. An employer can improve a current cost measure while damaging its ability to detect mistakes, maintain relationships, develop successors, or adapt production. These are economically intelligible losses, and evidence from mentoring, departures, training, and organizational persistence makes them more than a metaphor. But their existence does not establish that every senior employee is indispensable, that expertise can only be acquired in the original firm, that AI necessarily impedes learning, or that the dismissed worker will later command a premium.

The most important revision is to separate three questions that the original rhetoric sometimes combines:

1. **Production:** whose present contribution changes useful output, errors, or risk?
2. **Reproduction:** which activities and relationships create future independent capability?
3. **Capture:** who pays for those activities and who receives their returns?

A valuable teacher need not be the best current producer. A knowledgeable operator need not be a capable teacher. A productive employee need not receive a matching wage. A firm can finance transferable training without benevolence, and a firm can rationally underinvest relative to society without being mistaken about its own return. Conversely, a profitable training opportunity may be missed because its benefits are poorly measured or because the people who supply teaching time are rewarded for a competing activity.

## 2. What is specific, and to what?

### 2.1 The classical distinction is useful but not a binary description of actual people

Becker's framework distinguishes training useful across employers from training whose returns depend on the current employment relationship. In the competitive benchmark, workers bear the cost of general training through lower initial compensation; specific investments can involve shared costs and returns because either party's departure threatens the other's return. His analysis already allows training to combine general and specific components. This is a theory of incentives under stated assumptions, not evidence that all practical knowledge is either perfectly portable or wholly stranded. [R09-S01]

Lazear's skill-weights model offers a different account: component skills can all be usable elsewhere while the employer's particular combination is unusual. Losing a close match then destroys value without destroying every skill. Search costs and the availability of similar jobs influence the loss. The paper also distinguishes skill-based tenure returns from matching, incentive contracts, and rent extraction. Its simulations demonstrate a possible mechanism; they do not identify the composition of any contemporary senior analyst's knowledge. [R09-S02]

Empirical evidence also resists a purely firm-bounded interpretation. Neal uses U.S. Displaced Worker Surveys from 1984–1990, restricting the principal analysis to 2,641 men displaced by establishment closure and reemployed full-time. Previous experience and tenure retain more wage value when workers remain in their industry. Selection corrections and industry/union controls address competing explanations, but industry switching is not randomized and full-time reemployment is selective. Wage premia cannot cleanly separate skills from rents. The study supports portability within a line of work, not costless movement across all employers or occupations. [R09-S03]

**Interpretation for the book.** “This farm's soil” is a useful image of adaptation, but it must not become a literal prohibition on exchange. At least five objects should be distinguished in any proposed case: general technique; sector/task knowledge; knowledge of the employer's records and systems; relationships and mutual expectations; and the coordination routines of a particular team. The location of acquisition does not determine the complete domain of use. Knowing an institution's approval chain may be unusually local; learning how to diagnose an ambiguous requirement may travel. An outsider can arrive with the latter and learn the former, at a cost.

Specificity is therefore best treated as a **replacement-cost and adaptation-time question**, not a declaration of impossibility. The relevant comparison is the departing person's contribution against the best feasible combination of successors, outside hires, records, tools, training, and workflow changes. That combination might be worse, expensive, slow, or unavailable at the required moment. It need not be unavailable forever.

Nor does a senior salary identify the magnitude of this asset. Age, organizational rank, tenure, occupational experience, skill, and teaching effectiveness are different variables. The book's concern can be made sharper by naming demonstrated expertise and developmental contribution rather than treating chronological age as their sufficient proxy.

### 2.2 Wage compression changes the training incentive

Acemoglu and Pischke show why the competitive benchmark is not a universal prediction. If wages rise less with skill than productivity does, the employer can capture part of the gain from technologically general training. Search frictions, asymmetric information, institutions, and complementarities can generate this wedge. Merely having a fixed mobility cost is insufficient: the return to additional skill must change the surplus. Their analysis also distinguishes worker financing from employer financing and discusses mixed empirical evidence on institutional channels. It establishes conditional incentives, not a general empirical finding that compressed wages or weak mobility improve welfare. [R09-S04]

This matters in two directions. The fear of poaching can reduce an employer's incentive to develop people, but poaching is not a complete explanation of training behavior. Conversely, evidence that an employer willingly trains workers does not prove that workers capture the resulting value. A labor-market arrangement can support investment while leaving employees with limited bargaining power. A good account of apprenticeship must ask both whether capability is reproduced and how gains and constraints are distributed.

## 3. Present production and future capability are different outcomes

### 3.1 The mentoring opportunity cost is observable in some settings

Emanuel, Harrington and Pallais's June 2026 revision studies a Fortune 500 retailer's software engineers over 2019–2024. The office-closure extract covers 1,055 engineers; a second extract studies return-to-office periods. Difference-in-differences compares teams whose proximity changed differently. Co-location increases coding feedback by 18.3%; less-experienced workers benefit more, while experienced engineers write less code. Reopening also improves code-quality proxies, and earlier co-location predicts persistent benefits after feedback equalizes. Identification requires parallel trends despite sorting and pandemic shocks; code deletions and reversions imperfectly measure quality. The study concerns proximity in one organization, not randomized mentoring assignments, retirement, or AI layoffs. [R09-S06]

That evidence is especially relevant because it makes a hidden accounting problem concrete: an individually measured producer may look less productive while helping coworkers become more productive. It does not follow that any meeting, co-location mandate, or senior headcount creates learning. The operative unit is an interaction that changes what a learner can subsequently do. A review process which silently fixes a junior's work can maintain output while transmitting little understanding; an explanatory review may do both.

The distinction suggests separate evaluation horizons. Present correction should be measured by avoided defects and downstream consequences under comparable workloads. Development should be measured by later independent performance, retention, transfer to unfamiliar cases, and appropriate escalation. An employer that records only shipped units or labor cost per completed document can miss the second; a training program that records only attendance can miss both. These are measurement proposals, not effect sizes estimated by the studies.

### 3.2 Training is not confined to inherited apprenticeship

De Grip and Sauermann randomize the timing of training among selected experienced Dutch call-center agents. The main 2008–2009 panel contains 74 agents after reassignment exclusions, with 1,859 agent-weeks. The estimated average performance gain is about 10%, without detected quality deterioration. Within-team timing also identifies a positive peer effect, though its direct estimate is only significant at the 10% level in the inspected working paper. Selection into eligibility, the small sample, reassignment exclusions, short follow-up, and possible cross-team spillovers limit generalization. This is evidence of teachable work skills and coworker effects, not proof of permanent gains or senior-exclusive transmission. [R09-S05]

Alfonsi and colleagues' Ugandan experiment offers an explicit outside-training comparison. It follows 1,714 disadvantaged applicants and roughly 1,500 firms, comparing six-month vocational and subsidized firm training. Both routes improve measured sector skills; firm-training gains arrive sooner but fade relative to vocational training. Take-up differs greatly: 68% versus 24%, so assignment and complier effects must not be conflated. The structural explanation emphasizes reentry to employment and certifiable skills; certification itself was not separately randomized. Selected institutes, intensive programs, eight trades, subsidies, and low-income labor-market conditions constrain export to professional knowledge work. [R09-S07]

These findings challenge two excessive conclusions. External education is not necessarily incapable of replacing parts of an internal pipeline; nor is sending workers to a course enough to recreate the institution's complete practical competence. The useful question is which component can be taught where, how success is verified, and how external learning is integrated with local responsibility. It is entirely coherent to need both portable preparation and situated supervised work.

### AI-mediated transfer and its limits

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond study 5,172 support agents, mostly outside the United States, during a staggered 2020–2021 rollout. Difference-in-differences estimates a 15% gain in resolved issues per hour, measured on the subset with quality data. Novices gain more; top performers show small quality declines. The model uses successful agents' conversations and internal documentation. Rollout was not the earlier pilot RCT. Outage analysis suggests retained learning, but outages are rare, last minutes to hours, and the outcome is chat duration, not resolution quality. The authors acknowledge noisy estimates and possible chat-composition differences; adherence is not randomized. Human agents retain discretion; mentor removal was not tested. Individual pay and aggregate demand are unobserved. This establishes a plausible training complement and partial transmission mechanism, not durable cross-domain mastery, full mentor replacement, or a wage forecast. [R09-S09]

The comparison with human mentoring should remain symmetrical. Neither proximity alone nor AI availability alone is an adequate description of a developmental treatment. One can ask whether either intervention increases explanation, practice, feedback, exposure to varied cases, and responsibility. It is also important to distinguish reproducing **known successful responses** from maintaining the capacity to diagnose genuinely new failures. A stock of good examples can help current entrants; the system still needs a process that detects when examples have become obsolete. Whether that process requires particular human roles is an empirical question for each workflow, not a permanent boundary assumed by this review.

## 4. Departures, retirement, replacement, and organizational memory

### 4.1 Departure studies isolate different losses

Jaravel, Petkova and Bell use U.S. tax/patent records and 4,714 premature inventor deaths in an event-study comparison with matched controls. Eight years later, surviving collaborators have approximately 3.8% lower labor earnings and 15% fewer citation-weighted patents. Noncollaborating coworkers show no comparable loss; excluding joint patents substantially reduces the effect. This isolates a relationship-specific loss more closely than generic firm-wide knowledge destruction. Death causes are unavailable, and the proposed accumulation-through-experience mechanism relies on explicitly noncausal heterogeneity tests. Patent collaborators' unexpected deaths do not identify planned-retirement effects or the efficacy of documentation. [R09-S13]

Jäger, Heining and Lazarus's June 2024 manuscript studies roughly 34,000 unexpected German worker deaths, 1980–2007, in establishments with 3–30 full-time employees. Matched difference-in-differences finds increased hiring alongside a net employment decline; remaining workers' earnings and retention rise. Same-occupation workers benefit more, while estimates for some manager/high-skill departures are negative for other occupations, with wide intervals. The version's thin-versus-thick wage differences are statistically insignificant and hiring heterogeneity is mixed, qualifying earlier summaries. Revenue and productivity are unavailable, wages are top-coded, and balanced small-establishment sampling limits extrapolation. Model-implied replacement costs are not measured knowledge losses. This is evidence of substitution and complementarity, not a uniform collapse mechanism. [R09-S14]

Sauvagnat and Schivardi study 306,246 Italian firm-years in 2005–2015 and 1,076 executive deaths at or below age sixty. Event studies find persistent profitability disruption concentrated in thin executive labor markets. Their retirement extension finds smaller, shorter disruptions, consistent with advance replacement planning. Death causes are unobserved; sickness exclusions and controls strengthen the design, but market thickness is not randomly assigned and retirement is more endogenous than premature death. The population is contractually designated executives, not all experienced workers. Results connect replacement opportunities to organizational loss; they do not establish a universal irreplaceable-person mechanism. [R09-S15]

These studies should not be averaged into a single “cost of losing a senior.” A deceased collaborator, a replaceable position, and a strategic executive are different treatments and production relationships. Death is valuable for identification precisely because it avoids some selection into dismissal or quitting; it also differs from a scheduled retirement or planned restructuring. Advance overlap, recruiting, succession, and redesign may change the treatment. A causal estimate for an unexpected loss is not automatically the effect of dismissing a person after transferring their responsibilities.

The coexistence of productive complementarities and substitution is not contradictory. Losing one worker can increase demand for colleagues who perform similar tasks while harming colleagues whose distinct skills were complementary. Recruitment can repair the vacancy without reproducing the former team match. These distinctions explain why “someone was hired” and “nothing valuable was lost” are not interchangeable statements.

### 4.2 Retirement is an outflow, not a measured trajectory of expertise

Burmeister and Deller's systematic review covers 28 English-language publications from 2000 through June 2015. It organizes retirement-related knowledge retention around knowledge, individual, relational, and contextual characteristics. Crucially, it characterizes the evidence as often anecdotal, with limited longitudinal and outcome measurement; mutual exchange may fit better than one-way transfer from old to young. It is useful for specifying mechanisms and measurement gaps, not quantifying today's retirement losses or proving that a generation's knowledge is unreproducible. [R09-S10]

For the book, a capability stock must be defined relative to a task and organizational need. Its evolution includes learning by existing workers, new entrants, experienced hires, returning workers, codification, and changes in what the organization needs to know, as well as retirement, departures, forgetting, and obsolescence. A fixed pre-AI birth cohort necessarily ages; the relevant productive capacity need not decline at the same rate. A particular firm can lose access while other firms gain it. Economy-wide destruction must be distinguished from reallocation and from a costly local interruption.

A demographic count alone also misses concentration. Ten departures with overlapping knowledge may be less consequential than one departure involving an undocumented dependency. Conversely, retaining the holder without time, motivation, or an appropriate learner need not preserve the capability for the next generation. This suggests mapping critical tasks and succession coverage, rather than using the age distribution as a sufficient knowledge-risk measure.

### 4.3 Documentation and external expertise can help, but maintenance still matters

Bloom and colleagues revisit an Indian textile-management experiment initiated in 17 firms and 28 plants. By 2017, substantial treatment-control differences in management practices persisted despite partial abandonment; practices also spread within treated firms. Original consulting assignment was randomized. Later managerial turnover and director-time explanations are observational and partly reported by participants. Long-run productivity is proxied using looms per worker, not a complete profit/productivity panel. This supplies both counterevidence to inevitable loss and evidence that maintaining routines requires attention. The small, selected family-firm setting and intensive consulting preclude a universal prescription. [R09-S11]

**Synthesis.** Knowledge can reside partly in people, partly in tools and records, and partly in coordinated practice. Treating a written manual as equivalent to demonstrated successor competence is an error; treating any transferable routine as necessarily dependent forever on its original author is another. A useful test of retention is whether successors can recover the rationale, recognize boundaries, perform the task, and update the routine when conditions change. That test can fail even when many documents exist, or succeed after the original senior leaves.

## 5. Why a firm might train, undertrain, or reduce capacity

### 5.1 Different failures call for different explanations

The literature supports several distinct mechanisms. The following is this review's analytical decomposition, rather than a claim that one study estimated them all:

- **A privately profitable investment is not recognized.** Quality failures or developmental benefits are not measured well enough to enter decisions.
- **A recognized return is delayed or uncertain.** Financing limits, business survival, changing demand, and technical obsolescence can make an otherwise useful investment unattractive now.
- **Benefits escape the decision-maker.** A firm loses trained workers to other firms; a department pays for learning used elsewhere; a senior supplies mentorship while evaluation rewards only individual throughput.
- **Incentives privilege a reporting horizon.** The decision-maker benefits from visible near-term savings while later costs arrive under different management.
- **The investment is genuinely no longer worth its cost.** Some expertise loses demand; some routines should be replaced; retaining every historical task can obstruct learning the new ones.

Calling all five “liquidation” would erase the distinction between avoidable destruction and ordinary adaptation. The comparison should include the expected future value of current correction and capability renewal, but also their real costs and feasible substitutes. No legal entitlement, optimal staffing level, or humane transition policy follows from the model alone.

Graham, Harvey and Rajgopal provide direct evidence about reporting pressure, using 401 executive surveys and 20 interviews. In the public-firm scenario responses, 79.9% would consider cutting discretionary spending to meet a quarterly earnings target and 55.3% delaying a project despite some value loss. The overall response rate is 10.4%; the main public-firm analysis uses 312 respondents. These are hypothetical/self-reported choices in the 2003 setting, not observed AI layoffs or a causal estimate of training cuts. They make the short-horizon channel credible without establishing the essay's specific chain of events. [R09-S12]

This is an important boundary on the “never hits the spreadsheet” language. Payroll and training costs are easy to record; that does not show managers never forecast retention, failure, recruitment, or development costs. An unrecognized asset need not literally appear on a balance sheet to influence a rational decision. Conversely, acknowledging it verbally does not ensure that a budget or promotion system rewards it. R-11 should govern formal accounting claims; R-09 only evaluates economic incentives and evidence.

### 5.2 Firm returns need not become worker earnings

Adhvaryu, Kala and Nyshadham randomize soft-skills training across lines and interested workers in five Indian garment factories: 2,703 applicants, including 1,087 selected trainees. The inspected April 2021 version estimates 13.5% higher productivity after training and positive coworker spillovers, but no detectable wage or retention effect. Production and personnel records are stronger than satisfaction reports; volunteer selection, one employer, attrition, and the local wage structure limit generalization. The package changes several skills together, so teamwork is a supported interpretation, not an independently randomized component. The result is a concrete counterexample to equating productive learning with proportional compensation. [R09-S08]

The implications for D-002 are conditional. Architectural work, tool education, and consequential operation can each create value. Their compensation depends on demand, outside options, observability, wage-setting institutions, and how the joint surplus is divided. Scarcity can strengthen bargaining, but being unusually useful inside one firm may also leave the worker with weak outside options. A firm-specific advantage is not automatically an employee's market-wide bargaining advantage.

The essay's rehiring-premium scenario requires several additional conditions: retained demand for the skill, inadequate substitutes, a hiring firm able and willing to pay, workers who remain available, and bargaining that transfers scarcity value to them. If demand disappears, an experienced worker can instead suffer displacement loss. If knowledge is portable and the market is deep, replacement may be quicker. If all firms depend on others to train, a collective capacity shortage is possible; its size and timing require equilibrium evidence. None of these branches permits a guaranteed two-to-three-year salary prediction.

### 5.3 Contemporary AI labor evidence: changing tasks is not yet a settled wage law

For the Danish comparison, see [R-05's Humlum–Vestergaard capsule](../R-05/README.md#the-danish-test-work-changes-before-pay) [R09-S16]. Adoption and occupational exposure define different comparisons; their results should not be treated as estimates of a common treatment.

Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen's **August 2026** revision uses ADP payrolls covering 3.5–5 million full-time workers monthly in a balanced firm panel through June 2026. It documents a 19% relative employment shortfall for ages 22–25 in more-exposed occupations against less-exposed peers, concentrated in reduced hiring. Pay differences are smaller; compensation excludes bonuses, equity and several variable-pay categories. The authors explicitly reject a causal interpretation: pretrends exist, education controls attenuate estimates, and ADP patterns exceed national-survey counterparts. Occupation exposure is not observed firm adoption; age imperfectly captures experience. The headline is a kept-pace comparison, not the share of jobs proved destroyed by AI. It flags a possible entry-route problem without establishing its cause or future skill consequences. [R09-S17]

A task-level productivity gain, a worker's time saving, an age-specific employment difference, and economy-wide prosperity are different outcomes. They need not appear together, and averaging can conceal redistribution within an occupation. In particular, fewer entry jobs could reduce opportunities for practice even when the workers who do get jobs learn faster. Better tools could also expand output and demand enough to sustain training. R-09 therefore treats junior employment and development quality as jointly relevant indicators, not substitutes for one another.

A wage or employment null over an early observation window is not a permanent immunity result. A negative exposed-occupation trend is not, by itself, proof that AI caused it or that the occupation's skill stock will collapse. The evidence can justify watching entry routes and bargaining outcomes without licensing an inevitable transition narrative.

## 6. Ford: useful attributed case, unresolved personnel counterfactual

The [R-18 Ford capsule, §4](../R-18/ford-surplus.md#4-the-2026-gray-beard-story-verified-core-and-inference-limits) owns the recovered reporting origin, hiring composition, date window, initial-quality measure, and their limitations. Its [assertion ledger](../R-18/ford-surplus.md#7-claim-level-evidence-ledger) owns the historical wage/productivity corrections. Those factual capsules are incorporated by reference, not duplicated here.

R-09's additional targeted searches looked for a personnel chronology separating dismissals, voluntary exits, retirement, supplier recruitment, and internal promotion; compensation terms; and separately measured mentoring effects. Searches of Ford's public reporting and subsequent coverage did **not locate those records**. This is a documented non-recovery, not a claim that such records do not exist. News repetitions do not multiply independent confirmation of a single reporting lineage.

The narrow safe use remains an attributed example of management seeking complementary expertise. The key unresolved counterfactuals are whether the same people had been removed for AI substitution, whether earlier retention would have been cheaper, which intervention improved outcomes, and whether successors acquired durable independent judgment. Those questions cannot be answered by the subsequent quality ranking alone.

The analogy also creates a testable distinction for the book. “Veterans help repair and improve automation” is compatible with partial transfer into machines. “Veterans prove the machine can never acquire the relevant capability” is a different, unsupported proposition. Likewise, the historical coexistence of high productivity and a wage policy cannot predict the compensation of a new review job. R-18 has already rejected that inference; the contemporary training and labor evidence does not restore it.

## 8. Implications, investigated gaps, and a research design that could close them

### 8.1 What the literature permits the book to argue

The warning becomes more useful when stated as an omitted-investment risk: **automating current production without measuring and deliberately preserving effective capability formation can impose costs that a short-run payroll comparison misses**. That formulation leaves room for successful substitution, external education, institutional memory, labor mobility, and better teaching technology. It also directs attention to operational choices rather than claiming that the final pre-AI cohort must be preserved intact.

A conditional claim is not a weak call for inaction. An organization can face high-consequence risk before there is a mature longitudinal AI literature. But precaution should be proportionate to observed dependencies and alternatives. It is reasonable to ask for evidence that successors and controls work before removing a critical dependency. It is not evidence-based to assume every incumbent role is equally critical or every old task developmentally necessary.

For workers, developmental opportunity is one important job attribute, not a universally dominant choice over pay. Household constraints, program credibility, transferable learning, treatment by the employer, and future opportunities affect the comparison. The literature does not justify telling every junior to accept lower compensation on an unverified promise of apprenticeship.

### 8.2 Important gaps actually investigated

1. **AI substitution followed by expertise loss.** The recovered studies cover task assistance, training, proximity, deaths, and employment patterns. They do not jointly identify senior layoffs → reduced current correction → diminished successor competence → costly later rehiring in the same organizations.
2. **Retirement versus expertise.** Retirement-retention scholarship and executive planned-exit evidence were examined. No representative contemporary measure establishes the essay's net shrinking stock of institution-specific judgment across the knowledge economy. This is narrower than claiming no retirement research exists.
3. **Long-run AI-assisted learning.** The principal workplace evidence contains useful but bounded retention tests. Domain transfer, rare failures, multi-year competence, and future mentor production remain unmeasured in the core case. R-08 should test learning more broadly.
4. **Ford personnel history and wages.** Provenance is settled upstream; the individual employment histories, compensation comparison, and causal training effects remain unrecovered after targeted searches.
5. **Documentation as a complete replacement.** Organizational persistence and AI-mediated transfer establish possibilities, not an equivalence theorem between records and experienced judgment. No broad claim that documentation alone is sufficient is admitted.
6. **Employer motives and externalities.** Theory and reporting surveys support several possible mechanisms. Their relative quantitative importance in current AI staffing decisions remains unknown. A claim of universal executive myopia would exceed the evidence.
7. **Equilibrium rebuilding.** Firm-level results cannot determine whether outside hiring merely reallocates a fixed stock or whether wages, education, entry, and innovation replenish it. R-12 must assess this system-level question.

### 8.3 A discriminating prospective design

A useful study would follow comparable teams before and after AI adoption, distinguishing adoption from changes in staffing, office proximity, task allocation, and mentoring incentives. Where feasible, randomize protected mentoring time or training designs rather than merely randomizing tool access. Record predecessor experience, successor baseline ability, skill specificity, recruiting availability, and the quality of accessible records.

Use separate outcomes: quality-adjusted completed work and costly errors now; independent delayed performance and escalation judgment later; the time and cost of developing or hiring replacements; and worker wages, hours, exits, and promotions. Include unfamiliar cases and test with and without assistance. Track whether high-performing learners can teach others, rather than declaring the pipeline repaired after an initial productivity gain.

Identification would require addressing selective layoffs, changing demand, anticipation, and spillovers across teams. A staggered comparison should not treat every change after adoption as its effect. Measure what survives departure in documents, software, other people, and external networks. Estimate the private employer return separately from the worker and social returns. Those distinctions would allow the book's mechanism to be falsified as well as illustrated.

### 8.4 Downstream handoffs and author choices

- **R-08:** use the shared S09 capsule for AI workplace retention evidence; test curricular design and the specific efficacy of D-001 rather than assuming the labor evidence proves it.
- **R-05/R-15:** analyze bargaining, appropriability, and ownership/control without treating valuable human capital as an automatically owned or freely transferable asset. This review makes no legal portability conclusion.
- **R-11:** retain the distinction between an economically valuable capability and its accounting recognition; do not infer invisibility to managers from absence on a balance sheet.
- **R-12:** reconcile entry employment, productivity, wages, and consumer gains with demand and adjustment; no universal Ford wage trajectory survives.
- **R-06/R-14:** examine how practice is represented and transferred, without treating today's unrecorded knowledge as permanently unlearnable by machines.

Luke decides whether to keep the seed metaphor, how forceful to make the institutional warning within these limits, and what obligations employers have toward development and displacement. Research does not decide those normative questions. It does rule out presenting guaranteed collapse, impossible replacement, or assured wage rescue as established facts.

### Review method and access limitations

This is a targeted analytical review, not a preregistered exhaustive search or meta-analysis. Searches deliberately paired the senior-loss hypothesis with mobility, training substitution, wage compression, organizational persistence, and replacement evidence. Established human-capital theory was read alongside experiments, quasi-experiments, administrative panels, organizational case follow-up, systematic review, executive survey, and early AI evidence. Those designs answer different questions; their estimates have not been pooled into a universal effect.

Publicly accessible author, university, journal, NBER, IZA, and official-repository copies were preferred. Several direct fetches failed: the older IZA Lazear conference copy, the Maastricht published de Grip–Sauermann copy, a Darr–Argote–Epple scan, and some publisher landing-page redirects. Recoverable alternatives were used where stated. The Darr scan and the Gathmann–Schönberg task-transfer paper were discovery leads, not full-text evidence in this review. Subsequent Ford articles were not treated as an independent personnel dataset. Paywalls, registrations, and private data were not bypassed.

Small cooperative-firm experiments are not representative of all employers; administrative data improve coverage without making every event exogenous. Wage outcomes are not direct skill measurements. A result based on years of earlier production should not be presented as an experiment with 2026 AI. Work is strongest on mechanisms and existence counterexamples; it is weakest on cross-sector magnitudes, multi-generation skill dynamics, and future equilibrium compensation. Explicit gaps do not block a literature review's completion, but they do block categorical empirical claims.
