# R-08 Apprenticeship and learning

**Research date:** 4 October 2026. **Status:** substantive literature review accepted after independent quality review; not author adoption or a manuscript chapter. Remote durability is verified separately after publication. **Claims:** C-015, C-016, C-017, C-018, C-021 and the human-capability component of C-053. Source IDs R08-S01–S16 belong to this review.

## 1. Findings and the claims being tested

The evidence supports a conditional apprenticeship problem. A production system can improve the work delivered today while giving its human participants less opportunity to acquire the capabilities needed tomorrow. It does not establish that AI necessarily destroys learning, that reading machine-generated work is intrinsically noneducational, or that an AI-assisted builder necessarily learns faster. The consequential distinction is what the learner actually does, what feedback follows, and what the person can subsequently do under a specified level of assistance.

D-001's combination of foundational understanding, informed tool use and supervised substantive judgment is consistent with several established learning mechanisms. The reviewed literature does not validate that complete sequence as a universal curriculum, determine its required manual dosage, or demonstrate that it reliably produces advanced institutional judgment. Treating D-001 as a tested educational intervention would confuse the author's accepted position with evidence. D-002 likewise makes tool-use education a distinct productive activity; showing that it is a coherent activity does not establish its effectiveness, its substitution for professional supervision, or its compensation.

The admitted claims have different strengths. C-015 is most defensible as an account of capability formation and a risk from removing developmental activities. C-016 survives when “does not by itself” means that checkpoint occupancy cannot certify learning, rather than that all review is sterile. C-017 requires separating seniors' present production contribution, teaching contribution and authority. C-018 is a conditional career-development hypothesis, not an observed general transition from station operator to foreman. C-021 identifies a possible learning pathway whose acceleration, depth and cross-domain transfer must be measured. C-053 is a synthesis to be evaluated component by component, not an empirical law established by this review.

## 2. The outcomes must be separated before comparing interventions

An organization can need both excellent assisted performance and a reserve of independent human capability. These are different objectives, and their appropriate weights may differ across jobs. The following distinctions are analytical definitions used throughout this review:

1. **Assisted performance:** quality, speed, completeness and safety of the human–tool system while the aid is present. A better artifact may reflect retrieval, automated execution, human learning, or all three.
2. **Immediate independent performance:** performance just after instruction or practice, without the experimental aid. This is stronger evidence of acquisition than an assisted output, but can still rely on short-lived memory or very close task similarity.
3. **Retention:** independent performance after a meaningful delay. The delay, intervening practice and available resources must be specified; “post-test” alone does not imply retention.
4. **Transfer:** application to changed tasks. New numbers in the same equation, an unfamiliar bug in the same library, a different project, and a different occupation are progressively different tests. Transfer must specify what changed and what remained available.
5. **Advanced judgment:** detecting an inappropriate problem frame, choosing among imperfect methods, anticipating consequences, handling novelty, seeking help and knowing when to stop. A narrow examination can test components, not the whole construct.
6. **Authority and accountability:** the permission and obligation to make or escalate a consequential decision. These are institutional assignments, not learning scores. A competent person may lack authority; a formally accountable person may lack competence.

A further distinction separates **failure to acquire** a new skill from **loss of a previously acquired** skill. The two require different baselines. A worse immediate quiz after assisted practice does not, by itself, show that an experienced professional's established expertise has atrophied. Nor does faster assisted work show that a novice is independently catching up with an expert.

A fair experiment therefore measures at least two trajectories: productive output under the intended tools, and human capability under explicitly defined testing conditions. It also measures the cost of instruction, supervision and assessment. For safety-critical work, an unaided test need not require removing all real-world references; a degraded-tool or misleading-advice condition may better test the fallback capability actually required. The proper fallback standard is a design question, not automatically a demand to reconstruct every historical manual process.

## 3. What established learning science does and does not support

### Making the learner's activity visible

**R08-S01.** Collins, Brown and Holum's cognitive-apprenticeship framework combines modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection and exploration. It emphasizes making expert and learner thinking inspectable, varying contexts, and fading support. Its sequencing includes understanding the whole task before mastering isolated parts. The article develops its framework through reading, writing and mathematics examples; it is a theoretical and instructional synthesis, not a randomized estimate of the entire package. It also allows learning through active reading and listening. [S01, “From Traditional to Cognitive Apprenticeship”; “A Framework for Designing Learning Environments.”]

This gives the book a better unit of analysis than whether a person occupies the middle seat. A reviewer who diagnoses a flaw, explains why it matters, proposes a competing interpretation and receives informed feedback performs substantial cognitive work. A reviewer who approves an attractive answer may do little of it. “Review” names their place in the workflow, not their learning activity. Equally, a builder can spend an entire session accepting proposed changes without forming a useful model of the system being built. The operator/builder distinction is therefore insufficient as a learning taxonomy.

### Useful difficulty is different from avoidable friction

**R08-S02.** Roediger and Karpicke compared studying and retrieval using prose passages in two experiments with 120 and 180 Washington University undergraduates. In Experiment 1, restudy was better after five minutes, whereas initial testing was better after one week: 56% versus 42% of idea units recalled. Experiment 2 likewise separated confidence from delayed recall. The endpoint was memory for studied prose, not professional decision quality or far transfer. [S02, pp. 250–254, Experiments 1–2, Figs. 1–2.]

**R08-S03.** Kalyuga and colleagues review cases in which instructional support advantageous for novices becomes redundant or disadvantageous with experience. Worked examples and progressively reduced guidance are central cases, including mechanical-trade and database tasks. Their account attributes these interactions to prior knowledge and cognitive load. It is a narrative review, without a pooled universal effect or a single threshold for “enough” expertise. It does not test language models. [S03, pp. 23–29, especially “Interactions Between Levels of Expertise and the Worked Example Effect,” pp. 26–27.]

**R08-S04.** Sinha and Kapur's three-level meta-analysis covers 53 studies and 166 comparisons of problem solving before instruction versus the reverse. The pooled advantage for conceptual knowledge/transfer is g = 0.36, 95% CI 0.20–0.51; procedural knowledge is approximately null. Instruction that builds on learners' attempted solutions is among the favorable design features. Most evidence concerns school or undergraduate learning over hours or days. Citation-based selection, mixed experimental quality and nonrandom implementation moderators limit generalization; moderator associations do not isolate each mechanism. [S04, Method; Results, “Pooled Effect Size Estimates”; Discussion.]

These findings resist a binary choice between effortless AI and virtuous struggle. The relevant question is which operation a learner needs to practice. Generating a diagnosis, recalling a governing principle and comparing alternatives can matter; searching a confusing interface or repeating already mastered formatting may add little to that objective. Conversely, eliminating all errors before the learner encounters them may remove opportunities to diagnose a failure. Support should be evaluated against the intended learning outcome, rather than judged by how much discomfort it creates.

The same distinction qualifies “manual fundamentals.” Unaided practice can mean the learner must produce an answer, rather than receive a completed answer. It does not require absence of explanation, teaching, examples or feedback. Foundation-building and tool-supported instruction can be interleaved. A curriculum might require independent mastery before delegating a class of decisions while using AI explanations from its first lesson. That arrangement differs from a strict prohibition on tools until all fundamentals are complete, although both might serve D-001's underlying-understanding objective. Whether the author intends such flexibility is an author question; this review does not silently choose it.

### Practice is consequential but its amount is not a universal law

**R08-S05.** Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald synthesize 88 studies, 111 independent samples and 11,135 participants. Accumulated practice explains markedly different shares of performance variation across domains; the professions category contains only seven effects and 321 participants. Most measures are retrospective and the analyses are correlational. Their challenge to practice-only explanations is material, but unexplained between-person variance is not a causal estimate of how much training can improve a person. [S05, Method; Table 1; General Discussion, inspected OnlineFirst pp. 3–4, 8–9.]

**R08-S06.** Ericsson and Harwell contest broad coding of “deliberate practice,” emphasizing individualized, teacher-guided improvement with feedback, and distinguish it from purposeful, structured and maintenance practice. Their reanalysis and argument defend a narrower construct and recommend measuring practice quality, rather than accumulated hours alone. The paper represents a participant in a methodological dispute, not an independent resolution of it. [S06, “The Original Definition”; “Quantification of the Amount and Quality of Practice”; concluding recommendations.]

The important disagreement concerns what was measured, which populations were compared, and whether a correlation between hours and rank answers a question about effective instruction. It is unsafe either to proclaim a fixed-hours recipe for expertise or to infer that practice barely matters in occupations. For R-08, training should be described by task, feedback, difficulty, spacing, duration and assessment. Calling ordinary repeated production “deliberate practice” would obscure the very institutional investment the seed-corn argument asks readers to notice.

### Contact with consequences is not self-validating

**R08-S07.** Kahneman and Klein reconcile naturalistic-decision and heuristics-and-biases perspectives around two conditions for skilled intuition: sufficiently informative environmental regularities and opportunities to learn them. They distinguish valid recognition from confidence produced by misleading cues, and stress the quality of feedback. This is a joint theoretical synthesis supported by prior studies, not a treatment trial proving one optimal curriculum. [S07, pp. 519–523, the recognition discussion and “Professional Intuitions.”]

A consequential workplace can provide poor teaching signals. A decision may succeed because of luck; a defect may surface after its author has left; a senior's intervention may prevent harm without revealing the junior's mistaken reasoning. Office conventions may teach conformity to power rather than better prediction. These are plausible alternative mechanisms, not evidence that all institutional knowledge is illusory. They imply that the book should distinguish exposure to consequences from feedback that allows the learner to attribute those consequences correctly.

## 4. Direct AI evidence separates assistance from acquisition

### Answer provision and task completion

**R08-S08.** Bastani and colleagues randomized classrooms in one Turkish high school to GPT-4 Base, a teacher-designed guarded tutor, or ordinary resources during four mathematics sessions in 2023. In the main analysis, assisted practice increases were 0.137 and 0.361 on a 0–1 scale. Subsequent closed-resource exam effects were −0.054 (SE 0.022) for Base and −0.004 (SE 0.013) for Tutor; the control exam mean was 0.321. These are immediate tests of closely related problems, not long-term retention. Randomization is by classroom; 2,848 observations are repeated student-sessions, not 2,848 people. Guardrails combine answer restraint with teacher-supplied correct solutions and hints. [S08, Experimental Design; Table 1; Discussion.]

**R08-S09.** Shen and Tamkin's preregistered main experiment assigns 52 Python-experienced participants, unfamiliar with Trio, to a GPT-4o chat assistant or no assistant for two coding tasks. The immediate unaided assessment favors control, reported d = 0.738, p = .01; average completion time does not significantly improve. Exploratory usage clusters associate conceptual engagement with higher quiz scores, but participants were not randomized to those behaviors. The study concerns a new library, not novices to all programming, and neither delayed retention nor production incidents are observed. Anthropic affiliations warrant disclosure. [S09, §§4–7; Fig. 6; Appendix C.]

Together, these designs support a narrower causal conclusion than “AI makes people less intelligent”: particular assistance arrangements can reduce acquisition measured immediately afterward. They do not identify an inevitable consequence of every model, workflow, profession or duration. The failure mechanisms could include avoided practice, misplaced confidence, errors in feedback and incentives to finish rather than understand. Randomizing tool access identifies the package's effect; attributing it exclusively to one psychological mechanism requires additional manipulation.

The coding experiment is particularly relevant to S-05 because its treatment participants also built working things. Successful construction is not enough to establish the proposed apprenticeship reversal. Conversely, its small behavioral subgroups do not prove a general curriculum based on a preferred prompting style. The correct inference is a research target: compare assigned interaction protocols that preserve independent reasoning, rather than advise people to imitate the best-scoring users and call the causal question solved.

### Designed tutoring supplies counterevidence

**R08-S10.** Kestin and colleagues used a randomized crossover in Harvard introductory physics, with 194 eligible students and two lessons in 2023. A purpose-built tutor at home outperformed active classroom instruction on immediate post-tests; the controlled regression reports 0.63 SD. Median tutor time was 49 minutes versus an assumed 60 classroom learning minutes. The intervention bundles self-pacing, expert-prepared prompts/answers, videos and structured interaction; it does not isolate generic chatbot access. There is no delayed retention test. Small peer groups were randomized, and the paper's eligible-student and lesson-observation denominators differ. [S10, Results; Methods; “Context, limitations, and future directions.”]

**R08-S11.** Wang and colleagues randomized access to Tutor CoPilot among human tutors serving a southern US district. The initial assignment involved 900 tutors; launch attrition left 782, and 1,787 students were identified. In 4,136 analyzed sessions, access increased unconditional exit-ticket passing by four percentage points (SE one point). The inspected v2 finds no significant end-of-year math improvement; students' exposure varied and was not itself randomized. Tutor retention is explicitly future work. One author worked part-time with the provider; expert-informed system development preceded deployment. [S11, §§3–7, Table 2, Appendix K and Conclusion.]

**R08-S12.** De Simone and colleagues studied six weeks of teacher-guided Copilot sessions in nine Nigerian schools. Of 1,328 students assigned, 759 completed the final assessment. Adjusted analyses report 0.238 SD for English (SE 0.068) and 0.31 for a broader English/AI/digital composite. Differential attrition is material; Lee bounds and weighting are reported. The intervention adds instructional time, computers, peer interaction and trained teacher support to usual schooling, so it does not identify the chatbot alone. There is no long-delay follow-up. The World Bank paper acknowledges Mastercard Foundation funding. [S12, §§2–3; Tables 2–3, 10–11; §4.2.]

The negative and positive studies answer different questions. A system optimized to deliver the correct answer is a different treatment from an environment organized around student responses and feedback. A lesson introduced through a tutor differs from optional assistance while completing a task quickly. A package added to ordinary schooling differs from replacement of an active class. Even comparable outcome labels can mask different tests, baselines and delays. It would be misleading to average these headline effects into a single “AI learning effect.”

The studies do show that the author's concern need not entail recreating every former junior task unchanged. Some educational functions can be reorganized around technology. They do not establish that junior jobs can safely disappear because tutoring software will recreate the full learning environment. In particular, better student outcomes from an assisted human tutor are not evidence that the tutor has acquired independent expertise. Educational services delivered and educator capability produced must be measured separately.

### In-work transfer and review interfaces

The detailed customer-support evidence belongs to [R-09, “AI-mediated transfer and its limits”](../R-09/README.md#ai-mediated-transfer-and-its-limits), source R09-S09. Its outage analysis is suggestive evidence against categorical learning failure, but does not establish broad professional transfer. R-08 does not repeat that study's factual capsule.

**R08-S13.** Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos studied 199 retained US online participants making food-substitution decisions with simulated AI. Cognitive-forcing interfaces improved decisions on erroneous-advice trials relative to simple explanations; unassisted participants still did better on those trials. The all-trial advantage of forcing over simple AI was not significant. Effects and preferences varied with motivation to think. This tests decision-time reliance, not subsequent retention. Exclusions and the low-stakes task limit generalization. [S13, §§3.4–4.3, Tables 1–3; Discussion.]

Thus asking for an independent judgment before revealing an answer has a plausible protective rationale, but its educational effect should not be inferred from a contemporaneous error-detection effect. Explanation displays, slow-down prompts and review gates can be useful controls without becoming apprenticeship. They also consume attention. If a workflow increases throughput while leaving time for neither explanation nor feedback, its nominal human checkpoint can coexist with both poor oversight and poor development. R-07 must examine that operational constraint directly.

## 5. Supervised consequential work and the limits of substitution

**R08-S14.** Beane's comparative ethnography includes 94 surgical procedures and 478 observation hours at five hospitals in 2013–2015, plus interviews across 13 additional institutions. Robotic arrangements could make residents' participation optional and delegation more discontinuous; effective learners sought alternative practice paths. The interview study deliberately sampled exceptional and ordinary learners using attending assessments. This identifies organizational mechanisms, not a population prevalence or randomized technology effect. Robotic surgery is not autonomous generative AI. The account also describes risky, undersupervised learning; it should not become a prescription to bypass safety. [S14, advance-publication pp. 7–8, 11–17, 18–24, 26–30.]

**R08-S15.** Seymour and colleagues randomized 16 Yale surgical residents to standard training with or without additional supervised VR practice to an expert-derived criterion. Blinded assessments of subsequent gallbladder dissection found fewer errors after VR: 1.19 versus 7.38 per case. The reported 29% faster dissection was not statistically significant in the Results text. This supports near transfer of trained technical skills to supervised clinical work, within approximately two weeks. It does not establish replacement of supervision, comprehensive surgical judgment or durable cross-procedure competence. [S15, Methods, Results and Discussion, including the discussion of judgment.]

**R08-S16.** Kraft, Blazar and Hogan synthesize 60 causal-design studies of teacher coaching, including 56 randomized trials. Pooled effects are 0.49 SD on observed instruction and 0.18 SD on student achievement. Evidence is concentrated in developed-country settings, especially early literacy. The interventions often combine coaching with other support; individual components and observed scale differences are not separately randomized. Larger programs have smaller average effects. This provides stronger causal evidence for professional-development packages than mere expert proximity, but does not test AI-workplace apprenticeship or establish effects after support ends. [S16, Method; Tables 1–2, 6; Sensitivity Analyses.]

These studies illuminate different sides of substitution. A technology can remove the reason an expert needs a junior's help, making participation a discretionary cost. A training technology can also let a learner practice a component without imposing its full risk on others. The second possibility does not automatically solve the first: access to simulation, sufficient practice, feedback quality and the transition into real decisions still need an institutional home.

There are at least four separately substitutable functions inside “senior support”: supplying examples and explanations; observing performance and diagnosing misconceptions; granting and calibrating responsibility; and intervening when consequences become unacceptable. A recorded demonstration, automated tutor or simulator may contribute to the first two. That does not establish who should exercise the latter functions or whether the tool recognizes situations outside its validated scope. Nor must all functions be performed by one long-tenured employee. Teams, external experts, near-peers and carefully validated systems are candidate complements or substitutes whose adequacy must be tested.

This qualification matters for S-02's strong language. The literature supports identifying which developmental functions disappear with a departure; it does not support the inference that removing any group of seniors inevitably makes expertise irrecoverable. Retaining experienced staff also does not guarantee learning if their incentives, workload or supervisory habits exclude juniors from meaningful attempts. Counting senior heads is an incomplete proxy for instructional capacity.

The stakes claim needs equal care. Actual work is valuable because it includes constraints and feedback that exercises may omit, but no ethical curriculum needs preventable harm as its teaching instrument. A junior can own a provisional diagnosis, defend a proposed action and observe the eventual outcome while an authorized supervisor controls release. Responsibility can increase as capability is demonstrated. Simulation can prepare rare or dangerous cases, but its transfer must be checked rather than assumed. These are evidence-informed design implications, not a finding that this complete arrangement has been experimentally validated across knowledge work.

## 6. A testable interpretation of D-001

The accepted sequence is most useful as a set of developmental obligations whose implementation can be tested. The following proposed architecture preserves the distinction between author intent and research judgment.

**Specify the foundation by its later use.** For each delegated activity, identify what a human must recognize, explain, reconstruct, test or escalate. An analyst may need to detect selection bias and distinguish a forecast from an observation; reproducing every formatting step may be unnecessary. For programming, understanding invariants and debugging a failure is different from memorizing every API. These examples illustrate assessment design, not validated occupational competency standards.

**Assess before fading support.** Use unaided or appropriately resource-bounded attempts, unfamiliar examples and explanations in the learner's own words. Track whether improvement survives delay. Do not infer mastery from attendance, completed AI-assisted work, satisfaction, fluency or a senior's unexplained approval. A failure should reveal the component requiring more instruction, rather than trigger either permanent exclusion or premature delegation.

**Teach tools explicitly, but test their scope separately.** Education about selecting a tool, structuring requests, supplying context, protecting information, inspecting evidence and recognizing uncertainty is a genuine curriculum. Its proximal endpoint is capable tool use. The further claim that it develops independent domain judgment requires domain-specific assessments and transfer tests. D-002's distinction between tool-use educators and consequential operators is therefore useful, while the causal bridge between their activities remains an empirical question.

**Create a genuine first judgment.** Before a senior reveals the preferred answer, the junior should state the proposed decision, assumptions, missing information, expected consequences and escalation conditions. AI may prepare material within the curriculum's permissions. The senior then reviews the junior's reasoning as well as the finished artifact. A change log that records only edits is inadequate if the learner never discovers why those edits were necessary.

**Protect access to varied cases and corrective feedback.** Repeatedly assigning a junior the same easy approvals can produce speed without preparation for a changed case. Conversely, assigning only exceptional failures may overwhelm a novice. Case selection should expose the limits of procedures while preserving enough continuity to learn them. Record who receives challenging work, whose attempted reasoning gets reviewed, and who is repeatedly bypassed. Otherwise observed promotion can reflect prior selection into learning opportunities rather than the effect of station occupancy.

**Evaluate reusable capabilities at their boundaries.** Tested components may reduce routine execution costs, but their tests do not establish that a learner understands their assumptions or that the current situation meets them. Ask learners to identify a valid use, an invalid use and a failure introduced by combining otherwise valid components. This is a proposal for assessing informed abstraction and composition, not proof that people must always perform these roles or that AI cannot learn them.

**Measure the supervisor's scarce time.** Compare total preparation, review, correction and rescue time across training regimes, alongside current output and future capability. Savings in explanation delivery can coexist with greater demands for contextual supervision. Conversely, better practice tools may enable seniors to concentrate on difficult diagnoses. Neither direction can be assumed from a model's price or a training platform's per-user charge.

D-001 is consequently neither disproved by a successful AI lesson nor confirmed by an adverse quiz result. The exact sequence, dose and graduation thresholds remain open. A stronger empirical defense would establish which activities can be omitted, which can be simulated, which can be supported from the outset, and which require supervised participation in real decisions.

## 8. Unresolved evidence and the comparisons that would resolve it

The most important absence is a longitudinal, task-level comparison of the five pathways specified by this program: output-only review; independent foundational practice; deliberately designed practice; AI tutoring plus building; and supervised consequential work. None of the reviewed studies randomizes that full set while following participants into advanced professional responsibility. This is an evidence gap, not a reason to score all pathways equally or treat every mechanism as speculation.

A useful study would assign comparable learners to explicit protocols, document actual behavior and measure assisted output, immediate independent performance, delayed retention, novel-case transfer, error calibration and escalation. It would test new cases against independent criteria, not use the same AI system as sole tutor and examiner. Repeated assessments would distinguish temporary deprivation effects from established capability. Baseline expertise, motivation and access to mentors would be measured; withdrawal, noncompliance and differential attrition would remain visible.

Domain-specific unresolved questions include:

- **Software:** durable debugging and architectural judgment after extended agentic building; maintenance and security outcomes; transfer to a changed library or system; whether tests expose shallow understanding or merely familiarity with an interface.
- **Analysis and administration:** causal identification, contextual fit and exception handling after removal of routine drafting/retrieval work; feedback where outcomes are delayed, political or weakly observable.
- **Education:** delayed subject learning versus short exit-ticket success; independent tutor/teacher learning after AI assistance; effects of trained teacher support separated from additional time and hardware.
- **High-stakes professional work:** whole-role rather than component transfer; safe fallback competence, rare-event practice and appropriate supervision; whether senior time saved on routine explanation is actually reinvested in development.
- **Cross-domain building:** acquisition costs including verification and repair; performance with and without the tool; breadth acquired at the expense of depth or another activity; selection of unusually motivated early adopters.

The central institutional comparison is not “keep every old task” versus “automate everything.” It is whether the replacement arrangement reliably reproduces the capabilities an organization decides it needs. That decision includes a normative choice about resilience, professional responsibility and future opportunity. Research can estimate consequences; it cannot assign those priorities for the author.

## 9. Downstream implications and future refinement questions

These are future refinements of accepted, revisable D-001/D-002, not reopened pending decisions.

**R-09** should estimate the staffing, time, incentives and portability conditions under which developmental functions persist. Do not convert R-08's risk mechanism into inevitable senior scarcity or a wage premium. **R-10** should separate supported breadth from retained expertise and far transfer; the book's autobiographical cases remain illustrations, not controlled evidence. **R-07** should test whether human judgment has time, information, intervention power and meaningful review, rather than assuming an interface label creates it. **R-02/R-13** should assess the full lifecycle and the independence of checks used as feedback. **R-05/R-12** should not infer compensation from pedagogical usefulness. **R-14/R-16** should not export human learning results directly into claims about persistent machine learning or simulation-trained actors.

Three author decisions remain: what independent human capabilities should institutions deliberately preserve; how to express the manual-foundations commitment while allowing evidence-sensitive sequencing; and how much weight to place on the hopeful builder pathway before long-term comparative evidence exists. No new author decision is needed to correct the conflation of assisted output with learning, or to remove a universal empirical claim unsupported by its evidence.

### Remaining access and verification limits

Several author/institution copies returned technical errors or incomplete responses; none was treated as evidence merely because a download succeeded. The reviewed S05 PDF was accessible to the web reader when a direct local request failed. The Beane text is a visible reproduction of the paper, not a secondary summary; its pagination and DOI match the advance version, but a separately obtained author-hosted final text would strengthen recoverability. No restriction was bypassed. Search snippets and promotional summaries were used for discovery, not as substitutes for the methods/results cited here.

The review deliberately does not use one-session brain-activity claims, generalized self-report “critical thinking” correlations, vendor testimonials, or productivity benchmarks as evidence of durable professional skill loss or gain. These may motivate questions, but do not replace outcome-matched identification. The remaining uncertainty concerns what the inspected evidence cannot identify, not whether the historical source corpus should be rewritten to remove its tensions.
