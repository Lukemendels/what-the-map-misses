# R-06 — Tacit and situated knowledge

**Program:** R-06; admitted claims C-010, C-011, C-047  
**Research date:** 4 October 2026  
**Status:** Substantive literature review accepted after independent quality review; remote durability verified separately after publication. Research judgments are not author adoption or a thesis freeze.

## 1. Scope, provenance, and result

The relevant author trajectory is unusually clear. S-01, “Polanyi’s floor,” argues that machine capacity for tacit territory knowledge is structurally zero. The S-06 steelman asks whether this supposed limit is instead an observation-cost asymmetry; it expressly makes counterfactual absence, surveillance-induced relocation, and consent provisional defenses. S-10 §4 rejects dependence on an eternal monopoly. The historical ledger preserves that evolution:

- **C-010:** “Humans possess an eternal non-computable monopoly on tacit territory knowledge; AI capacity for this input is structurally zero.” Historical status: **SUPERSEDED**.
- **C-011:** “Current situated human judgment can supply context, relationships and consequence-sensitive understanding unavailable in a particular model workflow.” This is a present, possibility, and workflow-specific proposition.
- **C-047:** “The tacit moat could erode through cheaper observation; counterfactual absence, relocation under surveillance and consent are provisional defenses.” Neither erosion nor a successful defense is assured.

These exact propositions govern the review. Auxiliary tests below disaggregate their mechanisms; they do not create replacements for the stable claims. The S-06 inference that only ownership pays if the floor erodes is separately constrained by D-002 and R-05/R-12/R-15. It does not become true by surviving an epistemological discussion.

**Main finding.** There is a substantial case for situated knowledge, but no sound inference from tacitness to a permanent human monopoly. The literature separates at least three questions: what a competent person can articulate; what another system can learn or reproduce; and who should be authorized to act. They have different answers and require different evidence.

The strongest correction runs in both directions. Recording is not identical to acquiring knowledge, but explicit verbal rules are not the only material from which machines learn. Observation, demonstration, corrective interaction, environmental feedback, and organizational redesign can change the boundary. Conversely, a camera does not automatically reveal decision-relevant causes, alternatives, relationships, or permission. Successful performance on one embodied task does not settle philosophical accounts of understanding or demonstrate competent institutional judgment everywhere.

The defensible research position is therefore conditional. Identify the actual missing information, access rights, learning process, feedback, and consequences in a named workflow. Then compare systems. Do not explain every human advantage as irreducible tacitness, or every machine success as proof that all remaining differences are temporary.

## 2. What the contested words mean

These are analytical distinctions, not a new canonical taxonomy. They overlap: a skill may be embodied, socially acquired, partly articulable, and poorly recorded at the same time.

| Term | Relevant distinction | What would have to be tested |
|---|---|---|
| **Unrecorded** | Information or behavior has not been captured in the accessible record. | Whether authorized recording, annotation, or retrieval closes the performance gap. Absence from a corpus alone says nothing about inherent computability. |
| **Unarticulated** | A practitioner has not stated a rule, reason, or distinction. | Whether questioning, comparison, demonstration, or analysis elicits something useful. Failure of spontaneous explanation is not proof of inarticulability. |
| **Inarticulable/tacit knowing** | On strong philosophical accounts, skilled integration is not exhaustively reducible to propositions about its components. | Whether the disputed claim concerns exhaustive explanation, identical experience, or task-equivalent performance. These are not interchangeable endpoints. |
| **Embodied** | Competence depends on perception–action coupling, bodily capacities, tools, and learned coordination. | Which sensory and action channels matter; whether another embodiment can achieve the required outcome. “Embodied” does not by definition mean biologically human. |
| **Relational/socially situated** | Knowing depends on shared histories, participation, access, expectations, or coordination with others. | Whether useful information is withheld, whether relationships change others’ responses, and whether access or participation can be transferred. Having a description of trust is different from being trusted. |
| **Counterfactual** | Judgment concerns alternatives not realized, including avoided actions and their anticipated consequences. | Whether alternative outcomes are identifiable under specified assumptions, whether safe interventions are possible, and whether the expert’s own inference is correct. |

“Tacit” should not be used as a residual bucket containing everything a system currently misses. A missing calendar entry, the ability to manipulate an unfamiliar object, an unstated professional norm, and a disputed moral judgment need different remedies. Conversely, a legible output can be the product of a process its producer cannot fully describe. Text is a record of behavior as well as a vehicle for explicit assertions. Training on text need not mean receiving an exhaustive written algorithm for producing it.

For the book’s economic argument, the crucial additional distinction is **reproducing a productive contribution versus reproducing a person’s entire knowledge**. A substitute need not know everything the displaced activity’s practitioner knows. A task can also be standardized, rearranged, or abolished. This is a logical qualification to the monopoly argument, not a prediction that any particular reorganization will be desirable or successful.

## 3. Polanyi and the disagreement over conversion

### Polanyi is stronger than a claim about missing documents

Polanyi’s 1965 Wesleyan lecture on tacit knowing distinguishes subsidiary awareness from focal attention: particulars acquire significance in an integrated performance. Subsidiary awareness need not be unconscious. He distinguishes contingently unidentified clues from the change in meaning produced by shifting attention onto them. His argument includes a strong claim that symbolic manipulation does not itself produce the experienced integration. Yet he also says analytical decomposition can improve a skill when followed by practice. These are philosophical and phenomenological arguments illustrated by perception, skilled action, and scientific inquiry, not a comparison of modern learning systems. [R06-S01, typescript pp. 2–7, 9–14, 19–21]

This rules out two convenient misreadings. Polanyi is not simply saying that an expert has an undisclosed instruction manual. But he is not establishing that every useful consequence of expertise vanishes once some part is described or instrumented. The corpus’s claim that digitization necessarily destroys the valuable thing is too categorical. A representation may omit the experience while still helping another person learn, supporting a tool, or enabling a different production method.

Nor should the review hide Polanyi’s anti-reductionist commitment and present him as an early theorist of cheap training data. The point of disagreement is genuine. A learned policy that succeeds without verbally specified rules is counterevidence to an operational impossibility claim; it is not by itself a refutation of the claim that phenomenal meaning involves personal participation. The economic inference fails unless it supplies the missing bridge from that philosophical claim to the impossibility of functional substitution.

Polanyi also offers an institutional account. His lecture on science links independent inquiry to overlapping expert judgments, mutual adjustment, and professional authority. Standards coordinate a distributed community rather than residing in a complete central specification. This is a normative and interpretive account of scientific organization, not causal evidence that all real expert communities are efficient or open to correction. [R06-S02, typescript pp. 14–19]

### Conversion, practice, and social participation

Nonaka’s 1994 theory makes interaction among socialization, externalization, combination, and internalization central to organizational knowledge creation. It gives articulation, metaphor, prototypes, shared experience, and action complementary roles. It does not reduce successful learning to uploading documents. The framework develops mechanisms and managerial designs through conceptual reasoning and illustrative organizational material; it is not an identified estimate of how much tacit knowledge becomes transferable. [R06-S03, pp. 16–24, 27–35]

Tsoukas disputes the conversion interpretation. In his prepublication chapter, tacit and explicit are interdependent aspects of knowing; producing statements about a performance does not extract the whole performance into propositions. Nevertheless, instructive dialogue can redirect attention and enable new action. The disagreement is therefore not “communication works” versus “communication is impossible.” It concerns what communication accomplishes and whether “conversion” misdescribes it. [R06-S04, manuscript pp. 4–9, 10–16]

A bread-making machine is a useful crux, not a decisive winner. If engineering uses a baker’s practice to build machinery that produces satisfactory bread, that can establish a transfer of economically useful capability. It does not establish that the machine possesses every skill, disposition, or experience of the baker. Conversely, denying that the entire skill was converted does not establish that the baker’s particular production contribution remains commercially indispensable. The philosophical disagreement can persist while the division of productive work changes.

Collins’s own exposition adds distinctions among relational, somatic, and collective tacit knowledge. His social account separates bodily execution from specialist linguistic fluency and emphasizes participation in a community. It also distinguishes skills that are tacit because of human bodily limits from collectively sustained social understanding. The stronger claim about collective knowledge is a theoretical position, not a demonstrated impossibility result for every future artificial participant. The complete 2010 book was not inspected here; the cited evidence is his substantive author-hosted explanation. [R06-S05, “Tacit Knowledge,” “Social and Minimal Embodiment,” “Somatic-limit and Collective Tacit Knowledge”; R06-S05b, 2010 book description]

Together these positions prevent a false consensus. Nonaka’s managerial vocabulary, Tsoukas’s practice epistemology, and Collins’s social taxonomy do not identify the same object when they say knowledge has or has not been transferred. An empirical study must state its endpoint: successful output, adaptable performance, explanation, participation, identity, or legitimate responsibility. Changing the endpoint after an inconvenient result makes a human-monopoly thesis unfalsifiable.

The strongest contemporary test is not whether a model can recite Polanyi. It is whether it can act appropriately when cues change, revise an ineffective response, recognize a changed norm, and seek help when necessary under realistic access and accountability. Passing such a test would matter economically even if philosophical disagreement persisted. Failing it would establish a bounded failure, not an eternal incapacity.

## 4. Institutions do more than store knowledge in people

### Rules, incentives, adaptation, and memory

The S-01 institutional bridge needs correction. North distinguishes formal constraints, informal norms, and enforcement, and connects learning, beliefs, and institutional change. He expressly denies that institutional evolution guarantees efficient outcomes. His framework is a historical–cognitive account, not a theorem that useful information can exist only in human carriers. [R06-S06, §§II–V, VII]

Williamson’s comparative account centers incomplete contracting, adaptation, asset specificity, and governance alternatives. It asks which feasible arrangements handle particular hazards at lower cost; it does not infer that every informal practice is valuable or that hierarchy is always superior. His warning against invoking transaction costs to explain anything after the fact applies equally to invoking “tacit knowledge” without a discriminating mechanism. [R06-S07, pp. 675–680, 684–686]

These theories explain why a cheaper recording technology might change organizational boundaries without eliminating institutions. Information can become easier to specify while enforcement, disagreement, opportunism, responsibility, and adaptation remain consequential. The direction is not predetermined: improved verification might support outsourcing; sensitive shared context might favor internal provision. The book cannot infer the institutional answer merely from the existence of tacit content.

Levitt and March’s routine-based account provides the direct foundation for organizational learning beyond individual memory. Lessons persist through procedures, technologies, relationships, and socialization, but may also be lost or mislearned. Their account explicitly discusses recording costs and computer-based changes to them, alongside competency traps and misleading interpretations of experience. It is a theoretical synthesis of prior studies, not a causal estimate for AI. The inspected copy is a later chapter reprint, with its own pagination. [R06-S08, reprint pp. 516–529, 531–534]

This supports the current synthesis’s proposition that an institution can behave differently without every actor internally remembering the original event. It does not prove that a repository entry, test, or rule actually changes future behavior. Persistence of a record, retrieval of that record, appropriate interpretation, and changed action are separate links. R-14 and R-13 must test those links rather than relabeling stored text as learned expertise.

### Practice studies and transfer studies answer different questions

Brown and Duguid use Orr’s service-technician ethnography to distinguish formal job descriptions from practical diagnosis, stories, and community participation. Their article is a theoretical synthesis drawing on that ethnography, not an independently randomized field study. It makes concrete how inadequate documentation can hide the work that keeps a service functioning. Its evidential reach is richer mechanism description, not a representative estimate of the prevalence of hidden human work in present AI deployments. [R06-S09, pp. 40–47, 48–54]

Szulanski studies 122 transfers of 38 practices in eight self-selected companies, using 271 questionnaires from sources, recipients, and third parties. Absorptive capacity, causal ambiguity, and difficult relationships emerge as leading correlates of transfer difficulty. Companies were encouraged to identify difficult transfers; aborted transfers remained elusive. Only 87 observations entered the first canonical analysis, with 142 in a reduced robustness specification. This is evidence of uneven, relationally conditioned transfer, not proof of impossibility or a clean causal ranking of obstacles. [R06-S10, pp. 32–37, measurement appendices]

The contrast matters. Ethnography can reveal work missing from management’s categories; a transfer survey can compare reported obstacles across practices. Neither licenses “humans simply absorb all this through years of exposure.” The learner may lack prerequisites, a trustworthy source, opportunities to try, usable feedback, or incentives to disclose. More detailed records address some of these obstacles, not all. But the existence of difficult transfers presupposes an important comparison with successful transfers; it is not evidence of an impermeable boundary.

Organizational codification can have several effects that the book should measure separately:

1. Preserve an already successful procedure.
2. Reveal inconsistencies through attempts to explain it.
3. Reduce the skill needed for a narrower task.
4. Support learning by directing attention rather than replacing practice.
5. Freeze a mistaken or obsolete practice.
6. Suppress useful exceptions because only documented behavior is rewarded.

These are alternative mechanisms. A documentation project can perform more than one at once. Success should be measured by changed, sustained work under relevant variation, not by the volume of records produced.

### Established cross-program evidence

The detailed [R-09 capsule on AI-mediated transfer](../R-09/README.md#ai-mediated-transfer-and-its-limits), source R09-S09, is the relevant workplace counterexample to equating difficult-to-explain skill with impossible machine mediation. Its published outcome and access limits remain there. R-06 uses it only to establish that some useful patterns can move through an AI-assisted workflow without an exhaustive prior verbal theory. The study does not identify the fraction of success caused specifically by tacit rather than explicit content, or test complete mentor replacement. The relevant source paragraphs on construction and training were inspected again for this question.

Likewise, [R-18’s wine-sector analysis](../R-18/portugal.md#wine-was-an-economic-system-as-well-as-a-crop), source P12, supplies the historical warning that the organizational role and bargaining position of local knowledge can change. The detailed archive-based account is not repeated here. Ricardo/Methuen provides no evidence of machine incapacity. Changing returns to intermediaries also does not establish that their knowledge was wholly codified or ceased to matter.

## 5. Machines can learn from more than articulated rules

### Three different demonstrations

Video PreTraining learns a behavioral prior from Minecraft video by using labeled contractor data to train an inverse-dynamics model and infer action labels for a much larger video corpus. Subsequent behavioral and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning produces capabilities beyond the initial prior. The difficult diamond-pickaxe result is only 2.5% of the reported 10-minute episodes, not reliable human-equivalent performance across the game. This is developer-produced experimental evidence in a simulated world, with measured outputs and ablations. [R06-S11, §§3–4.6, especially pp. 5, 8–9]

Open X-Embodiment tests cross-robot transfer. Its dataset breadth and its experimental training mixture are different denominators: 22 embodiments in the assembled dataset, nine in the reported mixture, and 3,600 evaluation trials across six robots. Performance improvements are heterogeneous, including underfitting by the smaller model in data-rich domains. The paper explicitly does not establish generalization to new robots or radically different sensing and actuation. [R06-S12, §§IV–VI, Tables I–II]

The π0.5 report goes beyond a single laboratory scene: household manipulation is evaluated in unseen scenes, including three real homes. Its quantitative endpoint is task-rubric progress, not an all-or-nothing measure of household competence. Standard comparisons use small repeated trial sets; canceled episodes can be removed. The authors report persistent problems with unfamiliar handles, occlusion, distraction, modest context, and simple instructions. This is substantial developer evidence for bounded generalization, not an independent deployment audit or measurement of occupational replacement. [R06-S13, §§IV–VI, Fig. 7, Appendix B]

These experiments matter for the *mechanism*, even though S-01 initially brackets physical work. They demonstrate routes from perceptual traces and interaction to functional performance without the expert first writing an exhaustive rulebook. They cannot, by moving into a different domain, refute a narrowly specified claim about an analyst’s relationships or public-sector judgment. R09-S09 is closer to the latter’s actual workplace setting, while still leaving broad institutional judgment untested.

The machine need not literally ingest a person’s subjective skill. It can learn a different implementation that uses recorded consequences of that skill. A categorical prohibition on “computing tacit knowledge” obscures this possibility. The stronger remaining question is whether the implementation achieves adequate performance and adaptation at acceptable full cost in the actual setting.

### Observation is a pipeline, not a magic input

The relevant chain is:

**authorized access → informative observations → usable training signals → learned policy → deployment feedback → sustained, acceptable outcomes.**

Cheap sensors improve only some links. A meeting transcript may omit a gesture, but video can include it. Video may still omit relevant prior agreements, private preferences, or consequences. More modalities can help if they provide relevant independent information; they can also increase spurious correlations, collection burdens, and privacy exposure. Neither universal success nor universal failure follows from recording more.

Demonstration also differs from passive surveillance. A cooperative expert can stage a contrast, identify an exception, or explain a correction. An observed worker may have incentives to conceal, simplify, or perform for the observer. A real system may need interaction rather than a bigger static archive. This distinction weakens both extremes: consent-sensitive demonstrations may transfer capability without ambient surveillance, while unrestricted observation may still produce poor learning.

Finally, training and operation costs matter. Sensor deployment, expert annotation, safe experimentation, curation, evaluation, and maintaining valid permissions are part of the acquisition process. A report of generalization does not establish that this process is economical everywhere. R-01/R-02/R-14/R-16 should attach full-cost and persistence tests to capability claims.

## 6. Counterfactual absence: a real inference problem, not a species boundary

S-06 says that avoidance leaves no data. That is too strong as an empirical generalization. An unsent memo’s text may be unavailable, but whether someone sends a memo can be an observed action variable; opportunities, preceding events, later outcomes, and repeated contrasts can also be recorded. What remains difficult is identifying why the person abstained and what would have happened otherwise. These are different missing quantities.

A purely illustrative example makes the distinction clear. An experienced official does not schedule a contentious meeting. A calendar records no meeting; a decision log might record that the option was considered; discussion might reveal an anticipated veto. None of those observations by itself proves that the meeting would have failed. Equally, failure to observe a meeting does not make its absence wholly uninformative. This is an analytical example, not testimony about any office.

Two technical problems strengthen the serious version of the objection:

- **Distribution changes when the learner acts.** Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell’s DAgger analysis shows why prediction accuracy on expert-generated states can fail to control sequential task error. Collecting expert labels at states the learner actually visits changes the learning problem. Its guarantees require specified losses, policy classes, and learning assumptions; the paper’s demonstrations are games and structured prediction. [R06-S14, §§2–4, Algorithm 3.1, Theorems 2.1–3.3]
- **Correlation can be mistaken for a cause.** De Haan, Jayaraman, and Levine construct and test imitation settings where extra information encourages a misleading shortcut. Targeted interaction or expert queries can improve performance, with task-dependent results. Their benchmark findings establish a failure mechanism and remedies, not that every observational learner fails or that all remedies transfer to open institutions. [R06-S15, §§2–5, Figs. 1, 3, 6–8]

Zhang, Kumor, and Bareinboim give a more exact account of unequal information. Under structural-causal assumptions, the learner’s observations may be insufficient to reproduce an expert’s reward distribution even with perfect estimation. They distinguish identifying a policy’s reward from identifying a policy that imitates expert performance, and derive conditions under which imitation remains possible despite latent variables. The inspected 2020 proceedings paper supplies theory and synthetic demonstrations, not an estimate of hidden institutional knowledge in workplaces. [R06-S16, §§1–4, Definitions 2–5, Theorems 1–2]

The important lesson is **specify the information structure**. Infinite observations of the wrong variables need not identify the required answer. But that impossibility is conditional on the variables, assumptions, policy space, and permitted interventions. It is not an impossibility for machines as a species. The same limit constrains a new human who lacks the relevant cues. An incumbent human may have an advantage because of extra experience or access, while also being wrong about the counterfactual.

Counterfactual skill should consequently be tested prospectively where possible: make predictions before the outcome, compare equally informed alternatives, record uncertainty, and examine cases where restraint was unnecessary as well as cases where it prevented harm. Selecting only spectacular avoided disasters makes the expert’s story impossible to check. A human’s remembered rationale is evidence about that person’s explanation, not automatic ground truth about the unrealized world.

The appropriate response is neither “cameras will see everything” nor “the unchosen can never be learned.” Some gaps admit richer observations, explicit elicitation, natural variation, safe experiments, or simulations validated against real outcomes. Others cannot ethically or practically be closed. Their importance must be assessed against the task’s tolerance for error. Rare catastrophic alternatives are especially difficult; R-13/R-16 must not treat plausible simulations as observed counterfactuals.

## 7. Surveillance can alter the territory, but relocation is conditional

Bernstein’s factory study combines embedded observation in China with a 2009 curtain experiment. Two physical lines, representing four line-shifts, received the privacy intervention; 28 line-shifts were controls. Defect-free output improved, while qualitative evidence identified concealment of useful adaptations under observation. The treatment was a bounded privacy change, with few treated clusters, not ambient AI recording; the design does not isolate every suggested mechanism. It supports the possibility that visibility changes work and obstructs learning, not inevitable migration of every meaningful conversation. [R06-S17, manuscript pp. 8–19, 20–28, 38–44]

Counterevidence is important. Pierce, Snow, and McAfee analyze staggered monitoring adoption in 392 US restaurant locations from five firms. Their October 2014 manuscript reports lower detected theft and higher sales, primarily through changed behavior rather than turnover. Identification depends on rollout timing and parallel-trend assumptions; detected theft is incomplete, and sales are not a direct measure of tacit knowledge. The authors cannot rule out substitution into unmeasured misconduct and distinguish their unobtrusive transaction monitoring from cameras. NCR supplied data but, according to the disclosure, neither funded the authors nor controlled publication. [R06-S18, §§3–6]

The studies need not contradict one another. Monitoring can discourage an activity whose suppression improves the measured objective, or discourage experimentation the organization needs. Feedback may be trusted or punitive; the observer may be a helpful teammate or an evaluator. Employees may gain from better service or fear losing credit, autonomy, or employment. These contextual differences predict heterogeneous effects. Neither study establishes the effect of persistent AI observation on professional knowledge transfer.

The relocation hypothesis therefore needs at least four measurements: changes in where communication occurs, changes in what people disclose, changes in actual performance, and changes in the learner’s usable knowledge. A decline in visible discussion could mean concealment, successful standardization, reduced need for discussion, or reduced collaboration. Observing one of these is not observing all four.

The Lucas-critique analogy in S-06 is useful only as a warning about behavioral response to a changed regime. It is not an estimated law that economically important activity always moves beyond the sensor’s reach. Some work cannot readily relocate; people may willingly demonstrate it; observation may create useful feedback; and practices can change in ways that make formal coordination more effective. The magnitude and direction remain empirical.

### Consent and permissible information flows

Nissenbaum’s contextual-integrity account rejects the idea that visible or accessible information is unrestricted information. Appropriate collection and appropriate flows depend on contextual relationships and purposes. This is normative analysis, not an estimate of adoption speed or proof that consent will be withheld. It helps distinguish permission to attend a meeting from permission to retain, repurpose, or disclose its contents. [R06-S19, §IV, especially PDF pp. 20–25]

There is also a concrete legal distinction. The European Data Protection Board’s current public guidance treats consent as one possible GDPR basis, with specific conditions; employment power imbalances can make freely given consent problematic. It also describes other lawful bases and their limits. Thus an “institutional consent wall” is neither a universal legal veto nor a blanket license for management. This is a bounded EU illustration, not advice on a particular recording or a survey of all jurisdictions. [R06-S20, “What are the possible legal bases,” “Consent,” “Legitimate interest”]

The book should separate four questions:

1. Can the information be sensed or inferred?
2. Is access actually available in this workflow?
3. Is collection and subsequent use authorized and lawful?
4. Is that arrangement legitimate or desirable?

A capability improvement can affect the first question without deciding the others. Restrictions can be valuable protections even if they lower potential model performance. They can also differ across workplaces and change over time. Calling them mere friction wrongly assumes the technology’s desired data access is the social objective; calling them a permanent cognitive moat mistakes a governance choice for a property of intelligence.

Consent-sensitive alternatives deserve direct comparison: bounded demonstrations, purpose-limited records, selective retrospective explanation, local processing, restricted retention, and assistance that does not preserve a person-level history. These are proposed design options, not findings that every option is sufficient or legally available. No recording or data collection is authorized by this review.

## 8. Integrated judgment and implications for the book

The reviewed schools disagree over what knowing *is*, but converge on an important practical correction: competence is not adequately described by a stock of decontextualized statements. The unresolved philosophical issue should remain visible. It should not carry a stronger economic conclusion than the evidence permits.

Three competing mechanisms can coexist:

- **Information-access advantage:** an incumbent knows relevant facts or has observed relevant contingencies absent from a model’s inputs. Better authorized access can erode it.
- **Learning and coordination advantage:** productive adaptation requires practice, suitable feedback, and responsive counterparts. An archive alone may not supply these; interactive learning or workflow redesign may change the comparison.
- **Authority and relationship advantage:** a person’s participation changes what other people reveal, accept, or do. A model’s accurate description of this relationship need not reproduce its causal effect or legitimacy. Whether a different relationship can substitute is another question.

These are review-derived mechanisms, not claims that the author has already adopted. They make C-011 researchable without replacing it with C-010. A human may have valuable knowledge in one workflow and be unnecessary in another. A machine may replace a bounded contribution while needing human support elsewhere. Whether human placement should move is then a joint capability, institutional, and normative question.

R-06 also rejects a symmetrical overstatement: “all tacit knowledge is merely too expensive to record.” That is a plausible account of **some** unrecorded information, but does not resolve practice-based interpretations, causal identification, social participation, or rights. It is particularly weak when changing access changes the behavior being learned. The right criticism of an eternal moat is not an equally unsupported law of inevitable full capture.

For the manuscript, the safe argument is to show where the early claim erodes first, give genuine counterexamples, and specify what remains uncertain. Distinguish a capability demonstrated in a research setting from a stable, economical, acceptable production arrangement. Avoid replacing a failed monopoly argument with the promise that consent or counterfactual absence will always rescue it.

### Downstream reconciliation

- **R-07:** responsibility and legitimate authority must not be derived from a residual skill gap. An informed human role also requires actual access, time, and intervention power.
- **R-08/R-09/R-10:** knowing how to use a transferred procedure, retaining a skill, and developing adaptive judgment remain different learning outcomes. Tacitness does not prove that one particular junior-work pipeline is indispensable.
- **R-01/R-02/R-04:** record the cost of information access, demonstrations, feedback, verification, and reorganization. Neither cheap tokens nor cheap recording measures the complete productive service.
- **R-05/R-12/R-15:** a changing knowledge advantage does not determine wages or ownership returns. Relational position, legal entitlements, competitive alternatives, and bargaining remain separate.
- **R-13/R-14/R-16:** written institutional memory, a retrieved precedent, a changed policy, and an updated model are distinct mechanisms. Counterfactual and out-of-distribution tests require real validation.
- **R-17:** test whether relationship history improves outcomes beyond additional context, model capability, or selection of skilled users. “The pair knows” is an empirical question, not a consequence of labeling history relational.

Author-only decisions remain: how prominently to narrate the abandonment of the original monopoly claim; which human roles should remain protected even if substitutable; and what restrictions on observation the book should advocate. Research constrains factual statements but cannot choose these values.

## 10. Investigated gaps and discriminating tests

This review can close its literature synthesis while leaving genuine questions unresolved. The following gaps are specific to claims the sources cannot establish:

1. **The current prevalence of C-011.** Classic workplace studies identify mechanisms; modern assistance and robotics studies supply different comparisons. No representative estimate was recovered of how much present knowledge work depends on information available to an incumbent human but unavailable to an equally supported model. A useful study would compare a model with baseline records, the same model with an authorized richer record, a human with matched information, and an experienced human–model team. Outcomes should include novel cases, errors, escalation, and full cost. This design is a proposal, not an existing result.
2. **Ambient recording versus cooperative teaching.** Bernstein and Pierce study distinct monitoring regimes. Neither supplies a longitudinal comparison of continuous AI recording, selected demonstrations, structured elicitation, and ordinary documentation. Measure consent/participation, disclosure quality, learning, relocation, and employee consequences separately. A high opt-in rate would not by itself establish low coercion or high knowledge quality.
3. **Counterfactual competence in open institutions.** Causal-imitation results demonstrate limits under explicit models and remedies in selected settings. They do not settle what information makes an actual policy office’s avoided decisions identifiable. Safe natural experiments or preregistered predictions can help; irreversible high-stakes experiments may be unacceptable. Lack of an ethical test is a limitation, not proof of superior human access to truth.
4. **Long-horizon generalization and maintenance.** VPT, RT-X, and π0.5 show substantive learning, but not years of reliable institutional membership, rare-event judgment, or stable adaptation after a change of colleagues and objectives. Technical progress since these particular releases is not exhaustively benchmarked here; these are selected mechanism tests, not a claim about the latest frontier.
5. **What counts as understanding.** Original Polanyi text, Tsoukas’s critique, and Collins’s explanation expose real differences in the criterion. Neither a benchmark nor an assertion of ineffability resolves them. The book can distinguish operational adequacy from philosophical identity without making a final metaphysical commitment.
6. **Whose knowledge and whose losses.** Existing capsules on transfer and changing intermediary roles do not show who should own resulting models, who should be compensated for demonstrations, or whose confidentiality may be waived. Those are institutional, legal, and authorial questions; R-15 remains necessary.

Evidence that would change this review includes robust performance gains from equal-information machine participants across novel institutional settings; demonstrated failures that persist after relevant information and interaction are supplied; longitudinal comparisons of recording regimes; and evidence that consent-compatible acquisition is either effective or prohibitively limited. No outcome is preselected. An important stopping boundary is that an analytical review is not authorized to create a new workplace experiment, record people, or test private organizational data.

### Method, access limits, and research boundary

This is a targeted analytical review, not a preregistered systematic review or meta-analysis. Searches paired tacit-knowledge philosophy with conversion critiques, organizational learning, transfer failures and successes, demonstration learning, unequal observation, monitoring benefits and harms, and privacy constraints. Primary theoretical statements were preferred for interpretation; published organizational studies and public technical papers supplied empirical tests. Sources differ too much in unit, outcome, and identification for pooled effect estimates to be meaningful.

The technical sources were selected to test distinct mechanisms, not to provide an exhaustive October 2026 model ranking. Philosophy describes and argues about knowing; field studies estimate or characterize organizational behavior; controlled technical studies test specific learning setups; regulatory guidance specifies a bounded legal framework. None substitutes for the others.

No paid access, accounts, private sources, participant contact, or paywall bypass was used. Full-text source files and OCR were working aids, not intended for repository publication. Inaccessible books and articles were not counted as completed readings. The principal remaining uncertainty is the bridge from demonstrated bounded performance and classical situated-work mechanisms to current, long-horizon, consent-compatible institutional judgment. That bridge is specified for research rather than silently assumed.
