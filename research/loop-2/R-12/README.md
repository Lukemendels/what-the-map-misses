# R-12 Macro adjustment and distribution

**Research date:** 4 October 2026.  
**Status:** Substantial literature review accepted after independent quality review; R-11 accounting and R-01 demand-evidence reconciliation completed. Remote durability is verified separately after publication.  
**Program:** R-12; admitted claims C-025, C-040, C-041 and C-045; overlaps C-001, C-009, C-023–024, C-039 and C-044.  
**Authority:** [Loop 2](../../../loops/02-eighteen-literature-reviews.md). Research judgments do not revise the protected author corpus or adopt a thesis for the author. [D-002](../../../decisions/resolved/D-002-ownership-dominance.md) is the current, revisable author position.

## 1. Main conclusion and the claims under review

AI can improve particular production possibilities without ensuring rapid aggregate growth, higher wages for everyone, durable returns to every owner, or economy-wide deflation. These are separate propositions. The strongest literature explains why their relationships are conditional; it does not establish a universal sequence in which gains must pass from capital to workers and finally to consumers.

Two opposing mistakes would weaken the book. One is to multiply a striking task-level result by the entire economy. The other is to treat one conservative calibration of existing tasks as a bound on every possible future technology, product and research process. The evidence supports neither. Aggregate forecasts depend heavily on which tasks are affected, how firms adopt the technology, the costs that remain, capital accumulation, substitution, new activities and the response of demand. Distribution adds further questions about asset holdings, worker mobility, bargaining, market structure and policy.

C-025 is supported as its actual conditional proposition, with quantitative macro and distributional outcomes unresolved. Stronger conclusions tested below are identified as auxiliary inferences, not attributed to the current synthesis. The research therefore supports D-002's separation of productive labor activities from ownership and its refusal to promise compensation. It does not certify architectural work, education or consequential operation as permanently scarce. Nor does it prove that competition will eventually eliminate all rents. An economy can combine cheaper reproducible services with expensive bottlenecks, contested rents, displaced workers and rising aggregate output.

### Admitted propositions and provenance

- **C-025:** The current synthesis, §§7 and 16, proposes that bespoke software and abundant cognition might expand productive services or welfare without matching market revenue; it explicitly leaves the macroeconomic implications unsettled.
- **C-040:** The predecessor in `source/drafts/essay-5-seed-what-i-cant-see.md`, “The resolution,” made ownership a dominant strategy across alternative technological endpoints. D-002 has explicitly revised ownership-only dominance. The empirical question is what kinds of claims on returns remain valuable under which conditions.
- **C-041:** The registered proposition says no single model/workflow permanently differentiates its holder; repeat redesign, competition and unresolved rights condition realized value. Its categorical no-permanence prediction must be tested separately from the conditional capture mechanisms. It is not identical to a claim that every rent disappears.
- **C-045:** `source/drafts/cognitive-factory-floor.md`, §§1 and 5, asserts a capital → labor → consumer sequence, highly elastic cognitive demand, a compensation treadmill and a deflationary endpoint. These remain predecessor hypotheses, not the current synthesis's settled conclusions.

[R-18](../R-18/README.md) has already qualified the historical sequence and Ford wage/price analogy. This review supplies the economic mechanisms and contemporary evidence; it does not repeat the historical source capsules. The detailed accounting and welfare evidence belongs to [R-11](../R-11/README.md). Competition and firm-level capture belong principally to [R-05](../R-05/README.md), with rights and portability in R-15.

## 2. Keep the outcomes separate before aggregating them

The following distinctions organize the review.

| Quantity | What an increase or decrease does establish | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Task throughput or time saved | A change in a defined activity under its evaluation conditions | Quality-adjusted process productivity, retained learning, employment or GDP |
| Firm revenue | Receipts at observed prices and quantities | Social surplus, economic profit or aggregate value added |
| Real output and labor productivity | Output volume and output per hour within a specified boundary | TFP, a causal AI contribution or distribution across workers |
| TFP | Output growth beyond the measured contributions of included inputs | A pure technology measure unaffected by utilization, reallocation or measurement |
| Hourly pay, annual earnings and wage bill | Different compensation outcomes, with different hours/employment denominators | The same thing as one another or as the labor share |
| Labor share | Labor compensation relative to the chosen income/output measure | The level of wages, the median household's welfare or a partition of consumer surplus |
| Capital income and economic profit | Returns to assets, with profits requiring an explicit cost-of-capital definition | Guaranteed rents, returns to each investor or the value of all software |
| Consumption possibilities and welfare | Benefits under an explicit valuation and distributional framework | A number that can automatically be added to GDP |

BLS's production accounts distinguish capital services from asset expenditure and separate labor-productivity growth into TFP, capital intensity and labor composition. Its compensation measures also require imputation for the self-employed. Thus even carefully measured official aggregates are constructed quantities, not interchangeable observations of a single productivity effect. [R12-S19]

The accounting identity is especially useful. Write labor compensation as a share, s, of nominal value added, PᵧY, and divide by hours H and the consumption price index P꜀. Average real hourly compensation is then:

**s × (Y/H) × (Pᵧ/P꜀).**

This is an identity, not a theory of wage determination. It makes three possible movements visible: productivity, the compensation share, and workers' terms of trade between what is produced and what is consumed. It says nothing by itself about medians, job losses, hours per person, bargaining or causation. For illustration, if output per hour rises 40%, the labor share falls from 60% to 50%, and relative deflators are unchanged, average real compensation rises 16.7%. If output rises only 10% under the same share change, it falls 8.3%. These are this review's arithmetic examples, not forecasts.

A lower labor share consequently does not mechanically mean lower wages. Conversely, rising average wages do not establish that displaced people recovered their earnings or that inequality declined. This distinction is needed throughout C-040–045.

## 3. What the theoretical disagreement is actually about

### Task assignment changes the distribution of productive opportunities

Acemoglu and Restrepo distinguish automation, productivity improvements within existing tasks, and new labor-intensive tasks. Moving tasks from labor to capital creates displacement alongside cost savings; genuinely new tasks can reinstate labor. Their wage-bill decomposition also includes sectoral composition and substitution. The signs commonly summarized as “automation lowers labor share” and “new tasks raise it” belong to their specified task-allocation model, including adoption and cost-minimization assumptions. Their historical decomposition infers task-content changes partly from residual labor-share movements; it is not a direct census of disappearing tasks. [R12-S01]

This framework changes how the book's four D-002 positions should be evaluated. Architecture, training and operation are economically meaningful if they improve valued outcomes, create useful activities, or relieve a constraint. Calling them complementary does not establish their compensation. A firm can need less human time per service even when the remaining human is more capable. A new job title may describe a relabeled old task; a new task may be largely machine-intensive; a nominal approval may create responsibility without productive discretion. Each must be measured rather than counted as automatic reinstatement.

There is also no contradiction in a tool both complementing and substituting for labor. It can help one worker complete a task, replace another subtask, expand an employer's output and draw customers from competing firms. “Augmentation” measured inside the treated workplace does not fix the aggregate sign. The relevant counterfactual includes untreated firms, customers, suppliers, new entrants and workers who do not get hired.

### Capital supply separates optimistic and pessimistic wage results

Caselli and Manning provide a serious optimistic benchmark. With constant returns, competitive markets, labor as the only fixed factor, unchanged interest rates and comparison of steady states, technical improvement cannot make every worker type worse off. Average wages rise under their additional relative-investment-price condition. This permits severe distributional losses, and the authors examine failures under other fixed factors, changing market power, interest rates and transitions. The theorem is a conditional result, not an empirical guarantee that adjustment is painless. [R12-S02]

Moll, Rachel and Restrepo deliberately relax an important part of that benchmark. Their heterogeneous-household model has upward-sloping long-run capital supply and wealth-dissipation risk. Automation can then raise returns to wealth while limiting capital deepening and depressing exposed wages. Their extended model distinguishes risky capital from safe bonds; falling safe yields do not identify the relevant return to investors. The quantitative exercise calibrates automation partly to the historical labor-share decline. Matching inequality patterns therefore does not independently prove that automation caused that entire decline. [R12-S03]

These papers should be compared by assumptions, not scored as mutually exclusive predictions. If capital expands cheaply and sufficiently, complementary labor can share in a larger production scale. If investment is constrained, fixed factors capture gains, or market power changes, that adjustment differs. Workers with different capabilities and asset positions need not experience the same result. Neither framework supplies an empirically established AI transition path for the next decade.

### New tasks and research can change growth, but neither is an automatic rescue

Aghion, Benjamin Jones and Charles Jones show why automating goods production and automating the discovery of ideas are different mechanisms. Essential complementary tasks can retain expenditure weight as other activities become cheap; under some assumptions this prevents an explosion in aggregate growth. Other specifications permit sustained acceleration or mathematical singularities. Their sectoral evidence does not cleanly identify automation as the cause of changing capital shares. The chapter is a collection of conditional models and research directions, not an estimated arrival date for abundance. [R12-S04]

Korinek and Suh examine the edge case more directly. Their March 2024 scenarios vary the distribution of task complexity and the speed of automation relative to capital accumulation. Bounded versus sufficiently thick unbounded task distributions produce sharply different wage paths. Fixed factors and automated research alter them again. Importantly, the baseline's “wage collapse” means loss of labor's scarcity premium toward the model's capital-equivalent return; it is not a demonstrated fall to literal zero. The AGI dates and growth paths are illustrative assumptions, not estimated probabilities. [R12-S05]

The point for D-002 is exact. Evidence that current human redesign or judgment helps cannot disprove a hypothetical endpoint defined to eliminate all economically distinctive human work. Equally, a model that assumes such an endpoint cannot establish that it will arrive. Current skill evidence, capability forecasts and institutional choices belong in separate columns. New demand protects human employment only insofar as meeting it still requires human contributions or creates new ones.

A recent contrary quantitative contribution also deserves notice. Autor and Kausik's January 2026 preprint uses a competitive constant-returns framework and CES-based fits for twelve economies to argue that lower labor shares can increase average wages. Its empirical exercise is historical model fitting, not an exogenous AI experiment; the authors exclude rent-extraction explanations from the optimistic interpretation. This review accepts the possibility result but does not treat its estimated wage-maximizing shares as validated policy targets. Functional-form, capital-measurement and normalization sensitivity require independent replication. [R12-S25]

## 4. Aggregation requires demand and the whole production network

### The expanding volume of cognition is not the same as expanding human employment

Bessen's demand model explains how automation can initially expand industry employment and later reduce it as demand becomes less elastic. It fits long historical series for cotton, steel and automobiles, while acknowledging substitutes, trade, new products and other omitted influences. These fits illustrate why elasticity may change; they do not identify the present price elasticity of useful AI services. He explicitly notes that if an occupation is completely automated, more demand need not restore its human employment. [R12-S06]

The predecessor's Jevons inference therefore skips several steps. A lower effective price can increase the number of AI-assisted analyses, but human hours equal completed services multiplied by human hours per service. The number of services can rise while the latter product falls. Demand for machine inference, demand for a finished service, and demand for the worker supplying its remaining tasks are different demands. Their price and income elasticities also differ. The relevant detailed cost definitions are developed in R-01; token counts are not a sufficient measure. Its [demand review](../R-01/README.md#6-demand-expansion-is-conditional) also examines competing provider-level inference-demand estimates. Substitution among providers cannot by itself identify economy-wide demand expansion or demand for human labor.

A simple illustration makes the uncertainty concrete. Suppose a service's human requirement halves while its selling price falls by only 20%, because other costs remain. Under a constant price elasticity ε, unchanged income and otherwise identical conditions, employment in that service is multiplied by 0.5 × 0.8^(−ε). Employment rises only if ε exceeds about 3.11. This is not an estimated AI elasticity or a general-equilibrium model. It demonstrates why “elastic demand” alone is too imprecise to imply a wage or employment outcome. Competition, pass-through, new applications and reallocation change this calculation.

Nor does perfect competition force a fixed compensation treadmill. In a competitive model, equilibrium pay responds to marginal products, supplies, opportunities and output prices; it is not fixed by a story about a manager raising expectations. Actual organizations can impose work intensification, share rents or negotiate shorter hours. Those are empirical and institutional questions, not consequences of the word “Jevons.”

### Bottlenecks can attenuate or redirect gains

Baqaee and Farhi distinguish Hulten's first-order aggregation result for efficient economies from larger changes with endogenous production-network weights. Substitution elasticities, network position and factor reallocation affect higher-order responses. Their calibrated examples show why a sector's sales share alone is insufficient for large shocks. This does not invalidate first-order accounting; it identifies its scope. Their estimates concern historical production networks, not a direct measurement of an AI multiplier. [R12-S07]

Acemoglu, Autor and Patterson study imbalances in upstream productivity using 462 U.S. manufacturing industries, with 42 additional nonmanufacturing industries in the broader analysis. Supplier-productivity dispersion predicts weaker downstream growth. Their international-instrument strategy addresses common-shock concerns only imperfectly; first stages are weak, and the proposed historical counterfactual remains model-dependent. The evidence makes complementary bottlenecks economically serious without proving that every AI failure is a workflow-design failure. [R12-S08]

For this book, three different constraints should not be merged:

1. **Within a process:** a faster draft leaves verification, authorization, user acceptance or execution unchanged.
2. **Across suppliers and sectors:** compute, electricity, construction, skilled installation or other inputs must expand alongside AI services.
3. **In innovation itself:** a design or hypothesis still needs validation, manufacturing, deployment and demand.

The constraints may be removable, partly substitutable or costly to relax. Calling them bottlenecks is a question about the feasible production system, not evidence of an eternal human role. A cheap model can make a scarce complement more valuable, but the same technological change may later remove that scarcity. Its owner can receive rents; its worker can receive wages; its regulator may constrain access. These are different mechanisms.

An arithmetic check is useful before any aggregate extrapolation. If a stage taking 20% of unchanged process time becomes twice as fast, total time falls to 90%, so throughput rises 11.1%, not 100%. This serial-process example assumes no overlap, queueing or redesign. It is not a macro model, but it exposes a common denominator error. Redesign may improve the other stages too; that additional claim needs evidence.

## 5. Comparing the major quantitative forecasts

These studies do not estimate one common treatment. Their output concepts, horizons, technological scopes and adoption rules differ. The numbers below are their conditional results, not this review's forecast or a range with statistical coverage.

### Existing-task cost-saving calibrations

**Acemoglu, published 2025.** The inspected advance-published article yields **0.66% cumulative TFP over ten years**, or **0.53%** after its easy/hard-task adjustment. Its baseline combines 20% exposure, a 23% economically feasible share, and 27% labor-cost savings discounted by an exposure-weighted labor share of 0.535. GDP is **0.93–1.16%** higher with its modest-investment benchmark, versus **1.4–1.56%** with its stronger capital response. These are level differences, not annual growth rates. Hard-task savings, adoption transfer and capital adjustment are assumptions. Its proposed upper bounds concern this mechanism and parameterization, not arbitrary future innovation. [R12-S09]

**OECD G7, June 2025.** The United States' projected addition to annual labor-productivity growth is **0.41, 0.99 or 1.28 percentage points** over the next decade across its three scenarios. These combine task exposure, a **30% micro-level TFP gain**, country/sector composition and S-shaped adoption paths informed by earlier technologies. The paper explicitly recognizes an alternative interpretation: experimental gains may be labor-cost savings requiring a labor-share discount. Its central and high scenarios also use expanded capability through complementary software. Cross-country adoption harmonization remains incomplete, and the equilibrium comparison omits transition costs. These are scenario outputs, not confidence intervals. [R12-S10]

**Penn Wharton Budget Model, September 2025.** PWBM projects TFP/GDP levels **1.5% higher by 2035**, roughly **3% by 2055**, and **3.7% by 2075**, with a peak annual contribution near **0.2 percentage point in 2032**. It estimates 40% exposure, retains a 23% feasibility assumption, projects labor-cost savings from 25% toward 40%, and models diffusion. Its appendix attributes part of its difference from Acemoglu to exposure-category mapping. That is PWBM's documented interpretation; this review has not independently replicated the final underlying classification. Quality changes, new products/tasks and innovation effects are excluded. [R12-S11]

These forecasts retain their own source vintages. Later micro-study revisions reviewed in R-05/R-09/R-10 cannot be substituted into an earlier calculation without recomputing it.

These three comparisons identify an important disagreement about *units*. A worker finishing faster is not automatically evidence that every input needed to produce the output falls proportionally. On the other hand, if enabling that worker also saves capital, space or downstream resources, discounting only by labor's share can miss benefits. Resolving the dispute requires full-process resource and quality measurement, not selecting the preferred aggregate headline.

Another critical assumption lies upstream. Svanberg and colleagues' **23%** is a modeled currently cost-attractive share of **vision-task compensation at firm-level deployment**, not a measured ten-year adoption rate for language models. Their original study combines task surveys, required performance, engineering/training/maintenance costs, and firm scale; it also examines platform aggregation and declining costs. Thirty-three unsurveyed tasks use imputed mean requirements. Transferring this number to general-purpose language assistance is a substantive extrapolation, which Acemoglu acknowledges. [R12-S26]

Exposure estimates themselves require version discipline. The accessible August 2023 Eloundou and colleagues manuscript rates potential time savings at a specified quality level, using O*NET descriptions and human/GPT-4 judgments; it does not estimate adoption timing. It is a predecessor to the 2024 *Science* publication. This review inspected its rubric and limitations but could not retrieve the final supplementary classification through the publisher. It therefore does not silently substitute its early exposure percentages for those used in the later calibrations or declare PWBM's critique independently settled. [R12-S27]

### Broader potential and distribution scenarios

**Goldman Sachs, March/April 2023.** Its public official account presents a potential **7% global GDP level gain** and an approximately **1.5-percentage-point annual U.S. labor-productivity boost during a ten-year adoption period**, conditional on capability and diffusion. The frequently repeated 300-million figure is exposure in full-time-job equivalents, not a measured or predicted net layoff count. The full original report was not accessible from the official report host during this review; the substantive public account supports headline comparison, not independent replication of its model. It is commercial research with a clear interest in the technology's investment implications. [R12-S12]

**IMF staff, January 2024.** The U.K.-calibrated distribution model is especially informative about assumptions: it imposes a **5.5-percentage-point labor-share decline** and varies complementarity/productivity. The high-productivity scenario is calibrated to approximately **1.5 percentage points of annual labor-productivity growth** in the initial decade. Its broad income gains and increased wealth inequality are conditional model outcomes, not independent evidence for those assumed magnitudes. Heterogeneous skills/assets and restricted investment access matter. The comparison is between steady states; the note's exposure indices and historical worker transitions do not identify AI's causal employment effect. [R12-S13]

**AGI scenarios** address a different question from these diffusion exercises. R12-S04 and S05 explore what happens when technology changes the feasible task set or the innovation process radically. They expose omissions from a fixed-task forecast, but their strong results inherit strong technological and production assumptions. They should not be placed beside a ten-year cost-saving calibration as if both were noisy estimates of the same variable.

### What would make a forecast comparison fair

A recoverable forecast should state:

- Baseline year, geography, technological scope and counterfactual
- Cumulative level effect versus annual percentage-point growth contribution
- Labor productivity versus TFP versus GDP, and treatment of capital accumulation
- Exposure rubric, economic feasibility and adoption intensity, with separate denominators
- Task-quality treatment and full adjustment, oversight, maintenance and complementary costs
- New-product and research channels, including whether they are omitted or assumed
- Labor-supply, capital-supply, substitution and reallocation assumptions
- Nominal-price regime and distributional structure

No average of the listed forecasts meets these requirements. Neither publication prestige nor recency removes the need to inspect the mapping. Many share task-exposure and micro-study inputs, so their agreement would not constitute fully independent corroboration.

## 6. Empirical evidence that constrains the models

### Industrial automation gives different answers at different levels

Graetz and Michaels study 17 countries and 14 robot-using industries over 1993–2007. Robot adoption is associated with productivity gains; their counterfactual attributes about **0.36 percentage point of annual labor-productivity growth** to increasing robot density. They find lower output prices and no statistically significant total-hours reduction, alongside adverse low-skill employment-share effects. Instruments use historical task replaceability and reaching/handling. They acknowledge exclusion concerns and that the aggregate calculation does not model all cross-industry spillovers. A statistically imprecise hours estimate is not proof of zero displacement. [R12-S14]

Acemoglu and Restrepo study U.S. commuting-zone exposure using earlier industry composition and European robot diffusion. Their local estimates imply about **0.39 percentage point lower employment/population and 0.77% lower wages per additional robot per thousand workers**. Their smaller aggregate estimates, **0.2 percentage point and 0.42%**, require additional trade/spillover assumptions. Pretrend and other-shock checks support the design but cannot make European adoption exogenous to every global industry shock. The distinction between local estimates and modeled aggregation is explicit in the paper and essential here. [R12-S15]

Autor and Salomons use 19 countries and 28 industries, principally 1970–2007, tracing productivity changes through own-industry, input-output, composition and demand channels. Their indirect employment gains offset direct losses, while labor-share losses are not similarly reversed. Technology is proxied through productivity, including common cross-country variation and alternative measures; that does not isolate one technology. The framework also fails to explain the acceleration of labor-share decline in the 2000s. It supplies evidence for offsets, not a universal compensation theorem. [R12-S16]

These findings differ in geography, technology, outcome, identification and aggregation. The international industry result need not cancel the U.S. local result. A productive sector can reduce prices, gain market share and employ more people while another location loses work; an aggregate can recover jobs without restoring earnings to the same individuals. None of these robot coefficients can responsibly be multiplied by the number of AI agents or tokens.

### New work exists, but its beneficiaries change

Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller construct new-title measures from Census occupational coding volumes, link them to Census/ACS employment and patent-text measures, and analyze 1940–2018. New work's location shifts toward professional and lower-paid service work after 1980. The often-cited approximately 60% of employment in post-1940 specialties is an imputation from title shares within broader occupations, not a direct count showing that technology caused 60% of all jobs. New titles can reflect demand and social changes as well as invention. Patent matching and instruments do not capture every innovation or eliminate all endogenous demand. [R12-S17]

This is consequential counterevidence to both a fixed stock of jobs and an automatic rescue story. Economic activity is not restricted to today's task list. But a new occupation elsewhere, requiring different skills and appearing years later, does not compensate a particular displaced worker. D-001's developmental concern and R-09's institutional labor evidence matter to who can move into new work; their existence is not established by an aggregate employment count.

### Wage compression can signal lost rents rather than universal leveling up

Acemoglu and Restrepo's November 2025 rent-dissipation manuscript studies U.S. demographic groups over 1980–2016 using task-displacement measures and several worker-rent proxies. In their model, firms can automate high-wage tasks partly to avoid rents rather than because the replacement creates comparably large social savings. The evidence connects exposed groups with lost high-rent positions and compressed within-group wages; the general-equilibrium magnitude remains dependent on measured task displacement, wage-premium interpretation and model structure. This is a warning against treating smaller wage gaps as necessarily beneficial or every payroll saving as an equal resource saving. [R12-S18]

Contemporary generative-AI evidence remains more local and short-horizon. See [R-09's assisted-work capsule](../R-09/README.md#ai-mediated-transfer-and-its-limits), [R-10's experimental comparisons](../R-10/README.md#6-direct-ai-evidence-on-productive-breadth-and-its-limits), and [R-05's March 2026 Danish work/pay comparison](../R-05/README.md#the-danish-test-work-changes-before-pay). The updated descriptive junior-employment evidence is assessed in [R-09](../R-09/README.md#53-contemporary-ai-labor-evidence-changing-tasks-is-not-yet-a-settled-wage-law). These designs should not be combined into a pooled macro treatment effect. No source in this set jointly identifies technological capability, economy-wide adoption, equilibrium prices, lifetime earnings and household welfare.

### Official data show diffusion and investment without identifying an AI growth dividend

Census's May 2026 BTOS account reports **17–20%** of businesses using AI during December 2025–May 2026. Crucially, the question expanded in November 2025 from use in producing goods/services to use in any business function. The later level cannot be directly spliced onto the narrower earlier series as technological acceleration. Firm counts also differ from employment weighting and from the fraction of work affected. [R12-S22]

BLS's May 2026 investment analysis reports software investment growing **11.1% annually during 2019–2024**. Software is broader than AI, the period begins before the recent GenAI rollout, and an investment classification is not causal attribution. The September 3, 2026 productivity release reports **2.2% year-over-year nonfarm-business labor-productivity growth in 2026 Q2** and **−0.1% real hourly compensation growth**. These observations illustrate that the measured outcomes can diverge; they do not isolate AI's contribution or establish a permanent distributional change. [R12-S20–S21]

## 7. Owners workers and consumers can gain at the same time

The predecessor's compulsory surplus-phase narrative treats overlapping positions as consecutive eras, a restriction the evidence does not establish. The same person can earn wages, own retirement assets, rent housing, pay for electricity and consume cheaper digital services. A firm may obtain temporary rents from an innovation, lose them through entry, and continue earning a normal return on productive assets. A consumer can gain immediately while particular producers retain market power. No conservation rule requires consumer surplus to wait until producer profits disappear.

The predecessor also moves too quickly between welfare surplus and factor income. A wage bill is not the same as worker surplus after the opportunity cost of labor; accounting capital income is not synonymous with economic profit; consumer surplus is not a component to add mechanically to GDP. A careful welfare calculation can allocate benefits across participants, but it needs consistent costs, prices and counterfactuals. It cannot infer a migration of a single measured pool simply because these labels sound like a partition. The relevant valuation limitations are in [R-11](../R-11/README.md#6-welfare-beyond-revenue-and-the-limits-of-valuation).

Three accounts are needed.

**Owners.** Asset returns depend on acquisition price, depreciation, maintenance, finance, competition and control rights. Returns to reproducible compute need not match rents to scarce power connections, land, data access, distribution or organizational assets. A useful technology can benefit society while a particular investment loses money. A higher capital-income share also does not demonstrate an increase in economic profit after the cost of capital. The relevant rights and market mechanisms are analyzed in R-05 and R-15.

**Workers.** Changes in demand, task assignment, bargaining and mobility affect hourly pay, access to jobs and lifetime earnings. Architects and educators can be paid without receiving residual ownership. Consequential operators can improve outcomes without enough bargaining power to capture the corresponding gain. Conversely, worker rents or collective arrangements can produce compensation exceeding a simple replacement-cost calculation. These channels are compatible with D-002 but do not guarantee that every role remains valuable as capability changes.

**Consumers.** Gains depend on quality-adjusted prices, access, budget shares and the value of new services. Someone whose income falls can enjoy a cheaper product yet have fewer overall consumption possibilities. People excluded by connectivity, language, skills or institutional rules may receive less benefit. Prices can also shift toward scarce complements that different households consume in different proportions. A gain to an average consumer cannot settle the distributional question.

The retired Ford draft's actual AI forecast is that a middle-seat job at a well-run company will **likely** fund a good life, with **probably** rising living standards. That is weaker than a guarantee. It remains **unresolved**, because the evidence does not identify a probability for that employer-defined group or specify the income, household, location, hours and security needed for a “good life.” Refuting an unconditional guarantee does not refute this conditional possibility.

The general-equilibrium models help map these channels, but aggregation can hide the transition. A prediction that a final steady state permits compensation is not evidence that compensation is paid. Taxes, transfers, social insurance, public provision, bargaining institutions, access policies and forms of broad asset ownership can change incidence. Whether to favor any of them, how to trade off investment and redistribution, and what counts as an acceptable loss are normative author choices. This review does not select them.

The review also rejects a false binary between growth and distribution. Depending on the mechanism, a policy can change both the size and allocation of gains. Removing an inefficient bottleneck differs from redistributing a fixed rent; supporting training differs from preserving every obsolete task; taxing a scarce rent differs from taxing an additional unit of productive investment. Their effects need policy-specific evidence rather than a blanket claim that competition or ownership solves distribution.

## 8. Relative price declines do not establish general deflation

A technical improvement can lower the resource cost of a service and its price relative to other goods. **Disinflation** means a slower increase in an aggregate price index. **Deflation** means that index falls. A lower price level relative to a no-AI counterfactual can coexist with positive observed inflation. These statements are not equivalent.

The long-run nominal path also depends on monetary institutions. The FOMC's January 2026 reaffirmed strategy targets **2% PCE inflation**, rather than a mechanically falling price level as productivity improves. This is a stated policy framework, not a guarantee that every year hits its target. [R12-S24]

AI investment can initially increase demand for inputs before completed systems expand supply. Expected future income and asset values can also affect current spending. Cook's September 2026 speech explicitly distinguishes sectoral price pressure, broader investment/wealth effects and conditional future disinflation. It is a policymaker's assessment, not a causal decomposition of inflation. Its usefulness is to expose the omitted demand and timing channels, not to substitute authority for identification. [R12-S23]

A supply-frontier claim should therefore be conditional: if a technology makes valued, quality-adjusted output feasible at lower full resource cost, it expands possibilities holding other conditions fixed. Realized output may respond later or less; measured GDP may differ from welfare; distribution may be unequal; nominal prices depend on demand and policy. Calling the invention a positive supply shock does not establish that every observed macro variable will move favorably.

An additional consistency check matters for the predecessor. If cognition demand expands so strongly that spending and resource demand surge, that demand response cannot simultaneously be ignored when predicting aggregate deflation. The two mechanisms can coexist, but their relative size and timing must be specified. A particular input's cheapness does not decide the aggregate price index.

## 9. Reconciliation with accounting welfare and the historical review

[R-11's boundary analysis](../R-11/README.md#3-the-production-boundary-is-not-the-cash-register) prevents a central mistake: absence of a software sale does not establish absence from measured production. Paid own-account creation, commercial open-source activity and unpaid independent volunteering require different treatment. Its source-version distinction between the adopted 2025 SNA and actual national implementation also matters. Its distinction between gross construction and durable net accumulation also prevents an investment boom from being counted as an equal welfare gain. The macro review should not turn a changed international standard into an assertion that current U.S. accounts already implement every recommendation. [R-11](../R-11/README.md)

Three consequences follow for C-025.

First, a builder can create useful services outside a conventional software market without proving that the whole activity is omitted from GDP. The omitted component must be identified, valued and bounded. Second, replacing an expensive purchase with cheaper own production can reduce one seller's revenue without determining real aggregate output after reallocation. Third, replacement cost, capital stock, annual capital services and consumer welfare cannot be summed as separate additions to annual GDP.

R-11's [mismeasurement review](../R-11/README.md#7-the-mismeasurement-debate-is-a-debate-about-magnitudes-and-boundaries) adds that valuable unpriced services do not by themselves explain a measured growth slowdown. A high benefit level and an accelerating omitted growth contribution are different hypotheses. Benefits may appear downstream in a user's measured output, and imported inputs separate domestic production from domestic consumption gains.

The implication is a measurement program rather than a missing-trillions assertion. Track the resources committed, maintenance and failure, who uses the result, which purchased services it replaces, which new activity it enables, and the accounting boundary. Measure quality and user benefit separately, including errors, lost privacy, fraud and other external costs where relevant. Additional paid activity can be defensive or harmful rather than a net welfare improvement; a claim about useful output must not count all generated activity as equally desirable. A price of zero is neither proof of no value nor evidence of infinite welfare.

R-18 adds the parallel historical discipline. Its [Ford/surplus review](../R-18/ford-surplus.md) does not support a compulsory capital/labor/consumer calendar, a universal prosperity guarantee or general deflation inferred from a cheaper manufactured product. Its [buildout review](../R-18/buildout.md) separates investment intensity from realized technological benefit and financial return. R-12's theoretical and empirical findings reinforce those distinctions without treating history as an AI forecast.

## 11. Investigated gaps and dependencies

### Gaps that remain after examining the evidence

1. **Representative full-cost effects.** The micro studies underlying the forecasts concentrate on selected tasks and organizations. Full production costs, error consequences, maintenance, complementary investment and output quality remain incompletely measured. R-01/R-02/R-04/R-13 supply the relevant task and system tests.
2. **Price elasticities and pass-through.** Historical demand models and official price data do not establish a stable elasticity for useful cognitive services or its incidence on human hours. Platform-level inference demand would still require a separate bridge to final-service demand. R-01 investigates directly conflicting provider-level estimates and owns that evidence capsule; neither sign settles long-run aggregate useful-task elasticity.
3. **Forecast-input replication.** The original vision-cost assumption was inspected, but transferring it to language assistance is unvalidated. PWBM's final exposure-category disagreement was recovered; the final *Science* supplement was blocked. The older arXiv paper is not a silent replacement. Replicating mappings and sensitivity is an explicit remaining task, not a completed check.
4. **Capability paths and new work.** Current task taxonomies cannot determine future products or whether their production is human-intensive. Historical new-title emergence does not estimate future reinstatement. AGI scenarios identify conditions rather than probabilities.
5. **Capital and distribution.** Models disagree materially about long-run capital supply, asset returns and wealth dynamics. Household balance sheets, depreciation and scarce-factor exposure need to be linked to actual AI adoption. This review did not estimate those elasticities anew.
6. **Transition and geography.** Evidence is concentrated in advanced economies, particularly the United States. U.K. calibrations and historical robot panels do not settle informal labor, international service relocation, capital ownership across borders, or low-income-country incidence. The global modeling gap remains real.
7. **Causal macro attribution.** Official adoption questions have changed, AI software overlaps older assets, and aggregate productivity includes many simultaneous shocks. A credible design must not infer causation from coincident trend changes or investment announcements.
8. **Policy effects.** The reviewed mechanisms show why distribution can change; they do not compare all feasible tax, insurance, ownership, training, competition or working-time policies. Choosing an objective and a feasible policy package is not a missing coefficient to be filled in by the researcher.

These gaps do not prevent rejecting categorical inferences. They do prevent assigning a confident aggregate growth, wage, rent, price-level or welfare path.

### Reconciliation gates

- **R-11:** retain its production-boundary, own-account, price/volume, asset-service and welfare distinctions. No replacement-value total becomes annual missing GDP.
- **R-18:** retain the qualified historical findings; investment magnitude and memorable cases do not establish a macro law.
- **R-05/R-15:** distinguish creating value, bargaining for compensation, owning rights and sustaining rents.
- **R-08/R-09/R-10:** retained learning, junior recruitment, senior stocks and productive breadth affect adjustment but are not aggregate wage estimates.
- **R-01/R-02/R-04/R-13:** feed demonstrated, quality-constrained process gains and adoption constraints into macro interpretation, rather than benchmark speed or prototype output alone.
- **R-03:** reconcile the book's workflow language with formal task and production concepts. “Supply shock,” “surplus,” “labor” and “capital” should not change meanings between chapters.

### Author questions after research

The evidence does not require choosing either technological pessimism or optimism. It does require abandoning guarantees unsupported by the models and data. The author must decide whether the book should frame the macro implications as explicit competing scenarios, emphasize institutions that shape incidence, or limit itself primarily to organizational mechanisms. The preferred distribution of gains, acceptable transition losses and desired balance of income, leisure and agency remain normative choices. No choice is made here on Luke's behalf.

## 13. Method limitations and completion boundary

This is a targeted analytical literature review, not a preregistered systematic review or meta-analysis. Sources were selected to expose disagreements in mechanisms, magnitudes and identification, including optimistic competitive benchmarks, unequal-growth models, task displacement, new work, demand saturation and production-network constraints. Public primary papers and official measurements were prioritized. Abstracts and search snippets were discovery tools, not substitutes for the substantive text used in the main synthesis.

The study compares peer-reviewed articles, accepted/working manuscripts, institutional scenarios, administrative/industry empirical work and official definitions. They carry different evidentiary weight. No numerical model was reestimated; no private microdata, paid dataset or API was obtained; no account was created or paywall bypassed; no third party was contacted. Public access failures and incomplete version comparisons are explicit. Individual papers' causal claims are reported alongside their assumptions rather than adopted by default.

The review's conclusions are strongest on invalid inference, outcome distinctions and conditional mechanisms. Its weakest areas are the future capability distribution, sector-specific adoption and elasticity, general-equilibrium transitions, and household welfare incidence in underrepresented economies. Completion of this review means those questions have been investigated and bounded, not that uncertainty has been eliminated. Publication, semantic quality review, cross-program reconciliation and remote durability are separate project gates.
