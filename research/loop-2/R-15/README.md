# R-15 — Residual rights and experiential assets

**Research date:** 4 October 2026.  
**Scope:** C-034, C-037, C-038, C-039, C-040, C-041 and C-050.  
**Status:** substantial analytical literature review accepted after independent quality review; coordinated publication checkpoint. Research findings are not author adoption or a canonical thesis.  
**Jurisdiction:** the legal discussion is a bounded United States illustration, with California provisions expressly identified. It is not advice on any person's employment, intellectual property, privacy or licensing agreement.  
**Reading convention:** R15-S01–S26 resolve to the bibliography/access appendix. Relative repository links assume publication as `research/loop-2/R-15/README.md`.

## 1. Result: the asset and the right must be specified separately

The literature makes the ownership question more precise without producing a universal answer to it. A productive history can alter the value of a relationship; control over complementary resources can alter bargaining; and contracts can influence incentives to develop reusable capabilities. None of those propositions establishes that a worker, employer or model provider owns everything that an AI system acquires during work. Nor does the ability to duplicate a file establish permission to copy it, permission to use it elsewhere, or preservation of its productive value.

The main result is a four-part separation:

1. **Technical reproducibility:** which state can actually be exported, duplicated or reconstructed?
2. **Legal permission:** which parties may copy, modify, disclose, license or deploy each component, for which purposes?
3. **Operational portability:** can the exported components function with the destination's models, tools, records and permissions?
4. **Economic portability:** how much useful performance survives outside the original worker, team, firm and customer relationships?

These are this review's analytical tests, not four results already demonstrated for persistent AI counterparts. They prevent a definition of “experiential machine capital” from silently containing the conclusions that research is supposed to establish.

The strongest supported proposition is C-039: making or operating productive machinery is distinct from holding its legal rights or capturing its returns. The strongest unresolved propositions are the scale, separability and transferable performance of the future assets in C-034 and C-037. C-038 correctly identifies a consequential problem; research supports the relevance of the question, not a particular allocation. The predecessor ownership-dominance claim in C-040 has already been revised by [D-002](../../../decisions/resolved/D-002-ownership-dominance.md). This review supplies no reason to restore it as an unconditional prescription.

A second result concerns the human analogy. Human beings retain their minds when they leave an employer, but the economic value of everything they learned need not travel, and confidentiality obligations do not disappear because information is remembered. Conversely, a firm's control over an AI-related repository need not give it the departing person's judgment, cooperation, customer relationships or future improvements. The book can examine how these boundaries move without claiming they have vanished.

## 2. Exact corpus questions and limits of this review

The [historical claim ledger](../../../evidence/claims/CLAIM-LEDGER.md) and [current synthesis §§10–13](../../../source/synthesis/2026-09-11-current-author-synthesis.md) preserve several deliberately conditional formulations:

- **C-034:** potentially valuable learned histories **may** be copyable, transferable, leased or forked. This is a proposed category whose properties remain open, not an assertion that all learned capability is a freely alienable asset.
- **C-037:** accumulated human–machine history **could** form relational capital and an executable résumé. The hypothesis concerns capability and demonstration, not a presently standardized credential.
- **C-038:** joint worker–firm experience raises **unsettled questions** about residual control, departure, confidentiality and separable capability. The author asks whether either or both parties can retain a copy; he does not allocate title.
- **C-039:** creating or operating valuable workflow machinery does not itself determine ownership or surplus capture.
- **C-040:** the earlier parked seed presents ownership as the robust winner in two hypothetical worlds. D-002 expressly revises that dominance formulation.
- **C-041:** the corpus denies permanent differentiation from one model/workflow and emphasizes redesign, competition and unresolved rights. The word **permanently** is stronger than evidence about ordinary erosion or conditional durability.
- **C-050:** the earlier [cognitive-factory-floor draft](../../../source/drafts/cognitive-factory-floor.md), especially §§2–5, calls no-code a proprietary transitional trap and presents raw code plus role renegotiation as an escape. That combines a technological comparison, a contractual proposition and a compensation prediction; they require different tests.

D-002 distinguishes architectural work, tool-use education, consequential operation and ownership. It also separates productive contribution from remuneration. These are current, revisable author positions. The following comparison of control designs does not choose which distribution of rights Luke should endorse.

R-14 must establish what kinds of durable machine adaptation actually exist and at what cost. R-17 must test whether relationship history yields performance beyond the components. R-05 examines competitive capture and R-12 its distributional consequences. R-15 concentrates on the intervening question: **what precisely could be controlled, by whom, under which rules, and with what incentive effects?**

## 3. What is the proposed asset?

“An AI counterpart” is a user-facing unit, but it is not necessarily one economic or legal object. A useful inventory distinguishes at least the following:

| Component | Candidate productive function | What cannot be inferred from possession |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation-model access or weights | General capability | Ownership of upstream rights; continuing service; permission for every use |
| Fine-tuned weights or adapters | Task or domain adaptation | Standalone operation; clean separation from restricted inputs; exclusive title |
| Prompts, workflows and executable code | Sequencing and transformation | Ownership of all dependencies or workplace-created contributions |
| Retrieval corpus, examples and evaluation sets | Local knowledge and correction | Permission to disclose customer, coworker or employer information |
| Interaction records and personal preferences | Continuity and personalization | That every record is the worker's to export or useful to another operator |
| Tool connections and operational authorizations | Access to consequential systems | Transferability of credentials, delegated authority or customer consent |
| Human skill, team coordination and mutual expectations | Interpretation, adaptation and cooperation | That these are fully represented by a machine-readable copy |
| Reputation and demonstrated work history | Information for hiring or contracting | Universal validity, authenticity or portability of the associated credential |

The table is a researcher-derived decomposition, not a declaration of legal ownership. Several components may be governed by multiple rights simultaneously. A firm may possess a server, license a model, own copyright in some human-authored code, owe confidentiality duties to customers, and give an employee revocable operational authority. Calling the whole arrangement “firm-owned AI” is convenient only if these qualifications remain recoverable.

Specificity must also be relationally indexed. A capability can be worker-specific, team-specific, firm-specific, occupation-specific or dependent on a particular provider. Its historical origin does not settle its alternative uses. The human-capital evidence and skill-weights interpretation are reviewed in [R-09 §2](../R-09/README.md#2-what-is-specific-and-to-what); they should not be reintroduced as an assumption that all workplace learning is economically stranded upon departure.

Finally, **a stock of useful experience, an accounting asset and an enforceable property right are different categories**. R-11 supplies the accounting boundary. Something can have productive value without being separately recognized on a balance sheet; being recorded as capital would not by itself settle an employment or licensing dispute. R-03 must reconcile the book's economic terminology rather than allow legal personhood, productive work and formal labor/capital classifications to slide into one another.

## 4. What the theories establish, and where their assumptions stop

### 4.1 Incomplete contracts: control changes incentives, not only the final division

Grossman and Hart distinguish specified contractual rights from residual control. In their two-period model, ownership changes bargaining after noncontractible investments and therefore incentives beforehand. Integration can strengthen one party's incentive while weakening another's. Crucially, they do not assume that integration makes every contingency contractible or eliminates opportunism. Symmetric information and efficient ex-post renegotiation isolate ex-ante investment distortions. This is formal comparative institutional theory, with an insurance-industry application, rather than a measured rule for allocating AI assets. [R15-S01, pp. 691–698, 716–718]

Hart and Moore extend the analysis to multiple assets and people. Their control mechanism is exclusion from assets; nonowner employees' incentives depend on who can exclude them. Rights to a verifiable profit stream are explicitly distinct from residual control. Conditional results favor indispensable parties or common control of strictly complementary assets. But the paper itself warns against universalizing them: its objection to multiple vetoes depends on investment in human rather than physical capital; joint investment in an alienable asset can change the conclusion. The concluding discussion also admits different results with overinvestment, investment-type choices or other bargaining specifications, and notes omitted financing and wealth constraints. [R15-S02, pp. 1120–1125, 1132–1138, 1150–1152]

**Application, not a theorem about AI.** Suppose a worker spends time teaching a system to diagnose a rare failure and a firm supplies access to the relevant environment. If either party expects the other to appropriate the resulting benefit, the valuable teaching or access may never occur. But allocating the entire system to one side could weaken the other's contribution. The empirical question is whose **marginal future investment** responds to which rights, not simply who worked first, who paid the largest invoice, or who is most deserving.

Copyable adaptation creates a particularly important model boundary. The machine state may be alienable while the human teaching effort is not; future value may depend on both. One should not take a result proved for nontransferable human-capital investment, replace “worker” with “model,” and retain the property allocation unchanged. Nor should the model's efficiency criterion be treated as a complete moral or legal theory of entitlement.

### 4.2 Relational contracting: promises can matter within and across firm boundaries

Baker, Gibbons and Murphy compare spot and relational governance under both employment and outsourcing. Their repeated-game mechanism is the value of future cooperation relative to the temptation to renege now. Ownership changes those temptations, so either integration or nonintegration can support the better relationship, depending on the environment. Independent ownership can protect a supplier after a buyer breaks a promise, while also encouraging effort aimed at improving an outside bargaining position. Their managerial conclusion emphasizes designing, communicating and maintaining informal arrangements. The inspected text is the June 2001 forthcoming manuscript, not a claimed reading of final 2002 pagination. Its stylized risk-neutral, continuing relationship is not evidence that a particular employer's promise is credible. [R15-S03, manuscript §§I–IV, V.D]

This mechanism helps frame a development problem rather than solve it by title. A firm might promise attribution, promotion, a bonus or continuing access in exchange for teaching a reusable agent. A worker might promise documentation and handover. The important empirical questions include whether both understand the promise, whether performance can be observed, and whether future dealings provide a credible reason to honor it. A worker facing impending redundancy and a provider able to discontinue a service are not automatically in a long-lived cooperative equilibrium.

The human–AI pair in C-037 must not be confused with the contracting parties in this theory. Relationship-specific productivity can exist without the AI being an independent legal person or a party with its own enforceable wage claim. In many relevant arrangements, the bargain is among the worker, employer, provider and customers; the model is part of what they jointly use or develop. Describing behavioral familiarity as a “relationship” does not establish the repeated-game incentives assumed in a human contracting model.

### 4.3 Access and power: asset ownership is not the only instrument

Rajan and Zingales model privileged access to a critical resource as a means of inducing specialization. Access can confer an opportunity to become valuable without transferring residual ownership. Its incentive effects depend on whether investments are substitutes, additive or complementary. They also analyze cases where ownership weakens specialization incentives or crowds out access-based incentives. Their model offers an alternative to identifying all organizational power with physical-asset title. It does not predict that access always benefits workers or that access is secure when the person controlling it can change the arrangement. [R15-S04, pp. 387–391, 402–412, §IV]

For a proposed AI counterpart, a person may own little of the underlying stack yet have valuable access to training environments, deployment opportunities, customers or decision-making authority. Another person may own a copy but lack all those complements. This is a reason to examine access arrangements alongside ownership, not to rename access “ownership.” It also cuts both ways: an organization can provide useful opportunities while retaining the power to exclude, replace or redirect their recipient.

The distinction clarifies the earlier “internal Product Owner” prescription. A role can assign responsibility and discretion. Whether it also supplies exclusion rights, departure rights, revenue participation or protection against reassignment is a separate contractual and institutional question. Demonstrated contribution may improve a bargaining position, but a job title cannot be assumed to grant an intellectual-property interest.

### 4.4 Coordination and knowledge: a file is not necessarily the organization

Grant's knowledge-based account treats firms as mechanisms for integrating specialized knowledge. Rules, sequencing, routines and group problem-solving provide different ways to combine expertise without everyone learning everything. Common knowledge enables integration. The paper's individual-centered assumptions differ from accounts that locate organizational learning in routines; it explicitly discusses that disagreement. This conceptual contribution explains why transferring information need not reproduce coordinated capability. Its strong assumptions about individual knowledge and tacit transfer are not evidence that machine systems can never acquire or externalize relevant capabilities. [R15-S05, pp. 109–119]

Holmström and Roberts challenge a one-problem, one-instrument account of firm boundaries. They distinguish transaction-cost predictions from property-rights predictions and discuss arrangements in which close, relationship-specific cooperation occurs without common ownership. Their examples motivate additional roles for coordination, knowledge transfer, monitoring and complementary incentives. This is a theoretically informed comparative essay, not a causal estimate of which boundary is best. It nevertheless supplies material counterevidence to the inference that observed specificity, by itself, identifies the efficient owner. [R15-S06, pp. 75–83, 89–92]

These perspectives are complementary only after their questions are separated. A bargaining model asks who has an incentive to invest; a coordination account asks how distinct contributions become completed work. A copying technology may change the second problem while leaving the first, or vice versa. For example, a portable evaluation suite could make a contribution easier to demonstrate without making the underlying customer database portable. Alternatively, a perfectly transferable adapter may require substantial retraining of its new operator. The review therefore treats the separability of data, skill and organization as a hypothesis, not an artifact of how a directory is organized.

### 4.5 Nonrivalry creates both sharing opportunities and distributional conflicts

Jones and Tonetti model data as nonrival while allowing its use to be restricted. Their comparison of consumer and firm control shows that firms' concerns about creative destruction can limit socially useful sharing, while privacy costs matter separately. The near-optimality of consumer ownership in their numerical examples is model-dependent: representative consumers, specified production relationships and an explicitly simplified privacy treatment do substantial work. Data and the productive algorithms developed from it are also distinguished. This is a theoretical and quantitative illustration, not an estimated worker–firm allocation rule. [R15-S07, pp. 2819–2827, 2841–2857]

Acemoglu, Makhdoumi, Malekian and Ozdaglar emphasize another mechanism: one person's information can reveal information about other people. Their correlated-data model yields excessive sharing and depressed prices under specified conditions; competing platforms need not eliminate the externality. They examine taxes and mediated sharing, with implementation and trust left as important limitations. Their analysis does not refute technological nonrivalry. It shows why an individual's willingness to sell is not a complete measure of the social costs of disclosure. [R15-S08, pp. 218–224, 233–243]

**Comparative implication.** A worker's interaction history may simultaneously encode that worker's preferences, coworkers' actions and customer circumstances. “Both parties keep a copy” can remove a scarcity problem while creating a disclosure problem for people absent from the agreement. Conversely, prohibiting all reuse may destroy gains that could have been achieved through a genuinely separable, authorized component. These are competing possibilities. Neither a private bargain nor an engineering claim of nonrivalry determines the socially preferred arrangement.

## 5. Empirical evidence: institutions affect movement, but movement is not capability transfer

### 5.1 A quasi-experiment in inventor mobility

Marx, Strumsky and Fleming exploit Michigan's 1985 change in noncompete enforcement using patent-based difference-in-differences. The sample contains 98,468 inventors; the principal mobility table uses 274,406 patent observations. Their reported 8.1% effect is a relative change in intrastate employer-mobility risk, not an 8.1-percentage-point decline. Effects are larger for their firm-specificity and technical-specialization measures. The paper addresses automobile-industry confounding and alternative specifications. Patent matching and assignee changes imperfectly observe employment, do not distinguish voluntary departures, and omit nonpatenting workers. A single state's institutional change also limits generalization. This supports legal constraints as a cause of reduced mobility in the studied setting, not the proposition that portable AI assets have the same effect or that reduced mobility improves welfare. [R15-S09, pp. 880–887, Table 4]

### 5.2 A countervailing investment channel, with an explicit version limit

Jeffers's accessible September 2017 manuscript combines changes in noncompete enforceability with LinkedIn employment histories and public-firm investment data, principally covering 2008–2014. Table 3 reports 9,479 departure firm-years and 5,053 investment observations. Its estimates associate stronger enforcement with fewer departures and entrants but more physical investment at knowledge-intensive incumbents. The design uses difference-in-differences and robustness comparisons, not randomized contracts. Platform selection, firm matching, state assignment and legal-change timing matter. The author acknowledges incomplete coverage; the inspected manuscript defines active membership using a 60-day window. The final 2024 article's metadata and abstract confirm the qualitative tension, but its full revised analysis was not recovered here. Accordingly, no manuscript coefficient is presented as a final-publication result, and neither version is treated as a welfare calculation. [R15-S10, manuscript §§2–6; final publication access record below]

### 5.3 What the comparison can and cannot support

These studies are useful because they make institutional constraints observable rather than treating exit as frictionless. They also study different populations, outcomes and periods. Patent-record mobility is not the same outcome as departure from a public firm, founding a new firm, or acquiring physical capital. An incumbent's investment increase and a prospective entrepreneur's lost opportunity are not contradictory observations. Adding them together requires a model of quality, alternative uses, time horizons and welfare that these comparisons do not provide for AI.

There are also limits to transporting the mechanisms themselves. An employee's physical departure can remove access to indispensable human labor. A lawful, usable machine copy might leave more capability behind. But a retained copy could be obsolete, lack crucial inputs, or require ongoing human complementarity. The degree of substitution is the research question. Neither “the worker left” nor “the firm retained the model” is a sufficient treatment description.

[R-09 §4.1](../R-09/README.md#41-departure-studies-isolate-different-losses) contains the detailed collaborator-, occupation- and executive-departure evidence. Its heterogeneous results reinforce the need to distinguish individual, relational and organizational contributions; those source capsules are not duplicated here. [R-17](../R-17/README.md) examines the machine-pair comparison directly. The reviewed legal and organizational evidence does not measure an executable résumé's credibility, a worker-owned counterpart's wage premium, or a clean model-adaptation export's effect on entrepreneurship.

A credible empirical program would measure not merely whether workers have access to an AI tool, but what they can retain, demonstrate, redeploy and continue improving after an actual change of employer. Selection is especially important: people with unusually valuable portable systems may self-select into contracting, senior roles or accommodating employers. Their income would not identify the causal return to ownership alone.

## 6. United States legal illustration: overlapping rights, different defaults

This section reports bounded legal propositions from official sources inspected on 4 October 2026. California rules are not nationwide rules. Agency guidance is identified as guidance; model allocations are not law. The appendix records a current-source access limitation for the U.S. Code's live preliminary edition. A publication or actual transaction needs a fresh jurisdiction- and agreement-specific review, including subsequent judicial treatment.

### 6.1 Copyright is not a general property right in everything learned

Under 17 U.S.C. §101, employee work within the scope of employment is one category of work made for hire. Specially commissioned work has a different route, limited to listed categories and a signed written agreement. The definition of a joint work turns on authorship and an intention to combine contributions. Sections 102–103 distinguish protected expression from ideas, processes and methods, and limit derivative-work protection to the relevant new contribution. Economically joint production therefore does not itself establish a legally joint copyrighted work. [R15-S11]

Section 201 generally starts copyright with the author, but for a qualifying work made for hire treats the employer or commissioning party as author and owner unless a signed written instrument provides otherwise. Section 202 separates ownership of a material copy from ownership of copyright. Section 204 generally requires a signed writing for copyright transfers other than by operation of law. Thus, having the repository or writing the code does not by itself settle all rights in it; a contractual allocation is also distinct from physical possession. [R15-S12]

For the book, the relevant objects may include human-authored source code, documentation and selected/arranged materials. The same analysis cannot simply be extended to every output, inference, remembered fact or numerical parameter. Determining whether a particular adaptation contains protectable expression and who holds any rights in it is an additional inquiry.

### 6.2 Patent inventions do not inherit copyright's work-for-hire default

In *Stanford v. Roche*, the Supreme Court rejected automatic vesting of federally funded inventions in the contractor under Bayh–Dole. Its analysis starts with inventors' rights and the importance of assignments, rather than treating employment alone as universal ownership. The Court did not decide the validity of the lower court's interpretation of the particular assignment language, an issue outside the granted question. This is a narrow but important caution against compressing patent and copyright defaults into “the employer owns all work.” [R15-S13, 563 U.S. 776, 784 n.2, 785–789]

*Dubilier* distinguishes employment to invent, which can entail an assignment obligation, from more general employment; it also describes the employer's nonexclusive “shop right” in appropriate circumstances involving employer time and resources. These are different interests from title. Its 1933 discussion must not be used as a current account of every government-employee regime or patent term. [R15-S14, 289 U.S. 178, 187–189]

California Labor Code §2870 limits certain employee invention-assignment provisions for inventions developed entirely on the employee's own time without specified employer resources. But its exceptions include inventions related to the employer's business or actual/demonstrably anticipated research or development, and inventions resulting from work for the employer. “Built at home” is not the entire statutory test. This provision does not resolve every copyright, confidentiality or model-license issue. [R15-S15, §2870(a)–(b)]

The joint-production question needs similar care. Under 35 U.S.C. §262, absent a contrary agreement, a patent co-owner can undertake specified exploitation without another co-owner's consent or accounting. That is not the same arrangement as two people each holding a veto. Section 261 separately addresses written assignments. Consequently, an economic comparison labeled “joint ownership” must specify its actual control and revenue rules. [R15-S23, §§261–262]

### 6.3 Trade secrets and confidentiality concern acquisition, use and disclosure

18 U.S.C. §1839 defines trade secrets through both reasonable secrecy measures and independent economic value from secrecy, not merely an employer's label. Its misappropriation provisions encompass specified improper acquisition and unauthorized disclosure/use with relevant knowledge or duties. The definition excludes reverse engineering, independent derivation and other lawful acquisition from “improper means.” That exclusion is not a blanket permission to violate a separate contract, copyright or other applicable rule. A license can also be part of the legally recognized interest in a trade secret. [R15-S16, §1839(3)–(6)]

The federal civil remedy in §1836 has a mobility boundary: an injunction cannot prevent entering employment, and employment conditions must rest on evidence of threatened misappropriation, not merely what a person knows; it must also respect applicable state restraints rules. That does not abolish remedies for actual or threatened misappropriation. Section 1833 additionally protects specified confidential reporting of suspected legal violations and sealed filings, with conditions and employer notice provisions. It does not authorize otherwise unlawful access. Confidentiality is therefore neither meaningless nor an unlimited gag. [R15-S17, §1836(b)(3)(A); R15-S18, §1833(b)]

**Application requiring proof.** A model component trained on confidential material is not proved to be a trade secret merely by the word “trained”; nor is it proved clean because it omits a readable source document. The object, secrecy practices, provenance, applicable duties and proposed use must be established. Whether a specific model state incorporates or discloses protected information is a factual and legal question. This review does not infer lawful copying from technical extraction.

The synthesis's statement that a human brain leaves with its employee is therefore best read as a physical-embodiment contrast. Its explicit “partially” matters. Human memory does not settle all lawful-use boundaries, and a machine copy does not make those boundaries disappear.

### 6.4 Mobility restraints are jurisdiction-specific and temporally unstable

California Business and Professions Code §16600 broadly invalidates employment noncompetes that do not satisfy a statutory exception; the text expressly rejects a narrow-tailoring escape from that rule. It should not be generalized to other states or treated as permission to take confidential materials. [R15-S19]

At the federal level, the FTC's 2024 nationwide Non-Compete Clause Rule is not an operative blanket ban. After judicial vacatur and the Commission's decision to abandon its appeals, the FTC removed the rule from the Code of Federal Regulations effective **12 February 2026**. This was checked against the official final rule, not an older announcement. Removal does not itself establish the validity of every noncompete or displace other legal constraints. [R15-S20, 91 Fed. Reg. 6507–6510, §I.C and removal instruction]

For C-050, an employee cannot be advised from this literature to “force” a title change, ignore contractual restrictions or retain a work-created system on the assumption that code portability has settled employment rights. The institutional question remains which enforceable arrangements and outside opportunities exist in the actual setting.

### 6.5 AI authorship and inventorship do not settle ownership of the whole system

The Copyright Office's January 2025 report maintains the human-authorship requirement and allows protection for qualifying human expression even when a work also contains AI-generated material. Its analysis of then-available systems concludes that prompts alone ordinarily do not provide the requisite expressive control; modification, selection or arrangement can present different cases. This is the Office's legal/policy analysis of outputs, not an adjudication assigning every trained weight or adaptation to a worker or employer. [R15-S21, pp. 7–11, 18–27, 41]

The USPTO's **November 2025 revised guidance**, which rescinded its 2024 AI-inventorship guidance, applies the same inventorship standard with or without AI assistance and limits inventors to natural persons. It distinguishes human joint inventorship from use of an AI tool. Inventorship, patentability, assignment and ownership of the computational system remain separate questions. Treating an AI as an experienced productive actor in an economic hypothesis does not give it inventor status under that guidance. [R15-S22, §§II–IV]

The genuine open issue for this review is consequently more specific than “nobody knows who owns AI.” Existing doctrines already govern many components and agreements. What remains unresolved here is how they apply to particular composite, jointly developed learned states, especially when general capability and restricted information cannot be reliably separated. No inspected primary source supplied a general rule allocating such a state by counting the worker's time, the firm's compute spending or the system's interactions.

### 6.6 Data portability is not synonymous with model portability

California Civil Code §1798.130(a)(2) requires covered disclosures in a usable form allowing transfer between entities, subject to the statute's request and applicability conditions. This concerns required personal-information disclosure. It is not, by its terms, a grant of the entire model, the provider's code or coworkers' and customers' information to an employee. Whether a particular record or inference falls within the relevant privacy regime requires separate analysis. [R15-S24]

An export of conversation history may therefore be valuable without supplying executable capability. A contractual promise to return “customer data” may also differ from one to return adapters, evaluation histories, audit records or a reproducible runtime. Rather than infer a broad right from the word “portability,” the research should name the object, format, permissions and assistance obligation. The inverse matters too: a provider's export feature does not override a firm's duties to other people whose information appears in the export.

### 6.7 Licenses can enable substantial use without conveying ownership

Apache License 2.0 grants broad copyright permissions subject to conditions, includes a defined patent grant, and requires specified notices on redistribution. It does not transfer all contributors' ownership or grant rights outside its scope. Its text is a concrete counterexample to equating useful control solely with exclusive title, while also showing that “source available” is not a complete license analysis. [R15-S25, §§1–5]

Meta's **Llama 3.1 Community License**, for the release dated 23 July 2024, separately defines model materials, grants a limited nonexclusive license, imposes redistribution/use conditions and additional commercial terms, and allocates modifications as between the licensee and Meta subject to Meta's retained interests. Its termination provisions matter. The license's “you” can be an employer represented by an authorized person; its allocation between Meta and a licensee does not decide a worker–employer dispute. This is a versioned contractual example, not a characterization of every open-weight model or later Llama release. [R15-S26, definitions and §§1–2, 5–7]

The legal inference for raw-code workflows is limited but decisive: a generally readable representation can reduce one technical dependency while leaving upstream licensing, hosting, data, employment and integration dependencies intact. Some no-code systems may offer useful export or contractual protections; some raw-code systems may remain operationally or legally difficult to move. The reviewed sources do not establish a universal product-class ranking.

## 7. Alternative control designs: comparative questions, not a recommendation

The following designs are researcher-constructed alternatives for evaluating the author's questions. They are not existing legal defaults, completed contracts or Luke's normative position. Every design requires permission for the relevant components and protection of third parties.

### A. Employer-controlled development with explicit worker compensation

The organization retains the work-specific state and continuity of deployment; the worker receives agreed compensation, attribution or participation in gains. This can reduce disruption when personnel change and make coordinated security controls easier. The weakness is not only fairness: if valuable teaching is hard to observe, the employee may have little reason to improve the reusable system beyond the immediate task. A promised bonus, a formula tied to audited outcomes and equity in an enterprise pose different enforcement and measurement problems.

The test is whether the compensation and opportunity structure actually induces development and whether retention preserves performance. It cannot be assumed that employer control eliminates dependency on the original developer, or that compensation accurately measures contribution. Equally, lack of individual title does not imply lack of productive work or zero remuneration.

### B. Worker-controlled preexisting system with a bounded employer license

A professional brings a previously developed system and licenses specified uses during the engagement. Preexisting components, permitted work-specific changes and post-departure rights would need to be identified. This can support investment across a career and make outside options more credible. The organization may worry about continuity, supervision, customer obligations and improvements financed by its work.

The important comparison is not simply “freedom versus control.” An employer license might be sufficient for reliable continuity, or it might fail when support stops or rights to necessary complements are absent. A worker's nominal ownership might yield little leverage if a provider can suspend access or if the useful state cannot lawfully leave the employer's environment.

### C. Dual-use rights or authorized copies for both sides

A worker and firm could negotiate permitted continued uses rather than insist that only one possess the bits. Such an arrangement could avoid destroying a nonrival capability at separation. But equal possession is not equal economic power: the firm may retain customers and infrastructure while the worker retains distinctive judgment, or neither may possess a complete functioning system.

The design must define confidentiality boundaries, derivative improvements, future updates, redistribution, commercial fields of use, security obligations and support. It must also decide whether each side can grant rights to others. “Both keep it” is too underspecified to evaluate. It could describe nonexclusive licenses, separate lawful copies, an actual jointly owned right or merely an informal promise, with different consequences in each case.

### D. Modular separation of personal and institutional components

A system could maintain separate personal preferences, employer records, generic workflows and work-specific adapters, with an authorized departure package. This may reduce the cost of agreeing on boundaries in advance. It can also impose engineering, evaluation and governance costs during normal work, and interactions among modules can make their marginal contributions difficult to isolate.

Modularity is therefore a candidate intervention, not proof of clean separation. A separately stored component might encode restricted information, depend on a confidential interface or lose its usefulness when another module is removed. The relevant outcome is an authorized package with retained performance and acceptable disclosure risk, not merely successful serialization into a separate file.

### E. Provider-mediated access or service continuity instead of export

Where a state cannot be meaningfully exported, a contract might specify continued access, service levels, migration assistance or a restricted interface. This can preserve utility without circulating all underlying data. It also makes provider continuity, pricing and enforcement more important; service access is not equivalent to irrevocable ownership.

The comparison must include provider exit, model retirement, version changes, interoperability and the cost of validating replacements. A promise of an API endpoint may be insufficient if performance depends on a changing hidden runtime. Conversely, demanding full weights may be unnecessary for a user who needs a well-defined service and reliable continuity.

### F. Collective or delegated governance of shared components

A team, cooperative, trust or other legal arrangement could control a shared capability and grant defined access to contributors. This may fit contributions that cannot be attributed to one person and make third-party interests easier to represent. It can also introduce decision delays, ambiguous accountability, financing problems and disputes about membership or departure.

Theoretical objections to a particular multiple-veto structure should not be generalized to every collective arrangement. Conversely, the word “collective” cannot establish good incentives or legitimate representation. The research must specify voting, delegation, exclusion, revenue rights and exit, then compare them with feasible alternatives.

Across all six designs, evaluate at least five outcomes separately: useful investment, quality-adjusted production, continuity after separation, distribution of gains and exposure of other parties. A design that increases an incumbent's surplus can reduce worker mobility; one that improves mobility can create new coordination or confidentiality costs. The review does not combine those outcomes into a preferred social ranking without the author's explicit evaluative commitments.

## 8. A departure scenario that reveals the missing evidence

Consider a hypothetical analyst who brings a personal assistant into a firm, helps create an internal workflow, and later leaves. This is an analytical example, not an account of Luke or an observed case.

First, ask what existed at entry. A general assistant subscription, a licensed local model, a personal prompt library and an independently written application are distinct starting points. Records establishing provenance can narrow a later disagreement, but provenance alone does not show the scope of a license or assignment.

Second, identify what changed. The analyst may have learned a better diagnostic method; human-authored code may have been written within employment; an adapter may have been trained on firm material; a retrieval system may contain customer records; and deployment may depend on permissions granted to the firm's account. Calling all five “experience” obscures the allocation problem.

Third, identify what each party wants to do. Retaining a copy for audit, continuing internal production, selling an adaptation to competitors and publishing an executable portfolio are different proposed uses. A consent sufficient for one is not automatically sufficient for the others. “Can the AI leave?” is therefore incomplete without a destination, use and component inventory.

Fourth, ask what lawful subtraction does to performance. Removing customer data may be easy, difficult or insufficient; replacing it with a permitted benchmark may reveal that most apparent advantage depended on privileged information. Alternatively, a reusable workflow may remain valuable with new data. Both outcomes are consistent with the theories. Evaluation must compare the authorized package with a fresh system on the same destination tasks, rather than giving the experienced system its original data advantage and calling the difference portable learning.

Fifth, examine what remains with the employer. A copy may preserve helpful routines without the worker; it may instead contain undocumented dependencies, obsolete assumptions or a brittle approximation. The worker may retain transferable skill without any copy. These counterfactuals prevent the book from treating retention as either complete expropriation or complete continuity by definition.

Finally, consider reputational evidence. A clean performance demonstration could make ability more legible to a new employer. But a résumé claim must distinguish the individual, the paired system and the previous organization's resources. A system that performed well with broad access and supervision has not necessarily demonstrated the same capability under a new mandate. Provenance, task conditions and limits matter as much as the impressive artifact.

This scenario shows why jointly produced improvements should be analyzed **before investment and after separation**, with the same component map. Bargaining after a valuable state already exists cannot reveal which investments would have occurred under a different initial arrangement. A successful later settlement also cannot establish that the original allocation was efficient or fair.

## 10. Counterevidence, investigated gaps and next tests

### Material counterevidence that the book must retain

- A worker's useful contribution can be strengthened through access or relational compensation without exclusive title; ownership can also weaken another necessary contributor's incentives.
- Conditional objections to joint vetoes do not establish a universal case against shared rights, particularly when investment changes an alienable asset.
- Nonrival information can be underused because of exclusion and over-shared because of effects on other people. These are different distortions, not a reason to assume either unlimited openness or blanket enclosure.
- Mobility restrictions can affect incumbent investment as well as exit and entry. Those effects do not constitute a welfare verdict.
- Existing law already supplies differentiated rules. Calling the entire area legally unsettled would obscure settled component-level constraints just as much as inventing a universal AI-ownership rule would.

### Evidence sought but not established

1. **A clean general-adaptation export.** This review did not recover a study proving that a work-trained persistent actor can retain all general capability while reliably excluding every protected input or inference. R-14 should examine technical separation and unlearning; a benchmark success would still not decide legal scope.
2. **An ownership-treatment experiment.** No inspected source randomly assigns economically meaningful control of persistent personal AI to workers versus employers and measures development, wages, mobility and firm performance over time.
3. **An executable-résumé standard.** No inspected evidence establishes standardized attribution, independent validation, lawful disclosure and cross-employer predictive validity for this proposed credential. Demonstrated outputs alone cannot establish those properties.
4. **A comprehensive contemporary contract sample.** This is not a survey of actual employer AI policies or all major provider agreements. The two versioned licenses show why terms matter; they cannot establish the prevalence of worker-friendly or employer-friendly allocations.
5. **A final-version reconstruction of Jeffers.** The final publication was identified, but full revised text was not recovered. Its quantitative findings should not be imported from the older manuscript without reconciliation.
6. **A jurisdiction-complete legal treatment.** The U.S./California illustration excludes a fifty-state enforceability survey, choice-of-law disputes, sector privacy rules, international data transfers and other jurisdictions' employee-invention regimes. These omissions are boundaries, not claims that such rules do not matter.

### Research designs that would discriminate among the hypotheses

**A portability comparison** should hold destination tasks and lawful data access fixed while varying original versus new operator and experienced versus fresh system. Add an employer-retained version to distinguish worker-specific complementarity from stand-alone state value. Measure completed-work quality, correction costs and adaptation time; record what was removed, licensed or substituted. R-17 should reconcile this proposed design with its paired-productivity evidence.

**A contracting comparison** should record terms before development, including preexisting components, work-created improvements, exit rights, support, revenue rights and provider dependencies. Compare investment effort and realized capability, while accounting for sorting into firms and bargaining positions. Observed ownership after success is particularly vulnerable to reverse causality and selection.

**A separability evaluation** should distinguish readable secret leakage, behavioral disclosure, dependence on privileged data and broader useful capability. Passing a finite test set is evidence under that test, not a legal certificate or proof of absence. Governance may require restricted use rather than an unsupported claim that everything sensitive has been removed.

**A distributional assessment** should examine workers, incumbent firms, entrants, providers, customers and affected third parties. An increase in licensing revenue is not automatically net social value; a fall in the price of a component is not automatically the disappearance of all rents. R-11 and R-12 supply the accounting and welfare reconciliation.

## 11. Historical analogy and downstream reconciliation

[R-18's integrative assessment](../R-18/README.md#exchange-development-and-capture-are-different-questions) separates productive development, exchange and capture. Its Portugal, Ford and infrastructure reviews do not establish a universal ownership winner. R-15 adds a mechanism for examining particular arrangements, rather than using a historical investor's success or failure as proof of who should control an AI adaptation.

The analogy fits where production requires complementary investments and parties face uncertainty about future uses or returns. It breaks where a scarce physical machine is treated as equivalent to an information component usable by several parties, where a model copy substitutes for a human relationship without evidence, or where a historical labor arrangement is assumed to supply current legal rights. Neither classical industrial integration nor modern cloud contracting is a ready-made template for every persistent counterpart.

The required synthesis gates are:

- **R-14:** identify which state changes are retrieval, external records, parameters or other adaptation; verify export and retention claims separately.
- **R-17:** establish paired versus stand-alone value, with attribution and destination controls.
- **R-05:** test durability, substitution and bargaining rather than equating ownership with rents.
- **R-09:** distinguish portable human skill, specific matches and replacement costs; do not treat machine retention as measured prevention of senior-stock loss.
- **R-02:** compare maintainability and deployability of concrete raw-code/no-code systems; neither representation settles rights.
- **R-11/R-12:** distinguish capital services, income claims, measured investment and social welfare.
- **R-06/R-07/R-13:** reconcile tacit knowledge, meaningful authority, privacy, provenance and verification limits.

### Questions reserved for the author

The evidence can identify consequences and unresolved trade-offs; it cannot select their normative weights. Luke may eventually need to decide whether the book advocates a particular default for work-created adaptations, proposes negotiated component-level arrangements, or primarily makes the problem visible. A further choice concerns whether an “executable résumé” is an economic forecast, a governance proposal or a metaphor for demonstrated capability. Those are different ambitions with different evidence burdens.

No such choice is made here. The research-ready formulation is that productive machine histories may create new assets and complementarities, while their development and distribution depend on technically feasible, legally valid and economically credible arrangements. That preserves the author's question without pre-answering it.

### Method, access failures and reproducibility limits

This was a targeted analytical review organized around the admitted claims, not a preregistered systematic review or a meta-analysis. Searches combined incomplete contracts/residual rights, access and relational contracting, firm-specific knowledge, nonrival data, labor mobility, employee inventions, trade secrets, AI authorship/inventorship and versioned licensing. The selection deliberately included theories that weaken a title-centered account and evidence of countervailing effects. Search discovery alone did not qualify a source for a substantive verdict.

Public PDFs were read through extracted text where available. Scanned Grossman–Hart, Hart–Moore and Grant copies required OCR. The Hart–Moore scan was retrieved from the author's migrated Harvard site after older Harvard links, repository endpoints and a textless Columbia scan failed to yield usable text. The conclusion's explicit assumption limits and key footnotes were checked in substantive OCR. OCR is not suitable evidence for a new algebraic proof; none is claimed. Original scholarly PDFs have not been reproduced in this public deliverable.

Publisher and SSRN routes did not yield Jeffers's final full article. The 2017 version is explicitly separated from 2023/2024 publication metadata, rather than silently combined. Groysberg–Lee–Nanda's star-performance portability paper was a relevant discovery lead, but no substantive full-text result from it is used here. Lazear and the human-departure studies are cross-referenced through R-09's existing capsules to preserve version and source-budget discipline.

**Current-law access qualification.** Several live `uscode.house.gov` preliminary-edition pages returned maintenance notices; attempted 2025 GPO section endpoints were unavailable. For §§1833, 1836 and 1839, the substantive legal text read was the official **2024** U.S. Code edition, including amendment histories. Current search-index records suggested unchanged text into September 2026, but those records were treated as leads, not a substitute for a successful current-edition read. Consequently, the precise positive statements here are tied to the inspected official text; this review does not certify an exhaustive legislative/current-case update through 4 October. The FTC 2026 removal, California current statutory pages and USPTO 2025 replacement guidance were directly retrieved. This limitation should survive publication and trigger a fresh legal check before those propositions are used as advice or as a categorical current-law statement.

The legal research did not use commercial citators or review every subsequent decision applying the cited statutes and cases. Common-law doctrines, scope of employment, agreement interpretation, choice of law, customer duties and specific protected material require facts absent from the public corpus. These are genuine limits, not reasons to invent an ownership allocation.

The empirical papers' underlying restricted employment data were not accessed. No original regression, causal replication, model export, confidentiality audit or contractual experiment was performed. Formal propositions, observational/quasi-experimental findings, legal text, agency guidance and this review's design proposals are kept distinct throughout.
