# R-05 Competitive advantage and value capture

**Research date:** 4 October 2026.  
**Admitted claims:** C-009, C-039, C-040, C-041, C-050.  
**Authority:** Loop 2 and the accepted, revisable D-002.  
**Status:** Substantive literature review accepted after independent quality review; coordinated publication checkpoint. Research judgments are not author adoption or a thesis freeze.

## Findings and scope

The evidence supports a conditional connection between organizational improvement and productive performance. It does not establish a general conversion from useful improvement into durable competitive advantage, or from either into the improver's pay. Those transitions need separate evidence. An institution can use a broadly available practice to improve markedly without acquiring an exclusive resource. An employee can create an exclusive resource without acquiring ownership. An owner can receive revenue without earning an economic rent after costs and risk. These are central distinctions, not qualifications to append after a promise of prosperity.

D-002's four positions are therefore useful analytical categories: architectural work, tool-use education, consequential operation, and ownership. The first three describe activities; the fourth describes rights and exposure. A person may occupy several at once. No reviewed evidence establishes a universal ranking among them. In particular, the predecessor claims that an architectural title commands a massive premium, that internal role renegotiation guarantees capture, and that visual no-code tools uniformly prevent ownership do not survive as established general propositions.

Repeated diagnosis, experimentation, implementation and correction can matter. But “the method is the moat” needs two tests: does a specified method improve quality-adjusted outcomes after its costs, and does its holder retain an advantage when others adapt? Management experiments address the first more convincingly than the second. Evidence of multiyear persistence is important but geographically and technologically bounded. Current AI studies show task gains, null effects, losses, changing skill gradients and selection problems. They do not yet demonstrate a durable economy-wide rent to human redesign specialists.

The public author corpus supplies the questions, not independent evidence. The principal provenance is C-009 and C-041 in S-04's continuing-redesign discussion and S-10 §§3–4; C-039 in S-03's “The decision,” S-07's intrapreneurial pivot and S-10 §§4,13; C-040 in S-06's ownership-dominance conclusion; and C-050 in S-07 §§2–5. D-002 expressly revises ownership-only dominance. It does not establish future compensation or permanent human scarcity. The historical ledger and opposing source text remain unchanged.

## Four contributions and four different compensation problems

| Position | Productive contribution or economic function | Possible income route | What must be established separately |
|---|---|---|---|
| Architectural work | Identify valuable outcomes, redesign dependencies, allocate tasks and authority, implement and maintain changes | Salary, consulting fees, project payments, royalties or an expressly allocated equity claim | Net improvement; attribution within a team; demand for the skill; alternatives to this supplier; ownership and compensation terms |
| Tool-use education | Help others select, understand and use tools and workflows effectively | Teaching wages, training fees, institutional training budgets | Retained capability and transfer; learner adoption; willingness and ability to pay; competition from other educators and automated instruction |
| Consequential operation | Make situated decisions, detect failures, resolve exceptions and act on consequences | Wages, professional fees, performance pay | Whether judgment changes outcomes; actual discretion and support; replacement options; risk and reward allocation |
| Ownership | Supply finance or other assets, hold claims on returns and exercise specified or residual control | Profits, distributions, license receipts, capital gains, possibly losses | Exact asset and rights; acquisition and maintenance costs; demand; imitation; complementary assets; competition and risk |

This classification avoids treating every useful skill as “ownership.” An architect paid to design a system may create an asset for an employer. An educator can make others more productive without owning their resulting human capital. An operator may receive a wage partly for availability and responsibility rather than for the volume of documents produced. An owner may also contribute all three kinds of labor; observed business income then needs decomposition before it is called a return to passive capital.

Nor is “productive” identical to “well paid.” The proposed test of contribution is counterfactual: what happens to useful outcomes, reliability and total resource use when this input changes? The test of capture concerns a different counterfactual: what compensation, outside opportunity or residual claim does this participant obtain? Fairness and legitimate human authority are further questions, not conclusions of either test.

## Competing explanations of advantage

### Complementary assets and appropriability

Teece's profiting-from-innovation framework asks why innovators can lose commercially while followers or owners of complements prosper. Appropriability, design evolution and access to specialized manufacturing, distribution and other assets jointly condition capture. Generic complements available competitively have different implications from complements on which an innovation depends asymmetrically. The framework is theoretical and case-based, not an estimated probability that every innovator should integrate. Its lesson for C-039 is to identify the scarce complement and the contracting problem, rather than infer returns from having created the invention. [R05-S01, §§3–5]

For AI workflows, possible complements include trustworthy data, integration rights, customer relationships, distribution, a deployable operating environment and the capacity to maintain service. This list is an application of the framework, not evidence that every listed item is scarce in every market. If an architect can readily buy these complements, independent provision may be viable. If the employer controls them, the same design may have little value outside that employer. Conversely, customer access or a legal right may preserve a firm's return even when its code is easy to reproduce. “The model is available to everyone” cannot settle the competitive structure of the complete service.

### Dynamic capabilities versus reproducible good practice

Eisenhardt and Martin challenge the inference that dynamic capability is inherently rare and inimitable. Identifiable processes such as product development and resource recombination can share best-practice features across firms. Their account locates lasting advantage in resource configurations, while rapidly changing markets may produce a succession of temporary advantages. Different processes can reach similar results. This is a conceptual synthesis with selected empirical illustrations, not a causal trial of continual redesign; it nevertheless supplies direct counterargument to an unconditional methodological moat. [R05-S02, pp.1105–1107,1116–1119]

Winter distinguishes ordinary operating capability from patterned capability for changing it, and both from ad hoc problem solving. Sustaining routines requires investment; a higher-order capability is attractive only when its benefits justify its costs relative to alternatives. Frequent change is therefore not itself evidence of excellence. Nor does every successful response prove that the organization possessed a general reusable capability beforehand. [R05-S03, manuscript pp.1–8]

The distinction helps repair a potential circularity in C-009. If “good method” means whatever successful firms did, success cannot validate it. Specify practices before observing outcomes: how problems are selected, alternatives compared, failures detected, updates retained, and redesign stopped when it is unhelpful. Then measure whether these practices predict later performance beyond prior performance and access to capital, demand, talent and technology. A method can be teachable and socially valuable while yielding little scarcity rent to its first user.

The empirical “management as technology” program offers a different perspective from purely contingent accounts of style. Bloom, Sadun and Van Reenen use surveys of more than 11,000 firms in 34 countries and a structural model incorporating managerial investment, adjustment and depreciation. Their evidence favors systematic performance relevance over the claim that all practices are merely local tastes. But the estimated contribution to productivity dispersion depends on the model and management measure; it is not a randomized decomposition of national prosperity or a measured return to an AI architect. [R05-S11, §§2–5]

Together, these approaches leave room for both common good practices and distinctive implementations. A firm may gain because it was previously far from attainable practice, because a configuration fits its circumstances, because learning is difficult to transfer, or because competitors face other constraints. These are different explanations. Persistent heterogeneity does not identify which one holds, and a positive intervention effect does not prove that its technique remains rare.

### Residual control and incomplete contracts

Incomplete-contract theory separates residual control from productive contribution; reallocating control can change investment incentives. [R05-S04; detailed treatment in R-15]

For the book, this prevents conflating an internal “Product Owner,” responsibility for maintenance, possession of a repository, and legal rights to exclude others, license, transfer or retain the asset on departure. The same words can denote different arrangements. R-15 must supply the relevant rights and institutional boundaries. R-05's conclusion is narrower: creation, operational authority and residual claims should be measured independently.

## What management interventions establish

### Productive redesign can be causal without being costless

Bloom and colleagues randomized intensive implementation support among large, family-managed textile firms near Mumbai. The June 2012 manuscript, subsequently published in 2013, reports a first-year productivity gain around 17%, through quality, efficiency and inventory changes. The study involves 17 firms; control plants also received diagnostics. An imputed annual profit gain of about $325,000 is not an audited cash-profit treatment effect. Commercial-equivalent consulting cost was estimated around $250,000 per plant, although the research arrangement differed. Selected participation and a small number of firm clusters constrain generalization despite randomization and finite-sample inference. [R05-S05, §§II–IV and profitability appendix]

This supports the possibility of valuable architectural and implementation work. It does not isolate the contribution of diagnosis from training, information systems, managerial attention and employee execution. Nor does it tell us what the consultants, middle managers or operators captured from the gain. Paying for a package that improves production is different from estimating a rent to a particular occupation.

### Persistence is neither automatic compounding nor immediate imitation

The follow-up evidence is consolidated in [R-09's organizational-persistence capsule](../R-09/README.md#43-documentation-and-external-expertise-can-help-but-maintenance-still-matters). A lasting organizational difference and a lasting rent are distinct claims; the latter requires financial outcomes and a competitive counterfactual. [R05-S06]

Giorcelli's historical Italian study provides a longer window. An unexpected reduction in the 1952–1958 US Productivity Program restricted already-applying firms' access to management visits and machinery. The inspected 2016 manuscript uses the resulting provincial allocation, pre-trends and comparisons among applicants; the final 2019 abstract confirms enduring management effects and complementarity with machinery. This is a quasi-experiment, not random assignment of firms to enthusiasm for management. Regional counterfactuals, applicant selection, postwar conditions and changing survivor composition require care. Its long horizon is valuable, but it is not a direct test of AI-era imitation speed. [R05-S07, manuscript §§2–5]

The persistence evidence favors investing in implementation and maintenance over equating method with a written specification. It also limits the claim that continuing superiority necessarily requires endless radical redesign. Some gains arise from sustained execution of comparatively ordinary practices. Continuous replacement of functioning arrangements could dissipate those gains.

### A better technique can fail inside a profitable firm

Atkin and colleagues introduced a material-saving cutting technology to Pakistani soccer-ball producers. Adoption was surprisingly limited. Piece-rate cutters could lose from slower cutting even when the firm saved materials; a subsequent incentive intervention increased adoption. The two-stage field evidence connects technical improvement to organizational distribution and information flows. It does not imply that all resistance is irrational or that any worker can independently renegotiate a gain-sharing contract. The intervention changed incentives in a specific production setting; several accounts of contracting rigidity remain possible. [R05-S08, §§III–VI]

This is particularly important for the intrapreneurial prescription. A workflow improvement is not a single pie that appears before anyone decides how to divide it. Expected distribution can change whether the improvement is disclosed, adopted, maintained or sabotaged. Bargaining and production are jointly determined. “Document the saving” may improve information, but does not by itself repair incentives or supply a credible outside option.

### Contrary intervention evidence matters

Karlan, Knight and Udry's Ghana experiment studied 160 tailoring microenterprises receiving consulting, capital, both or neither. The inspected 2014 paper reports initial practice and investment responses without higher average profits; some intermediate effects were negative and practices later reverted. Tailored expert advice was not enough to reproduce the large-firm India result. Small samples and noisy profits limit precision; a null average should not be turned into proof that nobody benefited. The authors' experimentation interpretation is plausible rather than a separately randomized mechanism. [R05-S09, §§I,III–V]

Comparison across India, Italy and Ghana suggests a conditional question: which constraints are being relaxed, for what firms, at what scale and cost? A practice that economizes coordination in a multilevel factory may consume scarce owner time in a small shop. Beneficial change can require demand, finance, personnel and complementary routines. Returning to an earlier practice may be rational learning, organizational decay, or both; abandonment alone cannot distinguish them.

## What AI evidence adds and what it does not

### Assistance can redistribute capability inside a workflow

The campaign's published-version evidence capsule is in [R-09, AI-mediated transfer and its limits](../R-09/README.md#ai-mediated-transfer-and-its-limits). For value capture, the key analytical distinction is between disseminating effective practices and compensating the people who originally supplied them. Productive diffusion does not by itself reveal an allocation of returns. [R05-S12]

Architectural, educational and operational contributions may therefore coexist with a reduction in the scarcity of some prior expertise. More people performing better can narrow an incumbent's performance lead. That is not a social failure; it is a reason to keep improvements in useful service separate from rents earned by a formerly scarce expert.

### Software performance is sensitive to task selection and tool vintage

METR's early-2025 randomized study assigned 246 real repository tasks among 16 experienced open-source developers to AI-allowed or disallowed conditions. Completion took 19% longer with AI, contrary to participants' beliefs. This bounded result measures experienced maintainers using the tools available in February–June 2025, not all coding or all noncoders. The paper examines quality and task-process explanations; it does not establish that architecture is permanently human or that visual development would outperform code generation. [R05-S13, §§2–4 and limitations]

METR's February 2026 follow-up explicitly calls its later estimate unreliable: developers and tasks selected out when participants disliked working without AI, compensation changed, and concurrent agents complicated time accounting. Raw speedup estimates are not a clean reversal or a current universal rate. The researchers judge acceleration plausible while withholding a reliable magnitude. This methodological update belongs beside the earlier finding whenever it is used. [R05-S14, opening and “Wider adoption”]

These studies show why total work matters. Coding time, review, rework, operational deployment and foregone alternatives can move differently. A low-cost prototype is not automatically a low-cost maintained service. R-02 owns the detailed lifecycle comparison; this review uses it only to reject an automatic production-to-capture bridge.

### Open-ended entrepreneurial decisions can produce losses

Otis and colleagues' 2026 manuscript reports a five-month 2023 randomized trial with 640 Kenyan entrepreneurs recruited online. Access to a GPT-4 WhatsApp adviser produced no statistically significant average revenue/profit-index gain; the treatment effect was worse for initially lower performers than higher performers. The proposed explanation concerns which advice participants implemented. That mechanism is abductive, not randomized, and outcomes are self-reported. The largely young, educated sample, brief training and specific tool limit extrapolation. The current version describes high-performer benefit as suggestive, so the older draft's confident “over 20%” should not be carried forward. [R05-S15, §§3–6, Table 1]

The implication is not that judgment is an indestructible human asset. Rather, access to advice is insufficient to determine its realized value; selection and implementation can matter. The same finding warrants comparing better training, domain adaptation, constrained recommendations and delegation designs, not merely celebrating the people who already perform well.

### Competitive performance is observable without establishing economic rents

Krakowski, Luger and Raisch compare conventional, centaur and engine chess among 112 identified players across tournaments observed through 2017. Prior ordinary chess strength does not explain hybrid performance in the same way; engine selection, tuning and human–machine experience offer a different source of heterogeneity. The setting has explicit rules and observable results, but participation is selected, capability is partly inferred from prior performance, and business profit or compensation is not measured. The article itself calls for field experiments. Its evidence supports a possible change in the source of advantage, not permanent human superiority or a labor-income guarantee. [R05-S16, §§3–5]

R-10's final-version [BCG task-boundary capsule](../R-10/README.md#bcg-task-boundary-capsule) and [P&G cross-functional capsule](../R-10/README.md#pg-cross-functional-innovation-capsule) should be read alongside these cases rather than repeating their estimates here. Their relevance is the contingent task and organizational boundary of assistance. None of these designs randomizes a sustained institutional capacity for repeated redesign across multiple model generations while following rivals and financial returns.

The apparent disagreement between equalization in a structured support workflow, heterogeneous entrepreneurial losses and coding slowdown is informative. The treatments, populations, objectives and outcome measures differ. Within a known task, AI may disseminate a useful answer; choosing the task or commercial action is an additional problem. The evidence does not justify either “expertise always becomes more valuable” or “expertise never matters after AI.” It requires identifying which expertise, relative to which system, and at what stage.

## Education as production and as a market

Teaching tool use is analytically distinct from designing the tool, operating it and developing broad professional judgment. The reviewed evidence does not identify an invariant market for AI educators or a guaranteed return to a training credential. It does establish why education should not disappear from the account simply because it is labor rather than ownership.

McKenzie's reassessment of developing-country business training combines experiments whose individually imprecise estimates had often been read as failure. The inspected 2020 version's random-effects synthesis estimates approximately 10.1% higher profits and 4.7% higher sales. Measurement noise, survival treatment, outliers and intervention heterogeneity matter. These are business-owner outcomes after training, not educators' earnings or a causal estimate of AI-tool instruction. The review also treats costs and delivery at scale as substantive questions. [R05-S10, §§2–5, Figures 1–2]

A training service can create value by reducing search and implementation mistakes even when the information taught is not exclusive. But its provider's income depends on the service buyers will fund, the effectiveness of substitutes and the cost of delivery. Making a lesson easily reusable may increase its reach while reducing the price of each copy. Conversely, local integration, feedback and credible assessment could remain costly. These are competing mechanisms requiring evidence, not reasons to declare either educator extinction or educator scarcity.

Evidence that a learner produces a better artifact is not enough to establish retained skill; evidence of retained skill is not enough to establish pay. R-08 and R-10 must separate these outcomes. For R-05, the principal unresolved question is compensation incidence: whether training benefits learners, their current employer, a later employer, customers, the provider, or several together. Subsidized research interventions cannot automatically identify a self-financing commercial market.

## From productive contribution to compensation

### Rent sharing and employer wage setting

Card, Cardoso, Heining and Kline synthesize matched worker–firm evidence and develop a model in which differentiated workplaces have wage-setting power. Estimates controlling sorting and productivity shocks commonly imply wages respond only partially to firm productivity. Their reported range is roughly 0.5–1.5% higher wages for 10% higher value added per worker. Firm effects, amenities, mobility selection and productivity measurement complicate interpretation; fixed effects do not themselves prove a causal employer premium. The work rejects a simple one-for-one mapping without implying that wages are wholly disconnected from productivity. [R05-S17, §§1–4]

Kline, Petkova, Williams and Zidar link US first-time patent applicants to business and worker tax records. Initial valuable-patent allowance versus rejection provides firm-specific variation; earnings gains are concentrated among incumbent, higher-paid workers, with unequal gains across groups. Their approximately 29-cent wage pass-through per dollar of patent-induced surplus is a sample-specific estimate, not a general labor share. Identification depends on conditional comparability and predicted patent value; the sample is newly innovative firms, not all employees. The retention/replacement-cost interpretation competes with simpler wage-posting accounts and directly shows some labor capturing innovation rents without owning the patent. [R05-S18, §§2–6]

Employer wage-setting power is often described as monopsony power; it need not require a literal single employer. Differentiated workplaces, limited information and costly mobility can weaken the competitive alternatives available to workers. These findings constrain two possible overreadings of the predecessor discussion. It is too strong to say the company necessarily captures 100% of an employee's improvement, and too strong to say demonstrable value forces a commensurate raise. Wage setting can involve outside opportunities, switching costs, internal comparisons, bargaining, retention, contract commitments and employer-specific surplus. “Replacement cost” itself is not merely the salary of the next applicant: losing a relationship can disrupt production. Yet costly replacement need not give every incumbent equally effective leverage.

An intrapreneur's situation therefore depends on timing. Before investing, that person might negotiate terms or choose among employers. After creating an asset inseparable from an employer's data and infrastructure, the credible alternatives can be different. A promise of future recognition, an enforceable bonus rule and an equity grant are different arrangements. Documented productivity can help make a contribution legible; it cannot alone supply missing rights or competition among employers.

### The Danish test: work changes before pay

Humlum and Vestergaard's March 2026 revision links surveys in 11 exposed occupations to Danish registers. The late-2024 survey covers about 25,000 workers and 7,000 workplaces. Difference-in-differences estimates rule out average earnings/recorded-hours effects larger than roughly 2% through December 2024, despite reported benefits and new AI-related work. Raw adopter wage premia predate ChatGPT. Occupational transitions suggest another adjustment margin but are not randomized. Adoption and employer initiatives remain selected; Danish institutions, early tools and a two-year window bound inference. This is precise early evidence of separation between work reorganization and average pay, not evidence of zero lifetime effects or zero individual gains. [R05-S19, §§1–3, Figure 8; appendices B,C,F]

### Skills, titles and managerial demand are different objects

Pouliakas and Santangelo use the 2021 European Skills and Jobs Survey across 29 countries. AI programming is associated with higher wages even against other programmers, but their task-based definition concerns developing AI, not generic chatbot use. The analysis is observational, uses self-reports and decomposition, and shows sensitivity to skill controls and survey mode. It cannot identify the effect of acquiring a new title or learning a particular AI workflow. [R05-S20, §§3–5]

Alekseeva and colleagues' 2026 study follows 823 Compustat firms using 2011–2022 US vacancies. A fixed-effects and shift-share design associates AI-related hiring with more managerial demand and changed skill requirements. Yet the managerial-wage IV estimate is near zero, with no robust evidence of a relative managerial wage increase. Vacancy proxies are not realized hiring; the authors acknowledge possible correlated occupational shocks and sparse salary coverage. This mainly pre-generative-AI evidence cannot certify emerging titles, but it directly cautions against inferring higher pay from a more important coordination role. [R05-S21, §§3–6, note 24]

The 2021 Alekseeva et al. vacancy paper is frequently cited for AI-skill wage premia. Only publisher/author metadata and abstract were recovered here, so it is not a load-bearing full-text source. In any case, an advertised skill differential within a title cannot establish that changing the title creates the differential. [R05-S22]

No inspected study identifies a causal compensation premium for the exact titles “AI Work Architect” or “Systems Product Manager,” or tests S-07's proposed reclassification strategy. A defensible test would compare actual compensation and duties before and after a title change against comparable workers without it, accounting for promotion, employer change, prior performance and labor demand. A salary website, selected job advertisement or successful negotiator's story cannot answer that question.

## Ownership is exposure to an economic arrangement

The claim that ownership “wins in both worlds” omits the price of acquiring the asset, financing, maintenance, failure and competing substitutes. A residual claim can pay nothing. Ownership of reproducible code is not equivalent to ownership of a scarce customer relationship, legal right, distribution channel or operating complement. The same activity can also yield consumer benefit while eroding the owner's margin.

Moskowitz and Vissing-Jørgensen find concentrated US entrepreneurial equity holdings and returns that do not exceed public equity in their principal 1989–1998 comparison, despite poor diversification. Their calculations require difficult valuation, survival and owner-labor adjustments. Kartashova's later extension through 2007 challenges the robustness of that relative-return result when the public-market comparison period changes. Neither study randomizes ownership or concerns AI-built businesses. Read together, they caution against selecting a return window or an accounting category as proof that entrepreneurship is uniformly superior or inferior. [R05-S23, §§I–IV; R05-S24, §§2–3]

For a conditional D-002, the important inference is not an investment recommendation. It is that a person's strategy must specify the asset, initial cost, exposure, diversification and alternative use of time. An owner-operator's receipts can compensate labor as well as capital. Buying an asset whose benefits are already fully reflected in its price does not promise excess returns. Even an owner who benefits from cognitive substitution need not be the original builder, the present employee or a small entrepreneur.

R-18 already rejects using Portugal, Ford or the capital–labor–consumer phase story as proof of a general capture law. Its printing and mobile-ecosystem capsules also show why widespread technical availability need not eliminate bottlenecks. Those reviewed histories are cross-references, not additional independent replications here: see [R-18 synthesis](../R-18/README.md), [Ford and surplus, §§2,6](../R-18/ford-surplus.md), and [Portugal](../R-18/portugal.md).

### No-code and raw code are not ownership categories

Bubble's official documentation says user data/design and the underlying platform code have different ownership treatment; runnable app code cannot be exported and migration requires rebuilding logic. This documents a concrete portability constraint. Its own page says governing legal terms prevail, so it should not be generalized into a legal opinion or a measured welfare loss. [R05-S25]

FlutterFlow's official documentation, by contrast, describes ownership of output, code export and mobile apps capable of operating independently of its service, while flagging third-party licenses. This is a counterexample to a universal claim that visual development necessarily prevents portable code. It does not prove costless migration, universal license clearance or superior business outcomes. [R05-S26]

The relevant comparison is contractual and architectural: exportable source and data; independent execution; dependencies; test and deployment capacity; ability to replace vendors; switching costs; licenses; and rights between builder, client and employer. Generating raw code changes some of these dimensions but does not determine all of them. A code repository can still depend on proprietary APIs, hosted state or an employer's confidential information. A managed platform can provide maintenance services that an independent builder would otherwise have to finance.

The stronger C-050 economic prescription remains untested. No controlled longitudinal comparison recovered here shows that noncoders using agentic raw-code development reliably earn more net income, retain more rights or face lower total lifecycle cost than otherwise comparable visual-platform users. R-02 must evaluate technical/lifecycle feasibility; R-15 must evaluate actual rights. The interface is an insufficient proxy for either.

## Integrated mechanism and boundary conditions

The most evidence-consistent mechanism has distinct stages:

1. A new capability changes feasible tasks or their costs.
2. Actors decide whether and how to combine it with other inputs.
3. Adoption, coordination and implementation determine realized quality-adjusted outcomes.
4. Demand, imitation and complementary-asset markets determine the surplus available.
5. Contracts, control, outside options, bargaining and institutions affect its distribution.
6. Learning, depreciation, turnover and technological change alter all five over time.

This is an analytical decomposition derived from the compared literatures, not an estimated law. It permits different outcomes without explaining them away. For example, high task productivity with little wage movement is coherent if gains are small at the job level, absorbed by other tasks, competed into prices, retained by firms, or offset by new costs. These explanations are observationally different and must not be asserted merely because one fits the preferred story.

Likewise, persistent profit could reflect superior adaptation, hard-to-copy complements, market power, risk, selection or accounting treatment. A study of routines cannot identify all of them. Persistent relative performance is stronger evidence than a before/after success, but still requires a specified market, horizon and comparison group. “Durable” should mean observed or forecast persistence over an explicit period, not immunity to change.

For architectural work, repeated changes are valuable when they improve the relevant outcome more than retaining or buying an alternative arrangement. For education, the test includes transfer, subsequent independent use and delivery cost. For consequential operation, it includes missed failures and false interventions as well as caught errors. For ownership, it includes invested capital and opportunity cost, not revenue alone. These criteria can reject bad redesign, ineffective instruction, ceremonial oversight and unprofitable assets without denying that each category can contribute.

There is also a tension inside the desired method. Codifying knowledge can make a system easier to copy and an employee easier to replace, while improving reliability and organizational reach. Keeping it obscure may preserve personal dependence but harm the institution and customers. Research can expose that trade-off; it cannot decide whose interests should govern. D-002's productive categories should not become advice to manufacture indispensable bottlenecks.

## Evidence gaps and downstream reconciliation

The missing evidence is not simply “more studies.” The load-bearing gaps concern particular transitions:

- **Method persistence:** prospective, repeated measures of a specified redesign practice across firms, model generations and competitor responses; include failed and discontinued projects and full implementation cost.
- **Contribution to pay:** link architectural, teaching and operational contributions to payroll, fees, bonuses, equity, job mobility and within-firm distribution, rather than infer pay from output or advertisements.
- **Training incidence:** distinguish short-run assisted performance, retained learning, third-party transfer, commercial demand for instruction and instructor compensation. R-08/R-10 own the developmental assessment.
- **Consequential operation:** compare genuine decision authority, supported intervention and ceremonial approval; value avoided losses without assuming every intervention helps. R-07/R-13 own the reliability and authority tests.
- **No-code alternatives:** matched or randomized comparisons of completed maintained systems with security, migration, licensing, integration and support costs. R-02 owns lifecycle evidence; R-15 owns the rights analysis.
- **Ownership and aggregation:** disaggregate owner labor from capital returns and economic rents; specify which asset is scarce. R-11 constrains accounting; R-12 handles price, entry, demand and general-equilibrium distribution.
- **Moving capability boundary:** neither current judgment advantages nor current software failures settle machine substitution of future diagnosis, education or redesign. Reconcile R-06/R-14/R-16/R-17 before making permanence claims.

R-18's historical findings are constraints on rhetoric, not an empirical substitute for these tests. D-002 remains current author intent throughout. Luke's remaining choices include how cautiously to use the moat metaphor, whether to retain a career prescription when exact compensation evidence is absent, and what normative distribution or worker rights to advocate. Research acceptance cannot make an unsupported guarantee true.

## Method and limitations

This is a targeted analytical review, not a registered systematic review or exhaustive search of every database. Searches combined exact paper recovery with opposing-evidence queries on management persistence, consulting failures, AI performance, wage premia, rent sharing, ownership returns and platform portability. Public publisher, author, university, working-paper and official documentation sources were prioritized. Search snippets served as leads; source claims rely on substantive text except the expressly limited S22.

The evidence mix is intentional: conceptual strategy and contract models identify mechanisms; interventions test changes in selected settings; quasi-experiments seek counterfactual comparisons; surveys and job postings describe broader patterns; official platform pages establish narrower product representations. None substitutes automatically for another. Publicly accessible prepublication versions are identified rather than silently treated as final articles. Where later versions materially differ, the review keeps denominators, dates and qualifications together.

No new experiment, private dataset analysis, employer-contract inspection, software export test, or independent financial audit was performed. Text extraction was checked against document headings, dates and locators; no claim of replicated table calculations is made. Positive research partnerships, subsidy, selected participation and proprietary data can limit external validity without nullifying internal comparisons. Sponsors and implementers are especially relevant where interventions were provided free: adoption under a research subsidy need not equal adoption at a market price. Official vendor documentation is evidence of stated features, not neutral evidence of comparative welfare.

Source summaries are deliberately bounded; repeated references denote the same work, not additional evidence. The R-18 historical capsules and R-10 task studies are cross-referenced to avoid duplicating their source summaries. Gaps in causal identification, exact-title compensation, durable AI-method rents and no-code lifecycle comparisons remain visible rather than being closed by theory, confidence or author preference.
