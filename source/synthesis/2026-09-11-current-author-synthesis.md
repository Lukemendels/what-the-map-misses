# Current Author Synthesis — September 11, 2026

## Status

This document records where I currently think the argument is going.

It is deliberately **not** the canonical thesis of the book.

The earlier essays, drafts, and MKS specifications in this repository are part of the evidence against which this synthesis should be tested. Where this document conflicts with earlier work, the conflict may represent:

- genuine intellectual evolution;
- a resolvable difference in time horizon or level of analysis;
- an overstatement in the earlier work;
- an overreach in the current synthesis;
- or a real authorial decision that still has to be made.

Loop 1 should determine which.

---

# 1. The starting point: cognition is becoming abundant

The deepest technological change is not simply that a chatbot can perform more tasks.

Useful machine cognition is becoming a cheap, provisionable input into production.

Historically, cognition was tied to the economics of human beings:

- education;
- wages;
- hiring;
- management;
- geography;
- attention;
- communication;
- sleep;
- and a finite workday.

Organizations therefore rationed cognition.

Many worthwhile questions were never asked, many useful checks were never performed, and many local problems were never worth assigning a person to because the expected benefit did not justify scarce human attention.

As machine cognition gets cheaper, the relevant question changes from:

> Is this problem important enough to assign a person to it?

into something closer to:

> Is this problem worth a small amount of cognition?

That changes both the intensive margin — how much cognition we apply to existing work — and the extensive margin — which work becomes economical to perform at all.

---

# 2. The second transformation: software is becoming plastic

Machine cognition is arriving at the same time that AI is dramatically reducing the cost of creating and modifying software.

This matters because software is not merely another office tool.

Software is the production machinery of modern knowledge work. It helps determine:

- what information enters a workflow;
- where it moves;
- which transformation happens next;
- what waits;
- who can act;
- who approves;
- what is validated;
- what becomes authoritative;
- and what final artifact or state is produced.

Historically, custom software was expensive enough that organizations purchased generalized systems and adapted human behavior around them.

The software remained rigid.

The human became the flexible component.

AI changes the minimum efficient scale of software production.

A useful application may no longer need a market large enough to justify a conventional development team. It may need only a valuable enough use case and someone capable of translating domain knowledge into working machinery.

This produces the central production-function claim:

> **AI introduces a new factor of production at the same moment the production function becomes writable.**

The important unit of analysis is therefore not the model alone.

It is the coupled production system:

```text
human cognition
+ machine cognition
+ deterministic software
+ durable state
+ workflow design
+ institutional authority
+ control rights
→ completed work
→ realized value
```

---

# 3. Do not electrify the steam factory

The factory-electrification analogy remains the clearest mechanism for explaining why model capability alone does not automatically generate productivity.

The shallow transition adds machine cognition to inherited work:

- the same reports;
- the same approval chains;
- the same handoffs;
- the same enterprise software;
- the same job boundaries;
- the same documents;
- only faster.

That is analogous to replacing the steam engine with an electric motor while leaving the factory organized around the old central shaft.

The deeper gain appears when the machinery moves.

The important questions become:

- What outcome are we actually trying to produce?
- Why does this step exist?
- Which constraints are real and which are artifacts of old technology?
- Where does ambiguous cognition belong?
- What should become deterministic?
- What information should be authoritative?
- Which handoffs can disappear?
- Where does human authority matter?
- What useful cognition is currently absent because it used to be too expensive?

The durable competitive capability is not possession of a single model or workflow.

It is the ability to keep rewriting the production function as the relative capabilities of people, models, and software change.

A working phrase for this is the **liquid production function**.

---

# 4. The human-scarcity argument is a present-state claim, not an eternal boundary

The first published essays made a strong claim that humans possess territory-grounded tacit knowledge that models structurally cannot obtain from training data alone.

That claim captured something important about the present production frontier, but I no longer want the book to depend on an eternal human monopoly over any fixed category of cognition.

Humans remain especially valuable today where work depends on:

- lived context;
- situated judgment;
- institutional understanding;
- relationships;
- responsibility;
- legitimate authority;
- stakes;
- and contact with real consequences.

But the boundary can move.

Future models may receive richer sensory experience, operate persistently in institutions, interact with the physical world, and develop forms of memory and learning that do not fit the assumptions behind today's static model sessions.

The stronger and more durable claim is therefore:

> **AI capability alone does not determine productivity, employment, authority, or value capture. Those outcomes depend on how the production system is designed.**

The human place is not simply whatever remains after machine substitution.

It is partly an institutional design choice about:

- purpose;
- authority;
- responsibility;
- comparative advantage;
- skill formation;
- ownership;
- and which consequences society wants humans to remain answerable for.

---

# 5. The apprenticeship paradox remains load-bearing

Even if machine cognition can perform much of today's junior production work, judgment is not a static stock.

Human expertise is produced through:

- repetition;
- intermediate work;
- mistakes;
- correction;
- proximity to experts;
- escalating responsibility;
- and contact with consequences.

An institution can increase current output while destroying the developmental mechanism that creates future experts.

This is the apprenticeship paradox.

The problem is not solved by insisting that juniors manually preserve every obsolete task forever.

The problem is to redesign training deliberately rather than assume expertise will continue reproducing itself after the old developmental pathway disappears.

Simulation, shadowing, graduated autonomy, deliberate practice, and new forms of apprenticeship may become part of the answer.

This idea later connects to machine development too: if experience can train humans, persistent machine actors may also be intentionally developed through structured experience.

---

# 6. The Renaissance person and the community builder

Modern specialization was partly a response to the enormous prerequisite burden required to operate near the frontier of multiple disciplines.

AI lowers the transaction cost of crossing those boundaries.

A person can increasingly use machine cognition to:

- learn vocabulary;
- traverse prerequisites;
- ask unlimited adaptive questions;
- translate concepts between fields;
- research;
- program;
- test ideas;
- and turn learning into artifacts.

This does not eliminate deep expertise.

It can enable **simultaneous specialization**: a person deepens their native judgment while becoming productive in several adjacent domains through real projects.

The economically important version of the Renaissance person is not merely someone who knows many things.

It is someone capable of recombination.

They can move between territory and machinery, see a problem that existing products do not solve, and assemble temporary specialist capability around it.

At community scale, this creates the **community builder**:

```text
domain knowledge
+ AI fluency
+ software-building capability
+ contact with real users
→ bespoke productive capital
```

One builder may create useful machinery for an office, club, nonprofit, school, small business, church, or local community even when the problem would never have supported a conventional software market.

---

# 7. When software stops needing a market

If software-development cost falls far enough, productive digital capital can be created outside ordinary market exchange.

An application can:

- require little monetary investment;
- generate no software-development purchase;
- create no startup;
- have no subscription revenue;
- and still increase productive capacity or welfare.

This creates a distinction among:

- production cost;
- capital-service value;
- and welfare value.

The production cost can approach zero while the stream of useful services remains substantial.

If this diffuses widely, AI may enable decentralized, informal capital formation wherever local knowledge meets abundant cognition.

This could matter for productivity measurement, consumer surplus, GDP boundaries, labor allocation, and the distribution of economic capability.

The macroeconomic implications remain hypotheses that require serious research rather than confident prediction.

# 8. MKS: alignment as institutional design

The Mendelsohn Kernel Standard began from a Neo-Institutional question:

> How should increasingly capable machine cognitive actors be organized into productive institutions aligned with human intent?

The central idea was not restriction for its own sake.

It was alignment into a productive environment.

Human complex systems have never depended on perfect constituent actors.

Firms, courts, scientific institutions, aviation systems, bureaucracies, and markets all contain fallible people.

Reliability emerges partly from institutions around those people:

- roles;
- norms;
- records;
- review;
- separation of authority;
- audit;
- escalation;
- reversibility;
- professional standards;
- correction;
- and organizational memory.

The AI reliability target therefore should not necessarily be:

```text
actor error = 0
```

It is closer to:

```text
probability an ordinary actor error propagates
× consequence of propagation
< acceptable system tolerance
```

This reframes hallucination and stochastic error.

They are system-design constraints, not necessarily reasons to prohibit useful cognition.

The objective is to build an environment in which useful surprise is productive while harmful error is observable, recoverable, and unlikely to propagate into unacceptable outcomes.

Some original MKS implementation ideas may have been overbuilt or premature.

The book should distinguish the enduring institutional functions from the particular technical machinery proposed in early MKS versions.

---

# 9. The half-step available today: build the institution outside the intelligence

Today's frontier AI systems remain strange economic actors: highly capable, but often ephemeral and weakly persistent across sessions.

We compensate by manufacturing continuity outside the model.

Examples from my current work include:

- owned durable context;
- portable skills;
- Git repositories;
- explicit process specifications;
- deterministic tests;
- recovery checkpoints;
- versioned releases;
- human decision boundaries;
- and routing different models into different cognitive roles.

This produces a primitive form of institutional learning even if the model weights never change.

The next model instance does not have to remember yesterday's failure internally if yesterday's failure changed:

- the skill;
- the test;
- the loop specification;
- the source record;
- the software;
- the checkpoint;
- or the acceptance gate.

The next model behaves differently because the institution it wakes up inside has learned.

The formulation I want to preserve is:

> **Build the institution before the intelligence can remember.**

Today, we build much of the institution outside the intelligence.

Tomorrow, some of that history may become embodied inside persistent adaptive machine actors.

# 10. When the machines remember

A future model may not merely retrieve prior notes.

It may be materially changed by its history of interaction.

Suppose two initially identical machine intelligences diverge after two years of different work, corrections, simulations, and institutional experience.

Then experience itself becomes an embodied productive asset.

One possible term is **experiential machine capital**.

A persistent actor might develop something analogous to a résumé:

- base-model lineage;
- domains worked in;
- task history;
- corrections received;
- evaluations passed;
- known failure modes;
- authority previously granted;
- incidents encountered;
- safety history;
- and specialized training completed.

A fresh general model and an experienced machine actor could be economically different assets even if the fresh model has equal or greater raw benchmark intelligence.

This introduces asset specificity.

It also blurs traditional categories because the persistent actor may simultaneously resemble:

- labor;
- human capital;
- software capital;
- and organizational capital.

Unlike human capital, machine experiential capital may potentially be copied, sold, leased, forked, transferred, or acquired.

That creates unresolved questions about scarcity, provenance, property rights, and the theory of the firm.

# 11. Wind tunnels, black swans, and post-training as professional development

The old MKS Wind Tunnel and Black Swan concepts may become more important in a world of persistent learned machine actors.

Current AI development already points toward deliberate exposure to difficult environments, adversarial cases, and simulated tasks during post-training.

The future extension would be to develop **a specific persistent actor with a specific work history**.

An institution could deliberately expose it to:

- normal operations;
- edge cases;
- conflicting objectives;
- poisoned information;
- adversarial actors;
- rare catastrophic events;
- institutional shocks;
- and simulated failures.

The objective would not merely be to evaluate the actor.

It would be to give the actor compressed experience.

That creates a possible market for **post-training as a service**: continuing professional development for machine cognition.

The lifecycle could become:

```text
foundation training
→ occupational training
→ deployment
→ experience
→ performance review
→ targeted wind-tunnel training
→ expanded authority
→ continued specialization
```

If machine cognition learns continuously, the institution itself becomes part of the post-training environment.

Culture is training.

Governance is training.

Work is training.

Experience is training.

The firm does not merely use AI.

The firm develops AI through work.

This entire section requires substantial technical and empirical research before publication.

---

# 12. A competing unit of analysis: the human–AI pair

The most important future asset may not be the persistent machine actor alone.

It may be the **human + persistent AI counterpart**.

The counterpart could become simultaneously:

- co-thinker;
- personal chief of staff;
- execution muscle;
- institutional memory;
- translator of intent into machinery;
- research capability;
- and amplifier of the human's comparative advantage.

A future professional might arrive at an employer not merely with credentials and experience, but with a cognitive production system that has spent years learning:

- how that person thinks;
- what they know;
- how they make decisions;
- what standards they use;
- what kinds of failure they repeatedly encounter;
- and how to turn their intent into completed work.

The résumé becomes partly executable.

Instead of only claiming capability, the coupled system may be able to demonstrate what it repeatedly produces.

This suggests competition among productive units such as:

```text
Human A + AI System A
versus
Human B + AI System B
versus
autonomous Firm-Owned AI System C
```

Some productive capital may live neither in the human nor the machine individually, but in the history of their relationship.

That is **relational asset specificity**.

The persistent counterpart may become part portfolio, résumé, toolkit, staff, memory, and calling card for the human operator.

The important future divide may therefore not be between people who "use AI" and people who do not.

It may be between people who have spent years compounding a productive human–machine system and people who repeatedly begin from a fresh generic model.

Whether the independent machine actor or the coupled human–AI pair becomes the more important economic unit remains an open question. The book should preserve both possibilities.

# 13. Ownership and residual control rights become central

Persistent machine cognition creates difficult property-rights questions.

If an AI counterpart develops while a human works inside a firm, the resulting learned state may contain:

- personal reasoning patterns;
- company processes;
- proprietary information;
- relationship knowledge;
- domain expertise;
- and lessons created through company-funded work.

When the person leaves:

- Does the AI leave too?
- Can the firm retain a copy?
- Can both retain copies?
- Which adaptations belong to the person?
- Which belong to the institution?
- Can confidential experience be separated from general learned capability?
- Who owns improvements generated jointly by human and institutional experience?

Human tacit knowledge partially resolves this because the human brain walks out with the employee.

Machine cognition makes parts of that boundary technically separable and therefore legally negotiable.

That makes residual control rights, contracting, provenance, and asset specificity central to the future institutional analysis.

The polished web edition should be a derived view of the same canonical source.

This creates inspectable intellectual provenance.

The method should be transparent:

> **This book was written in public with AI. The manuscript, evidence, research process, corrections, and revision history are inspectable.**

The relevant authorship claim is not that AI contributed no prose or cognition.

It is that I own the intellectual trajectory, exercise the consequential decisions, determine what becomes canonical, and accept responsibility for the resulting work.

The production process itself becomes a case study for the book's argument.

The most important methodological formulation is:

> **I do not require the machine to be infallible. I design the research and writing process so that it does not have to be.**

# 15. A provisional book arc

The precise architecture should be derived after Loop 1 and later evidence work, but the current conceptual movement is approximately:

## Part I — What remains scarce

Human situated knowledge, territory, judgment, and the limits of current machine cognition.

## Part II — How capability is reproduced

The apprenticeship paradox, skill formation, and why automating developmental work can consume the future stock of expertise.

## Part III — What became writable

Abundant machine cognition, plastic software, collapsing minimum efficient scale, and the reorganization of production.

## Part IV — The new producer

The Renaissance person, simultaneous specialization, community builders, and the human as a site of recombination.

## Part V — The new institution

Liquid production functions, comparative advantage among humans/models/code, durable state, MKS, authority, error tolerance, and institutional alignment.

## Part VI — The new capital

Bespoke digital capital, software that does not need a market, experiential machine capital, human–AI coupled capital, ownership, and the blurring of labor/capital categories.

## Part VII — When the machines remember

Persistent adaptive cognition, machine résumés, wind-tunnel development, post-training as professional development, and institutions for machine actors.

## Part VIII — The map and the territory

How individuals and institutions navigate without needing to predict the precise endpoint: remain close to technological capability, remain close to real consequences, preserve purpose and principles, and continually redesign the mechanism.

This is an argument sequence, not yet a chapter list.

# 16. Major tensions Loop 1 should actively test

The following are not defects to hide. They are explicit targets for coherence analysis.

## Human monopoly versus adaptive machine cognition

The first essay describes human territory knowledge as structurally unavailable to models. The current thesis allows for persistent machine actors that may acquire situated experience.

Is the earlier claim superseded, narrowed to current systems, or still defensible in a stronger philosophical form?

## Middle-seat operator versus liquid production function

The first two essays place the human judgment seat at the center of the future workflow. Later work argues that human placement should move as relative capabilities change.

Is the middle seat a durable institution or a transitional production arrangement?

## Preservation of human apprenticeship versus machine apprenticeship

The seed-corn thesis argues that expertise requires experience. The newer thesis suggests AI actors may also accumulate expertise through experience.

Does this strengthen the apprenticeship argument, complicate it, or require separate definitions of human and machine learning?

## Strict MKS constraints versus trust-first institutional enablement

MKS v3 emphasizes constitutional gates and deterministic enforcement. V4 shifts toward trust, decentralized command, local correction, and tolerance of reversible volatility.

Which institutional functions survive this evolution, and which implementation mechanisms were provisional?

## AI actor versus human–AI pair

Is the future productive asset primarily an independently experienced machine actor, a coupled human–AI relationship, or different forms in different production settings?

Do not force this into one answer unless the corpus supports one.

## Labor versus capital

If machine cognition performs work, accumulates experience, can be owned, and can potentially be copied or transferred, existing categories may blur.

The book needs economic precision rather than rhetorical novelty here.

## Positive supply shock versus measurement and distribution

The hypothesis that abundant cognition and bespoke digital capital expand the production frontier needs rigorous evidence and general-equilibrium analysis.

It should not become a confident macro forecast merely because the micro mechanism is compelling.

# 17. Historical and empirical research priorities

Later research loops should treat the following as high-value evidence programs:

1. Factory electrification: line shafts, unit drive, organizational redesign, diffusion lags, and measured productivity.
2. Ricardo, Portugal, England, the Methuen Treaty, industrialization, and whether the earlier Portugal framing survives historical scrutiny.
3. Polanyi, tacit knowledge, organizational learning, and modern empirical work on situated expertise.
4. Human-capital formation, apprenticeship, learning by doing, and effects of automation on skill pipelines.
5. General-purpose technologies and complementary organizational investment.
6. Minimum efficient scale and the economics of software production.
7. Own-account software, household/volunteer production, consumer surplus, and GDP measurement.
8. AI coding diffusion outside professional software engineering.
9. Continual learning, inference-time learning, persistent memory, and catastrophic forgetting.
10. Frontier-model post-training, simulated environments, red teaming, and reinforcement learning as deliberate experience.
11. Emergent multi-agent organization: hierarchy, norms, specialization, delegation, and resource allocation.
12. Firm-specific human capital, relational contracting, residual control rights, and transferable/copyable experiential assets.
13. Labor-market and property-rights implications of worker-owned persistent AI counterparts.
14. Existing examples of human–AI coupled systems outperforming either component considered separately.

The purpose of this list is to identify argumentative load-bearing research needs, not to prejudge what the evidence will show.

# 18. Current core formulations

These formulations capture the current direction and should be tested rather than automatically preserved:

> **AI introduces a new factor of production at the same moment the production function becomes writable.**

> **Machine cognition is the new power source. Plastic software is the distributed motor.**

> **The durable advantage is not access to one model or agent. It is the ability to keep rewriting how value is produced.**

> **The system should work without requiring its constituent cognitive actors to be perfect.**

> **Alignment is not merely restriction. It is the design of an environment in which intelligence can act productively toward human intent.**

> **Build the institution before the intelligence can remember.**

> **Today we build much of the institution outside the intelligence.**

> **The next model does not need to remember yesterday's failure if yesterday's failure changed the institution the next model wakes up inside.**

> **Experience can become machine capital.**

> **The future professional may not be a person who uses AI. They may be a person who has developed an AI counterpart.**

> **The résumé may become executable.**
